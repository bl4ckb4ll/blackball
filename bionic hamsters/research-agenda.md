# Bionic hamsters — research agenda

The point of this file is to turn a loose but important intuition into separable questions that can be measured, contradicted, or refined.

## 1. Build an advantage ledger

For a person, cohort, school, occupation, or institution, separate these inputs rather than collapsing them into “merit”:

| Dimension | Examples | Measurement ideas |
|---|---|---|
| Family economic capital | parental income, wealth, housing, emergency support | percentile ranks, wealth survey, home value, debt, transfers |
| Family institutional capital | parents' degrees, professions, admissions knowledge | parent education/occupation, alumni ties, professional networks |
| School opportunity | AP/IB courses, counselors, sports, arts, clubs | course catalogue, counselor ratios, activity availability |
| Purchased enrichment | tutors, test prep, music, travel teams, camps | dollars, hours, years of participation |
| Time freedom | no paid work, no sibling care, transportation | weekly discretionary hours |
| Appearance | rated attractiveness, height, grooming, teeth, hair, dress | blinded ratings + objective components where defensible |
| Social presentation | speech, confidence, eye contact, ease, accent | structured ratings / experiments, not vague personality labels |
| Achievement | grades, scores, competitions, publications, athletic results | direct records |
| Résumé optimization | number and strategic fit of activities/titles | application data, activity counts, leadership counts |
| Institutional selection | selective-school admission, honors, internships | admissions / placement records |
| Network access | high-status peers, mentors, referrals | network data, referral source, connectedness measures |
| Adult bargaining position | outside options, savings, family backstop | job offers, liquid assets, unemployment risk |

The analytical task is to estimate which columns are causes, signals, correlates, or consequences of other columns.

## 2. Distinguish ability, signal, and amplification

For every elite marker, ask three different questions.

### Ability

Does the marker predict actual performance on the task that later matters?

Examples:

- Does chess-club presidency predict management quality?
- Does an Ivy degree predict better legal judgment after controlling for prior preparation?
- Does attractiveness predict the productivity being paid for?
- Does varsity captaincy predict team coordination in adult work?

### Signal

Does the marker help an employer identify something real that is difficult to observe directly?

A signal can be useful even if the credential/activity does not cause the underlying trait.

### Amplifier

Does the marker create later opportunities that make the original difference larger?

Candidate examples to test:

- elite internship may increase access to a first elite job;
- a first elite job may create references and network ties;
- network ties may increase investor or executive access;
- high income may change partner-meeting opportunities, neighborhood choice, and children's schools.

A trait can be weak as an ability measure and still be a powerful amplifier.

## 3. The adolescent-to-adult relevance test

For every adolescent achievement used in selection, record:

1. **What exactly did it demonstrate at the time?**
2. **How long should that evidence remain probative?**
3. **Does later adult performance replace it?**
4. **Does someone without the marker get a later chance to demonstrate the same trait?**
5. **How much income/status difference can plausibly be attributed to it?**

This is the core logical test behind the user's claim that high school is something that happens while people are young and adult life should not remain permanently ranked by it.

## 4. The résumé-arms-race index

Construct a historical measure by graduating cohort and school type.

Possible components:

- number of advanced courses;
- number of extracurriculars;
- number of leadership titles;
- number of formal awards;
- number of national-level competitions;
- hours per week in organized activities;
- paid admissions/test-prep spending;
- summer programs;
- research/internship participation;
- application count;
- volunteer hours;
- essay coaching.

Questions:

- Has the index risen over time?
- Does the rise occur mostly among affluent applicants?
- Is there a positional arms race in which everyone's effort increases without materially changing anyone's relative rank?
- What activities disappear because students lack time?
- Are highly optimized applicants more anxious, risk-averse, externally motivated, or status-oriented later?

Deresiewicz supplies a historical and interpretive hypothesis; the empirical project should seek independent data.

## 5. Depth-versus-breadth test

Construct a spectrum from **single-minded depth** to **optimized breadth**.

Possible cases:

- mathematics student who spends years independently working through books;
- musician with thousands of hours in one instrument;
- competitive athlete with one consuming sport;
- generalist with many leadership and service roles;
- applicant whose activities are numerous but shallow.

Outcomes:

- admission probability;
- later domain achievement;
- persistence in the domain;
- original work;
- career satisfaction;
- graduate-school entry;
- research output where relevant.

The SL2/Colin anecdote is a candidate qualitative case after identity verification.

## 6. Beauty and appearance module

Do not use one vague variable called “looks.” Split it.

Possible components:

- facial attractiveness;
- height;
- body size;
- hair;
- teeth;
- skin;
- clothing;
- makeup / grooming;
- voice;
- age appearance;
- posture;
- photo quality.

Outcomes:

- interview callback;
- starting wage;
- performance rating;
- promotion;
- tips;
- sales;
- elected office;
- media exposure;
- partner income / education;
- customer ratings;
- disciplinary treatment.

Mechanisms:

- employer/customer taste;
- assumed competence;
- confidence;
- possible social-skill development following repeated warmer treatment (currently an uncited life-course hypothesis);
- occupation sorting;
- class investment in presentation.

Important design: compare situations where true task performance is independently observable with situations where it is not.

## 7. The charisma decomposition

Instead of treating “charisma score 18” as one thing, decompose it:

- actual ability to coordinate people;
- ability to explain ideas clearly;
- ability to read social cues;
- warmth;
- confidence;
- physical attractiveness;
- class-coded accent / vocabulary;
- institutional familiarity;
- dominance;
- willingness to self-promote;
- willingness to ask for favors;
- network centrality;
- status already possessed.

Then ask which components produce socially useful output and which mainly redistribute opportunity toward the person who has them.

## 8. The low-charisma floor test

Even if high charisma is productive, ask what follows for people low on the trait.

Measure whether low social smoothness predicts:

- wages below basic living costs;
- unstable schedules;
- employer abuse;
- lack of promotion despite strong task performance;
- exclusion from customer-facing work;
- exclusion from management;
- weaker access to credit/capital;
- smaller professional networks.

The normative question is separate from the predictive one:

> **What minimum material and social floor should remain independent of charisma?**

This is where the bionic-hamster framework connects directly to bargaining power and living-wage questions.

## 9. Assortative-mating matrix

Build a matrix rather than a slogan.

Cross-match spouses/partners on:

- income;
- wealth;
- education;
- occupation;
- school prestige;
- parental class;
- attractiveness;
- height;
- race/ethnicity where analytically appropriate;
- religion;
- geography;
- network overlap.

Then estimate associations and, where a design permits, causal downstream effects:

- household income concentration;
- household wealth concentration;
- neighborhood choice;
- school quality;
- fertility timing;
- childcare spending;
- extracurricular spending;
- college attendance and selectivity of children.

Keep **homogamy** (like with like) distinct from **status exchange** (one trait traded against another).

## 10. College as spouse/network market

The 2026 Abramitzky–Pérez–Price working paper gives a useful but bounded design lead: 60 coeducational U.S. colleges, students enrolled in 1915–1943, and child enrollment records through 1963. Its exposure and campus-overlap designs address same-college marriage. Do not generalize them without further evidence to current elite colleges, professional networks, spouse income, beauty/status exchange, or the causal effect on children's selective-college attendance.

Research questions:

- How much does college change the set of people one can realistically meet and marry?
- Does selectivity matter beyond education level?
- How much of the return to elite college is mediated by spouse income/education?
- How much is mediated by friendships, referrals, and professional networks?
- Does an elite college compound family advantage even when classroom instruction itself is not exceptional?

This can be connected to Blackball's **proximity to future authority figures** work.

## 11. Care-work mirror test

Take the traits elite institutions say they want:

- leadership;
- service;
- resilience;
- responsibility;
- empathy;
- teamwork;
- communication;
- grit;
- initiative.

Then identify low-paid jobs where workers must actually display them daily.

Examples:

- home-health aides;
- certified nursing assistants;
- direct-support professionals;
- childcare workers;
- special-education aides;
- elder-care workers;
- emergency shelter workers.

For each job collect:

- wage;
- benefits;
- turnover;
- injury rates;
- emotional demands;
- staffing ratios;
- schedule control;
- training;
- responsibility for vulnerable people;
- consequences of error;
- social status.

Then compare **rhetorical valuation** with **economic valuation**.

## 12. Appearance versus care contribution experiment

A conceptual experimental design:

Give evaluators standardized information about two workers whose observable contribution is identical but vary:

- attractiveness;
- elite-school pedigree;
- speech polish;
- occupation prestige;
- care-work versus corporate-work framing.

Ask for:

- deserved pay;
- promotion suitability;
- leadership potential;
- competence;
- moral admiration;
- willingness to trust with responsibility.

This could expose whether people verbally admire care while assigning economic desert to status cues.

## 13. Cumulative-treatment / interiority archive

Collect qualitative material under explicit rules.

Possible themes:

- “pretty sibling / ugly sibling” comparisons;
- school popularity and teacher attention;
- treatment changes after weight loss, dental work, grooming, or increased income;
- customer treatment;
- workplace meetings;
- romantic attention;
- aging and loss of beauty privilege;
- people who were high-status adolescents and later lost status;
- people whose adult competence emerged after being ignored in youth.

Do not infer prevalence from the archive. Code it for mechanisms, language, and lifetime effects.

## 14. Geographic / cultural style hypothesis

The user's “East Coast type” should be translated into variables rather than ethnic/regional caricature.

Possible dimensions:

- rule maximization;
- comfort with self-promotion;
- willingness to exploit technical advantages;
- status competition;
- admissions/career strategizing;
- attitude toward “unsporting” but legal behavior;
- fair-play norms;
- willingness to leave money/status on the table for principle;
- attitudes toward people who do not optimize.

Then compare regions only if adequate survey/behavioral data exist.

The key hypothesis is institutional:

> Systems that reward aggressive optimization may gradually select leaders who believe aggressive optimization is normal, rational, and morally self-justifying.

## 15. The “same person, different wrapper” test

Where possible, exploit natural or experimental variation in presentation while holding ability constant.

Examples:

- identical résumé with elite versus ordinary school name;
- identical work sample with attractive versus ordinary photo;
- identical idea attributed to high-status versus low-status speaker;
- voice/accent manipulation;
- blind audition or anonymized evaluation;
- performance observed before versus after pedigree information.

Within the population, outcome, and time horizon studied, these designs can identify an effect of the altered wrapper while holding specified information fixed. They do not automatically identify a career-long return or hold every unobserved interpretation constant.

## 16. Authority-access outcome

Do not stop at wages. Measure who gets to make consequential decisions.

Outcomes:

- managing employees;
- budget authority;
- hiring/firing authority;
- investment authority;
- board membership;
- political appointment;
- clerkships;
- judgeships;
- senior civil service;
- venture-capital / private-equity decision roles;
- editorial gatekeeping;
- faculty hiring / admissions committee participation.

The Blackball hypothesis is not just unequal reward after competition, but unequal access to the arena in which high-stakes competence can be demonstrated. Measure that access before stating its magnitude or cause.

## 17. Late-bloomer test

Track people whose meaningful competence becomes visible after the usual adolescent/college sorting period.

Questions:

- Do institutions reopen gates?
- How expensive is re-entry?
- Are later accomplishments discounted because pedigree was not acquired at 18–22?
- How much does age interact with class and family obligations?
- Can work samples replace credentials?
- Which occupations genuinely allow demonstrated adult competence to erase youthful ranking?

## 18. Joint accumulation and chain test

Estimate how links combine rather than presuming literal multiplication. A defensible chain needs temporal order, compatible populations and units, explicit mediators, and attention to shared causes, selection, interaction, and offsetting effects.

Example schematic:

`parental wealth`
→ better school / more enrichment
→ stronger application
→ selective college
→ high-status peers
→ prestigious first job
→ higher income + better network
→ high-resource spouse
→ expensive neighborhood
→ children's enrichment

For each arrow estimate:

- effect size and estimand;
- uncertainty;
- population and period;
- whether the arrow is causal, associational, or only proposed;
- treatment, comparison group, mediator, and outcome;
- whether adjacent estimates can legitimately be combined;
- whether a shared cause explains multiple links;
- whether advantage is zero-sum / positional;
- whether the arrow differs by gender, race, class, geography, or cohort.

Do not multiply coefficients imported from separate studies, double-count a mediator and its downstream outcome, or interpret a sequence of conditional associations as a person-level multiplier. The total system can in principle be strongly unequal even if no single link is enormous, but that is a proposition to estimate rather than a result supplied by the diagram.

## 19. Desert audit

For any reward attached to a bionic-hamster trait, write five columns:

| Trait | Causal productivity | Scarcity / positional value | Inherited component | Social preference / bias | Claimed moral desert |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

Examples:

- height;
- elite degree;
- beauty;
- confidence;
- standardized-test score;
- leadership title;
- national athletic award;
- parent network;
- social fluency.

The purpose is to prevent movement from **market price** to **moral worth** without argument.

## 20. Policy / institutional questions

Do not begin with a preferred policy. First identify the mechanism.

Possible interventions to evaluate:

- class-weighted admissions;
- elimination of legacy preferences;
- limits on listed extracurriculars;
- greater value for paid work / family care in admissions;
- blind evaluation where feasible;
- structured interviews;
- work-sample hiring;
- wage floors;
- stronger bargaining institutions;
- transparent promotion criteria;
- late-entry pathways into high-status occupations;
- public funding of enrichment;
- reduced reliance on prestige credentials;
- anti-lookism law where proposed or existing;
- pay reform in care occupations.

For every intervention ask about gaming, substitution, measurement error, and unintended new arms races.

## 21. Anti-caricature rules

1. Do not assume rich = incompetent.
2. Do not assume beautiful = immoral.
3. Do not assume elite-school graduate = sheep.
4. Do not assume ordinary-looking = virtuous.
5. Do not assume working-class = unstrategic or morally pure.
6. Do not assume every extracurricular is résumé padding.
7. Do not assume social skill is fake.
8. Do not assume markets cannot reward genuine differences in contribution.
9. Do not infer desert from market reward.
10. Do not infer absence of injustice merely because every individual transaction can be described as voluntary.
11. Do not infer causality from elite campus photographs.
12. Do not turn a regional stereotype into a fact without evidence.
13. Do not use “social skill” as a synonym for charisma, beauty, confidence, dominance, polish, or pedigree.
14. Do not splice coefficients from incompatible studies into a cumulative-effect estimate.
15. Do not describe an observational association as a causal arrow.
16. Do not generalize a laboratory result, historical cohort, country, occupation, or gender beyond its sampled setting without a separate argument.

## 22. Minimum useful output for each source

For every book, article, dataset, song, memoir, interview, or anecdote added here, record:

- source type and publication status;
- exact claim;
- design and estimand;
- mechanism measured versus mechanism merely proposed;
- population and sampling frame;
- time/place;
- treatment or exposure, comparison group, and outcome;
- whether the result is causal, associational, descriptive, interpretive, or anecdotal;
- uncertainty and evidence strength;
- obvious confounders and external-validity limits;
- what would falsify the claim;
- which arrow in the amplification chain it informs;
- whether adjacent arrows have actually been studied together;
- whether it bears on causation, justice, or both.

That keeps “bionic hamsters” from becoming a vague label and turns it into a reusable research program.
