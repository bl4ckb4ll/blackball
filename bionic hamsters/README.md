# Bionic hamsters

## Purpose

Blackball adopts William Deresiewicz's phrase **“bionic hamsters”** from *Excellent Sheep* and broadens it into an analytical category.

Deresiewicz used the phrase for the hyper-accomplished students produced by the modern elite-college admissions race: many AP courses, many extracurricular activities, leadership, service, awards, polish, and an ability to satisfy every visible criterion. Blackball's question is larger:

> What happens when childhood advantage, family resources, appearance, social ease, strategic optimization, elite selection, networks, and institutional trust repeatedly reinforce one another across a lifetime and then across generations?

This is **not** a folder about hating rich people, attractive people, ambitious people, Ivy League students, athletes, polished people, or people who were good at high school. It is not primarily an argument that Harvard selected applicant A when applicant B deserved the seat more.

The object of study is the **amplification machine**.

## Core claim to test

A society can begin with differences that are morally small, accidental, inherited, or specific to childhood and then convert them into very large differences in adult income, authority, ownership, security, social deference, and access to consequential decisions.

Examples of initial differences include:

- parental income and wealth;
- neighborhood and school;
- parents' education and institutional knowledge;
- coaching, tutoring, test preparation, travel, enrichment, and free time;
- beauty, height, grooming, voice, confidence, and ease in social settings;
- adolescent résumé optimization;
- athletic, musical, academic, or social opportunities;
- willingness and ability to treat every selection criterion as something to maximize;
- being admitted to a high-status institution;
- meeting high-status friends, spouses, professors, employers, investors, and authority figures.

None of these automatically establishes moral merit. Some reflect genuine skill and effort. Some are inherited. Some are luck. Some are learned forms of presentation. Some are positional advantages whose value depends on other people not having them. Usually the real case is a mixture.

The Blackball question is not “did this person earn literally nothing?” It is:

> **How much adult economic and political consequence is logically justified by the trait being rewarded?**

A sixteen-year-old being captain of three clubs may be evidence of energy and reliability. It does not by itself answer what a forty-year-old warehouse worker, home-health aide, janitor, machinist, or cashier deserves to be paid.

## Admissions fairness is not adult distributive justice

Keep these two questions separate.

### Question A: Did the admissions office choose correctly?

This can become extremely fine-grained:

- academics versus extracurriculars;
- depth versus breadth;
- athletics versus music;
- class rank versus standardized tests;
- legacy preferences;
- essays and recommendation letters;
- “well-rounded” versus “pointy” applicants;
- socioeconomic adjustment;
- race-conscious or race-neutral admissions;
- possible gaming of disability accommodations or other rules.

Those questions can matter. They are not the center of this folder.

### Question B: Why should adolescent optimization determine adult life chances so strongly?

Suppose the admissions office is perfectly fair by its own rules. That still does not establish that:

- a résumé maximized at age 16 should affect wages at 35;
- elite-school selection should produce lifelong authority;
- a teenager's access to enrichment should become an adult's access to capital;
- a prestigious first job should recursively open later prestigious jobs;
- attractive or socially smooth people should receive radically greater economic security;
- people who were ordinary adolescents should accept poverty wages for the next fifty years.

The second question survives even if the first is solved perfectly.

## Do not blame the hamster

The term describes a social process, not an evil personality type.

A beautiful person did not commit a wrong by being beautiful. A child with rich parents did not choose the family. A teenager who worked hard to satisfy an admissions system may have behaved completely reasonably. A person who is charming may actually be kind and socially skilled. A student who wins national competitions may genuinely be exceptional.

The mistake is to jump from:

> this person possesses traits that institutions reward

straight to:

> therefore whatever income, authority, access, comfort, or deference follows is deserved, and those without the traits deserve much less.

Blackball should repeatedly separate **causal advantage**, **demonstrated competence**, **moral character**, **economic bargaining power**, and **desert**.

## The amplification chain

A useful default model is:

**family position → childhood environment → opportunity set → optimization → selective institution → peer network → first job → later jobs / capital / authority → spouse / household → children's environment**

The arrows are hypotheses to measure, not assumptions to repeat.

There can also be side channels:

**appearance → confidence / treatment by others → social opportunities → hiring / pay / promotion**

**wealth → grooming / dentistry / clothing / leisure / health → presentation → treatment by others**

**elite institution → social legitimacy → presumption of competence → access to consequential roles**

**income / education / prestige → assortative mating → household concentration of resources → next generation**

The important object is the repeated multiplication, not any single coefficient.

## Beauty, lookism, and charisma

Beauty belongs in the model because it is an observable social variable with measurable consequences. Treat it neither as mystical nor trivial.

Questions include:

- wage premiums and penalties associated with rated attractiveness;
- whether appearance changes hiring before any work is observed;
- how much of a beauty premium operates through confidence;
- how much operates through employers incorrectly inferring ability;
- how grooming expenditures interact with class;
- height, weight, teeth, hair, skin, clothing, voice, accent, posture, and facial attractiveness;
- whether occupations sort people by appearance;
- effects on promotion, sales, media work, management, politics, and customer-facing jobs;
- marriage-market effects and household wealth;
- cumulative treatment over decades.

Do not reduce “charisma” to fraud. Social skill can be genuinely productive. David Deming's labor-market work is useful precisely because it creates a harder question: if social skill really improves coordination and team production, what portion of its reward is productive and what portion is social preference, class-coded polish, appearance, or gatekeeping?

Also do not make the reverse mistake. Even if charisma is productive, that does not establish that a low-charisma person deserves humiliation, insecurity, or wages below a decent life.

## Assortative mating and intergenerational amplification

The folk observation “rich men marry beautiful women” should be turned into testable pieces rather than treated as a universal law.

Research separately:

- income-income assortative mating;
- education-education assortative mating;
- occupation and prestige matching;
- attractiveness-attractiveness matching;
- possible beauty-status exchange;
- where couples meet;
- how colleges and elite workplaces alter the marriage market;
- how marriage combines two sets of income, wealth, education, networks, and cultural knowledge;
- how those combined resources shape children's neighborhoods, schools, health, appearance-related investment, extracurricular access, and admissions strategy.

The point is **reproduction of advantage**, not resentment of whom anyone marries.

The 2026 NBER paper by Abramitzky, Pérez, and Price on colleges and assortative mating is especially relevant because it directly studies colleges as marriage markets and follows educational advantage into the next generation.

## Optimization culture

A “bionic hamster” is partly a product of a selection system that makes adolescents optimize many visible dimensions at once.

Track:

- AP / advanced-course counts;
- extracurricular counts;
- leadership titles;
- national and regional competitions;
- varsity athletics;
- music credentials;
- volunteer hours;
- internships and research opportunities;
- tutoring and admissions consultants;
- standardized-test preparation;
- strategic school choice;
- essay coaching;
- résumé padding;
- opportunities purchased directly or indirectly by parents;
- gaming or exploiting rules where documented;
- how much time remains for reading, thinking, friendship, wandering, boredom, deep practice, or one consuming intellectual interest.

Deresiewicz's critique matters here: a system can reward the ability to satisfy specifications while suppressing independent direction and deep intellectual curiosity.

### Narrow depth as a counterexample

Preserve the counterexample of the student who spends high school mostly reading mathematics and doing problems because that is the activity itself, not because it fills five admissions boxes.

A system may claim to select excellence while penalizing exactly the kind of single-mindedness that later produces unusual work.

## The adult-dignity break

A central Blackball rule:

> **High school ends. Adult moral status does not remain a permanent ranking generated by adolescence.**

A person who did not maximize a résumé at 16 is still an adult citizen and worker at 26, 46, or 66.

Keep asking whether institutions quietly treat adolescent sorting as if it established a lifelong caste ranking.

Possible chain:

**high-school distinction → selective college → prestigious first employer → network → managerial authority → capital access**

versus:

**ordinary high school → nonselective college / no college → low-status first job → weak network → repeated proof requirements → low bargaining power**

The issue is not whether the first person “worked hard.” The issue is whether early sorting becomes self-validating and whether the second person ever gets a comparable arena in which judgment, intelligence, reliability, courage, or leadership can be demonstrated.

## Care work as a moral and economic counterexample

Keep low-paid care work adjacent to this folder.

Someone feeding an elderly person, cleaning them, helping a disabled child communicate, keeping a confused patient safe, or patiently assisting a person with basic daily needs may be displaying traits that institutions routinely praise in speeches:

- patience;
- reliability;
- emotional control;
- attentiveness;
- physical endurance;
- responsibility for another person;
- kindness;
- practical judgment;
- willingness to perform unpleasant necessary work.

Yet care occupations have often carried measurable wage penalties relative to other work after controls.

This creates a direct test of the prestige machine:

> If society says character, service, responsibility, and contribution matter, what happens when those traits appear in a low-status worker rather than in an elite application essay?

The point is not to romanticize care workers. It is to compare **what institutions say they value** with **what labor markets and status systems actually reward**.

## Interiority and cumulative treatment

Do not reduce this topic to earnings regressions.

Repeated preference can enter a person's self-conception:

- the attractive sibling who receives warmer attention;
- the polished child who adults assume is promising;
- the awkward child who becomes accustomed to exclusion;
- the employee whose ideas are ignored until repeated by a high-status colleague;
- the aging adult who still remembers decades of comparison.

Anecdotes cannot establish population magnitudes. They can identify mechanisms and questions worth measuring.

Blackball should preserve both levels:

1. population evidence about average effects;
2. qualitative evidence about what repeated differential treatment feels like from the inside.

## Gender and sexualization

Beauty, class, sex, and power overlap, but the folder must avoid crude one-way stories.

Historically and presently, women are often evaluated more heavily through appearance, sexual desirability, age, grooming, and body presentation. Girls can receive sexualized attention long before adulthood. Beauty can create opportunities and also expose a person to harassment, possession, coercion, or unwanted attention. Men can also receive appearance premiums and penalties, and some labor-market studies find substantial effects for men.

Research should therefore distinguish:

- advantage from attractiveness;
- costs of being judged primarily by attractiveness;
- consensual use of attractiveness as an economic or social resource;
- coercion and exploitation;
- class constraints on choices;
- gender differences in appearance rewards and penalties;
- childhood sexualization from adult marriage-market behavior.

Do not turn “beautiful women sometimes marry wealthy men” into an explanation of women as a class. Do not erase beauty as a real source of unequal treatment either.

## Winner optimization versus fair-play norms

The user describes a recognizable **win-at-every-margin** social type: squeeze every test rule, every résumé line, every connection, every appeal, every edge, provided it increases the probability of winning.

This should be studied as a cultural and institutional pattern, not encoded as “East Coast people are like this.” The geographical stereotype may itself be evidence worth documenting, but the measurable question is about norms:

- Is winning treated as sufficient justification?
- Are some advantages regarded as unsporting even when legal?
- Do institutions reward aggressive optimization more than restraint?
- Are people who decline to exploit every edge systematically selected out of elite pipelines?
- Does repeated selection produce leaders who regard every system primarily as a game to be optimized?

Contrast a **fair-play norm** (“how you play the game matters”) with a **maximization norm** (“use every permitted edge”). Neither should be assigned to a region without evidence.

## Friedman test case

Milton Friedman's *Capitalism and Freedom*, chapter VII, explicitly compares a preference for an attractive servant over an ugly servant with other discriminatory tastes. That passage belongs here because it sharply exposes a dispute Blackball wants to examine.

Friedman's framework asks what tastes individuals should be legally free to act upon and emphasizes the costs discriminators may bear in competitive markets.

Blackball's separate question is:

> Even when a preference exists and even when it is privately chosen, what social and economic consequences should follow from it—and what happens when the same preference is repeated by thousands of employers, customers, schools, voters, and spouses?

Do not attribute to Friedman the paraphrase “that's just the way it is” unless an exact source is found. Use the documented passage instead.

## Cultural probes

These are not statistical evidence. They are compact cultural representations of mechanisms the research can test.

- **William Deresiewicz, *Excellent Sheep*** — source of “bionic hamsters”; elite résumé arms race, credentialism, conformity, and hyper-achievement.
- **Bobbie Gentry, “Fancy”** — poverty, female appearance/sexuality, class mobility, powerful men, and the desperation behind using the resources available to escape deprivation. Reba McEntire's later recording made the song especially prominent in country music.
- **Billie Holiday and Arthur Herzog Jr., “God Bless the Child”** — possession of resources as self-reinforcing independence; useful for the “them that's got” / cumulative-advantage theme.
- **“Summertime” from *Porgy and Bess*** — the famous image of a child protected by a rich father and good-looking mother; useful as a cultural shorthand for inherited family position.
- ***The Karate Kid*** — Daniel LaRusso / Johnny Lawrence as a pop-cultural rich-suburban-antagonist contrast. Use as cultural archetype, not social science.

## What would falsify or weaken the framework?

This folder should not become a machine for confirming itself.

Evidence against strong versions would include:

- weak or disappearing beauty effects after credible controls;
- little persistence from parental advantage once genuine skill is measured;
- elite credentials adding little after selection effects are handled;
- substantial late-life mobility that overwhelms early sorting;
- employers quickly correcting appearance or pedigree bias after observing performance;
- low correlation between elite-school attendance and access to authority after background is controlled;
- weak assortative mating by income, education, status, or attractiveness;
- childhood résumé intensity failing to predict later elite outcomes;
- care-work wage differences fully explained by measurable compensating factors rather than devaluation or bargaining power.

The purpose is not to prove that every advantage compounds. It is to find which ones do, how strongly, for whom, through what institutions, and with what consequences.

## Working rule

When Blackball encounters a very successful person, do not ask only:

> Did this person work hard and possess real ability?

Also ask:

> What inputs were inherited? What was optimized? What did institutions amplify? Which advantages were positional? Which later opportunities existed only because of earlier selection? What was actually demonstrated? What did other people never get a chance to demonstrate? And why should any of this determine the floor beneath everybody else's adult life?
