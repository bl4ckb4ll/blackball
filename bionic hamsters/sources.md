# Bionic hamsters — source map

This is a starter bibliography and evidence map. Separate cultural examples, hypotheses, and empirical studies.

## Origin / elite-education frame

### William Deresiewicz — *Excellent Sheep: The Miseducation of the American Elite and the Way to a Meaningful Life* (2014)

Core source for the phrase **“bionic hamsters.”** Deresiewicz describes modern elite applicants as an almost alien class of hyper-accomplished students produced by escalating admissions competition. Useful topics:

- AP-course inflation;
- extracurricular inflation;
- résumé stuffing;
- “well-rounded” versus highly “pointy” applicants;
- credentialism;
- external validation;
- instrumental treatment of activities;
- risk aversion;
- independent-mindedness versus pleasing selectors;
- elite education reproducing class hierarchy.

Author page / book page:
https://billderesiewicz.com/books/excellent-sheep/

Useful later interview in which Deresiewicz describes students doing many extracurriculars, AP courses, leadership, service, an instrument, foreign language, etc. because those are the visible specifications of elite “excellence”:
https://www.persuasion.community/p/deresiewicz

Keep Blackball's extension distinct from Deresiewicz's own argument. The folder is not merely a summary of *Excellent Sheep*.

## Beauty / appearance and labor markets

### Daniel S. Hamermesh and Jeff E. Biddle — “Beauty and the Labor Market”

NBER Working Paper 4518 (1993); published in *American Economic Review* 84(5), 1994, pp. 1174–1194.

https://www.nber.org/papers/w4518

Reported findings include:

- respondents rated as plain earned less than average-looking respondents;
- good-looking respondents earned more;
- the estimated “plainness penalty” was approximately 5–10 percent in the studied data;
- effects were at least as large for men in their analysis;
- occupational sorting explained only part of the earnings relationship.

Use as foundational evidence that appearance can affect labor-market outcomes. Do not treat a 1990s estimate as a timeless universal constant.

### Markus M. Mobius and Tanya S. Rosenblat — “Why Beauty Matters”

*American Economic Review* 96(1), 2006, pp. 222–235.

https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F000282806776157515

Experimental labor market using a maze-solving task. Particularly valuable because task skill was not improved by attractiveness, allowing the authors to investigate transmission mechanisms. They report channels involving:

- worker confidence;
- employers inferring greater ability from attractiveness;
- oral / social interaction effects.

This paper is a clean bridge between beauty, confidence, perceived competence, and wages.

### Daniel S. Hamermesh, Xin Meng, and Junsen Zhang — “Dress for Success — Does Primping Pay?”

NBER Working Paper 7167 (1999); later *Labour Economics*.

https://www.nber.org/papers/w7167

Uses Shanghai data combining labor-market information, beauty ratings, and household spending. Relevant to the question of whether class resources can partly purchase presentation advantages through clothing and cosmetics. Their estimates suggest grooming expenditures have diminishing effects and are not simply a high-return earnings investment.

## Social skills / charisma

### David J. Deming — “The Growing Importance of Social Skills in the Labor Market”

*Quarterly Journal of Economics* 132(4), 2017, pp. 1593–1640.

https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjx022

Finds substantial growth in jobs requiring social interaction and stronger employment/wage growth in jobs combining social and mathematical skills. Deming models social skill as reducing coordination costs in team production.

Use this against simplistic versions of the “charisma is fake” claim. Some social skill can be genuinely productive. The Blackball problem is to distinguish productive coordination ability from beauty bias, class-coded polish, confidence effects, pedigree, and social preference—and then to distinguish productivity from moral desert.

## Intergenerational mobility / birth lottery

### Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren, Patrick Kline, Emmanuel Saez — “Where is the Land of Opportunity? The Geography of Intergenerational Mobility in the United States”

*Quarterly Journal of Economics* 129(4), 2014.

https://www.nber.org/papers/w19843

Administrative data on more than 40 million children and parents. Parent income rank predicts child income rank, with large geographic variation in upward mobility.

Use for the basic fact that family position and childhood location remain related to adult outcomes.

### Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren, Patrick Kline, Emmanuel Saez, Nicholas Turner — “Is the United States Still a Land of Opportunity? Recent Trends in Intergenerational Mobility”

*American Economic Review* 104(5), 2014.

https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.104.5.141

Important formulation: rank mobility for the studied cohorts was fairly stable, while rising inequality increased the consequences of the “birth lottery.” Useful for separating relative mobility from the stakes attached to where one starts.

### Raj Chetty et al. — *The Opportunity Atlas*

Published in *American Economic Review* 116(1), 2026; project work began earlier.

https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20200108

Maps adult outcomes by childhood census tract using large longitudinal administrative data. Useful for neighborhood effects and for resisting stories in which adult outcomes are treated as if they originated wholly inside the individual.

## Selective colleges and class

### Raj Chetty, John Friedman, Emmanuel Saez, Nicholas Turner, Danny Yagan — “Mobility Report Cards: Income Segregation and Intergenerational Mobility Across Colleges in the United States”

*Quarterly Journal of Economics* 135(3), 2020.

https://opportunityinsights.org/paper/undermatching/

Key relevance: students from low-income families can have excellent outcomes at selective schools, but relatively few low-income students attend them. Useful for distinguishing what selective institutions may do from who gets access to them.

## Assortative mating / reproduction of advantage

### Lasse Eika, Magne Mogstad, Basit Zafar — “Educational Assortative Mating and Household Income Inequality”

NBER Working Paper 20271; revised 2018.

https://www.nber.org/papers/w20271

Documents positive educational assortative mating across several countries and estimates a non-negligible contribution to household income inequality.

### Ran Abramitzky, Santiago Pérez, Joseph Price — “Colleges, Assortative Mating, and Intergenerational Mobility in Twentieth-Century America”

NBER Working Paper 35602, August 2026.

https://www.nber.org/papers/w35602

Especially important for this folder. Uses records for 1.2 million students at 60 coeducational U.S. colleges, 1915–1943, linked to later records. The authors report that more exposure to opposite-sex classmates and more campus overlap causally increased same-college marriage; children of same-college couples later attended selective colleges at higher rates.

This provides a direct empirical route for:

**college selection → spouse pool → household educational advantage → children's educational outcomes**.

### Beauty–status exchange

There is a literature on whether physical attractiveness is exchanged for socioeconomic status in mate selection. Results vary by society, period, measurement, and model, so do not elevate the folk claim “rich men marry beautiful women” into a universal law.

One recent example:

“Beauty–status exchange in mate selection in China,” *Research in Social Stratification and Mobility* 89 (2024), 100872.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rssm.2023.100872

Use as one case, not as a general proof.

Research also needs work on attractiveness-attractiveness matching, income matching, wealth matching, and how elite schools/workplaces alter meeting pools.

## Care work / mismatch between social value and pay

### Paula England, Michelle Budig, Nancy Folbre — “Wages of Virtue: The Relative Pay of Care Work”

*Social Problems* 49(4), 2002, pp. 455–473.

https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/our-work/working-papers/2002/ipr-wp-02-07.html

Using NLSY panel data, the authors report that care work pays less than other occupations after controls for education, experience, job characteristics, and stable individual characteristics. Both men and women in care work experienced the relative penalty in their analysis.

Use this as a direct counterpoint to prestige rhetoric about service, character, leadership, and contribution.

### Michelle Budig — “How care-work employment shapes earnings in cross-national perspective”

*International Labour Review* 149(4), 2010/2011.

https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1564-913X.2010.00097.x

Examines 12 countries and finds substantial cross-national variation in care-work wage effects. Useful because it shows that the penalty is institutionally variable rather than a simple natural price of care.

### Nancy Folbre, Leila Gautham, Kristin Smith — “Essential Workers and Care Penalties in the United States”

*Feminist Economics* 27(1–2), 2021.

https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13545701.2020.1828602

Connects essential care work to wage penalties, bargaining power, burnout, and turnover.

## Milton Friedman / discrimination as taste

### Milton Friedman — *Capitalism and Freedom*, chapter VII, “Capitalism and Discrimination”

University of Chicago Press.

Google Books metadata:
https://books.google.com/books/about/Capitalism_and_Freedom.html?id=iCRk066ybDAC

Friedman explicitly asks whether there is a difference in principle between a householder preferring an attractive servant to an ugly servant and preferences based on race, apart from which tastes observers approve of. He also argues that discriminators bear costs in competitive markets and opposes coercive regulation of many private employment choices.

Blackball should read the entire chapter rather than rely on paraphrase.

Research questions:

- Does competition eliminate appearance discrimination when customers or employers themselves value appearance?
- What if appearance changes perceived productivity even when true productivity is unchanged?
- What if many independent actors share the same preference?
- What is the difference between explaining an equilibrium and morally justifying its distributive consequences?

Do **not** write that Friedman literally said “that's just the way it is” unless an exact source is found.

## Cultural sources / hypothesis generators

These belong in the folder because they compress class mechanisms into memorable stories. They are not substitutes for empirical evidence.

### Bobbie Gentry — “Fancy” (1969)

Written and recorded by Bobbie Gentry; later famously recorded by Reba McEntire.

Useful themes:

- severe poverty;
- grooming and appearance as scarce investment;
- female sexual desirability as a possible route out of poverty;
- powerful male patrons;
- shame, coercive circumstance, agency, and later wealth;
- moral judgment of survival strategies.

PBS performance/context confirming Bobbie Gentry authorship:
https://www.pbs.org/video/tift-merritt-qsnvtv/

### Billie Holiday / Arthur Herzog Jr. — “God Bless the Child”

Official Billie Holiday site:
https://billieholiday.com/song/god-bless-the-child/

Useful for cumulative advantage and the independence conferred by having resources of one's own. Avoid reproducing lyrics beyond brief quotation.

### “Summertime” — *Porgy and Bess*

Music by George Gershwin; lyrics associated with DuBose Heyward and Ira Gershwin, with attribution details worth handling carefully in a dedicated source note.

Useful as a cultural image of inherited protection: a child whose father is rich and mother is good-looking.

### *The Karate Kid* (1984)

Daniel LaRusso / Johnny Lawrence can function as a pop-cultural shorthand for class, polish, suburban advantage, masculinity, and audience sympathy. Do not confuse a film archetype with population evidence.

## Leads requiring verification

### “SL2” / Colin mathematician anecdote

User recalls a Twitter account named **SL2**, possibly a mathematician named Colin, later a mathematics postdoc, who said that in high school he mostly read mathematics books and did the problems rather than constructing a well-rounded admissions résumé.

Current web search has **not** verified the identity. Keep as an unresolved lead.

Do not accidentally substitute Calvin McPhail-Snyder merely because he owns `sl2.site`; that is a different evidentiary fact and does not establish he is the remembered account.

### Attractive students at elite campuses

Do not use casual Instagram/photo comparison as evidence that Harvard, Michigan, or other elite campuses contain more attractive people. If this question is pursued, design a defensible study:

- sampling rules fixed in advance;
- blinded attractiveness ratings;
- comparable age distributions;
- correction for photography selection and social-media self-selection;
- socioeconomic controls;
- gender-separated and pooled analyses;
- preregistered outcomes if possible.

A campus-photo impression is a hypothesis generator only.

## Source-discipline rule

For every claim, label what kind of evidence it is:

- **primary text** — what an author actually said;
- **administrative / observational data**;
- **experiment**;
- **quasi-experiment**;
- **qualitative interview / ethnography**;
- **memoir / anecdote**;
- **cultural representation**;
- **user hypothesis**;
- **unverified lead**.

The bionic-hamster concept is broad enough that sloppy evidence could make it explain everything. The antidote is to preserve the mechanism and evidence type for every arrow.
