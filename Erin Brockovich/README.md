# Erin Brockovich

This folder studies the gap between the **public image of law as justice** and the ordinary economic work lawyers are actually paid to do.

The title is deliberately cultural rather than doctrinal. A teenager can encounter *Erin Brockovich* and reasonably come away with a simple picture: law is how an ordinary person helps injured people confront a powerful corporation, uncovers wrongdoing, and forces a remedy. Erin Brockovich herself was not a lawyer; that makes the example useful rather than defective. The image is of the legal system as an instrument that can make power answerable.

The question is what happens between that image and the eventual job.

A prospective law student may enter with ideas about justice, rights, constitutional principle, public service, or helping people who cannot defend themselves. The downstream labor market may instead reward work such as:

- defending corporations, insurers, employers, landlords, creditors, or governments;
- contesting injury, disability, workers’ compensation, employment, consumer, or benefits claims;
- collecting debts;
- drafting demand letters and other deliberately intimidating communications;
- helping institutions avoid, reduce, settle, delay, or reclassify liability;
- transactional work whose social purpose is much less legible to the person who entered law school wanting to “help people”;
- work chosen primarily because debt, salary requirements, rank, recruiting, or sunk investment make it the feasible path.

None of these categories proves that every lawyer performing the work is acting wrongfully. It does mean that **“became a lawyer” is not a sufficient outcome measure**. Ask what the lawyer actually does, for whom, against whom, under what incentives, and what alternatives were financially available.

## Core transition to measure

**justice image → law-school selection and competition → debt / sunk investment → recruiting and labor-market sorting → actual clients and tasks → who ultimately benefits and who bears the cost**

A graduate can be highly paid and still have traveled very far from the reason law was sold to them or the reason they chose it.

## “Where are the students’ jobs?” is not enough

The next question is **what kind of job?**

A salary number hides the moral and institutional content of the work. $200,000 defending a company against injured workers and $70,000 representing those workers are both “legal employment.” They are not interchangeable outcomes for a student who entered law because of an image of justice.

Similarly, law-school faculty having desirable jobs is evidence about faculty careers, not automatically evidence about the value delivered to students. Academic placement must be reported separately from outside employers independently purchasing the graduate’s legal work.

## Law school as a transformation mechanism

Collect evidence on several mechanisms separately rather than presuming one story:

1. **Selection:** people already inclined toward status, competition, argument, money, or institutional careers may disproportionately enter or succeed.
2. **Grading and rank:** curved grades and high-stakes rank can make classmates competitors.
3. **Competitive socialization:** students may learn that misleading, obstructing, or outmaneuvering peers is part of succeeding.
4. **Legal reasoning:** learning to construct arguments for either side can create distance between ordinary moral judgment and professional role.
5. **Recruiting:** large firms offer the clearest, best-paid, most prestigious path at exactly the moment students carry debt and uncertainty.
6. **Sunk investment and identity:** after years spent becoming a lawyer, “I have to make this lawyer thing work” can be both a predictable psychological response and a rational response to the new constraint set.
7. **Professional role morality:** lawyers may distinguish advancing a client’s lawful interest from endorsing the client’s conduct.
8. **Language and procedural expertise:** professional language can make coercion sound technical, neutral, or inaccessible while still carrying severe consequences.
9. **Access asymmetry:** the institutions most able to pay for sophisticated legal services can repeatedly purchase expertise that ordinary people cannot afford.

Do not collapse these mechanisms into a single vague claim that law school “makes people bad.” Identify which mechanism operated, when, and with what downstream work.

## Competition and gamesmanship

Robert Granfield’s *Making Elite Lawyers: Visions of Law at Harvard and Beyond* is a central source. Descriptions of his ethnographic findings emphasize a first-year legal consciousness valuing **gamesmanship over ideals** and **competition and victory over right and wrong**, followed by elite-firm recruiting that supplies a path toward status, power, and money.

This belongs beside Anh Do’s account in *The Happiest Refugee* of disliking law school’s competitive culture and wanting greater concern with right and wrong.

Recurring stories about hiding library books, lying about study habits, withholding outlines, or sabotaging shared materials support more than the claim that the stories are memorable. The same conduct appears across source types, time, and institutions. The exact population rate remains open, but recurrence and cultural prevalence are already evidenced. Even supposedly opposite accounts normally say “not here” or “not that bad,” while invoking the same recognizable practices.

See [law-school competition and gamesmanship](law-school-competition-and-gamesmanship.md).

## Professed values versus conduct

Bryan Stevenson supplies one of the most important observations in the corpus. In *Just Mercy*, describing Steve Bright, Stevenson writes:

> “He showed none of the disconnect between what he did and what he believed that I’d seen in so many of my law professors.”

That sentence is an accountability test, not merely a complaint about Harvard professors. Compare professed commitments to justice, equality, public service, or professional ethics with actual work, institutional incentives, clients, and conduct.

## Lawyer work without the heroic speech

Lawyers the user knows have described their own work in ordinary terms such as writing vaguely threatening letters: comply, pay, stop, accept the demand, or face an expensive next step. Legal communities also recognize degrees of institutional language that outsiders may not read correctly—what insiders call extraordinarily strong language can look mild to the public.

The same asymmetry appears in court. A self-represented person may expect to explain the injustice in a great speech and instead lose on service, jurisdiction, pleading, evidence, deadline, preservation, or the form of requested relief. The represented repeat player possesses not necessarily the better moral position but the ability to translate its position into legally usable moves.

See [lawyer work as calibrated threat](lawyer-work-as-calibrated-threat.md).

## Workplace safety and injury claims

“Health and safety” can describe genuine hazard prevention. It can also include protecting the employer against recordable injuries, workers’ compensation costs, disability claims, regulatory penalties, and lawsuits. After an injury, staff may investigate whether the physical hazard should be fixed—or whether responsibility can be shifted onto the employee.

The user reports seeing Amazon attempt to place responsibility for a workplace incident or injury on the employee. That remains a first-person lead until reconstructed at the incident level. The general incentive conflict is independently recognized in OSHA guidance concerning retaliation, reporting disincentives, injury-rate bonuses, and no-fault recordkeeping.

For both safety careers and legal careers, inspect the ordinary task: prevent harm, correct hazards, manage the claim, reduce reported rates, contest causation, discipline the worker, or defend the institution.

See [workplace safety: worker protection or loss control?](workplace-safety-worker-protection-or-loss-control.md).

## The class question: law on paper versus usable law

There is a large distance between:

- constitutional theory, appellate doctrine, law-review argument, Supreme Court amicus work, podcasts, and carefully developed jurisprudential positions; and
- an ordinary person who needs a divorce, eviction defense, disability appeal, debt response, expungement, benefits claim, custody order, probate filing, workplace-injury claim, or help understanding a court form.

The existence of sophisticated legal principles does not establish practical access to those principles. Measure the cost of representation, lawyer availability, self-representation, form complexity, wait times, eligibility limits, unserved legal needs, and what happens when a person cannot afford the procedural expertise needed to vindicate a substantive right.

This is where money and class re-enter jurisprudence.

## Washtenaw County local test

Washtenaw County provides a concrete local test rather than an abstract national claim. Official sources describe both a lawyer-referral service and reduced-fee or modest-means pathways. The user’s experience is that two supposedly useful routes produced no usable representation in practice.

The case should be reconstructed precisely: date, legal problem, eligibility, advertised terms, calls and referrals, fees, whether a lawyer was actually available, and where the process failed. But the official existence of a program must not be treated as evidence that the customer received counsel.

**Front door:** a referral or modest-means program exists.

**Back door:** did this person obtain a lawyer capable of handling the matter at a price the person could pay?

## No compulsory reassuring conclusion

The purpose is to disclose the actual distribution of legal education and legal work. For some students the evidence may support becoming a lawyer. For others it may support not enrolling. Blackball does not require a reassuring conclusion and does not add exceptional success stories merely to offset discouraging evidence.

A path should count as hope only after it becomes concrete: number of openings, selection rules, cost, debt, probability, geography, actual clients, ordinary tasks, and who bears the loss if it fails.

The minimum outcome is that a student who proceeds cannot plausibly say afterward:

> Nobody told me this was what the job was.

## Questions for prospective law students

Do not stop at median salary, bar passage, or employment rate. Ask:

- What work do graduates actually perform five and ten years later?
- Who are their clients, and who is on the other side?
- What percentage enters public-interest, government, plaintiff, defense, corporate, prosecutor, debt-collection, insurance, compliance, transactional, academic, and non-law work?
- How strongly are those destinations associated with debt, family money, school rank, and class rank?
- What did students say they wanted before law school, and what did they actually do afterward?
- At which stage did their plans change: classroom, grades, debt, recruiting, job market, or actual practice?
- How many would choose the same path again knowing the work they eventually performed?
- How often does legal education create access to justice, and how often does it create people paid to manage, contest, delay, price, or threaten around claims?
- What does an ordinary person have to pay to obtain the expertise produced by schools that describe law as public service?
- What legal work is socially necessary but unable to support the debt imposed by legal education?
- Which employers can pay enough to purchase graduates constrained by that debt?
