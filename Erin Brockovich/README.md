# Erin Brockovich

This folder studies the gap between the **public image of law as justice** and the ordinary economic work lawyers are actually paid to do.

The title is deliberately cultural rather than doctrinal. A teenager can encounter *Erin Brockovich* and reasonably come away with a simple picture: law is how an ordinary person helps injured people confront a powerful corporation, uncovers wrongdoing, and forces a remedy. Erin Brockovich herself was not a lawyer; that makes the example useful rather than defective. The image is of the legal system as an instrument that can make power answerable.

The Blackball question is what happens between that image and the eventual job.

A prospective law student may enter with ideas about justice, rights, constitutional principle, public service, or helping people who cannot defend themselves. The downstream labor market may instead reward work such as:

- defending corporations, insurers, employers, landlords, creditors, or governments;
- contesting injury, disability, workers' compensation, employment, consumer, or benefits claims;
- collecting debts;
- drafting demand letters and other deliberately intimidating communications;
- helping institutions avoid, reduce, settle, or reclassify liability;
- transactional work whose social purpose is much less legible to the person who entered law school wanting to "help people";
- work chosen primarily because debt, salary requirements, rank, recruiting, or sunk investment make it the feasible path.

None of these categories proves that a particular lawyer is doing something wrongful. The point is to stop treating **"became a lawyer"** as a sufficient outcome measure. Ask what the lawyer actually does, for whom, against whom, under what incentives, and what alternatives were financially available.

## Core transition to measure

**justice image → law-school selection and competition → debt / sunk investment → recruiting and labor-market sorting → actual clients and tasks → who ultimately benefits and who bears the cost**

This is related to but broader than the question of whether law graduates are employed. A graduate can be highly paid and still have traveled very far from the reason law was sold to them or the reason they chose it.

## "Where are the students' jobs?" is not enough

The next question is **what kind of job?**

A salary number hides the moral and institutional content of the work. $200,000 defending a company against injured workers and $70,000 representing those workers are both "legal employment." They are not interchangeable outcomes for a student who entered law because of an image of justice.

Similarly, law-school faculty having desirable jobs is evidence about faculty careers, not automatically evidence about the value delivered to students.

## Law school as a possible transformation mechanism

Collect evidence on several mechanisms separately rather than presuming one story:

1. **Selection:** people already inclined toward status, competition, argument, money, or institutional careers may disproportionately enter or succeed.
2. **Grading and rank:** curved grades and high-stakes rank can make classmates competitors.
3. **Legal reasoning:** learning to construct arguments for either side may sharpen useful analytic skill while also creating distance between personal moral judgment and professional role.
4. **Recruiting:** large firms can offer the clearest, best-paid, most prestigious path at exactly the moment students carry debt and uncertainty.
5. **Sunk investment and identity:** after years spent becoming a lawyer, "I have to make this lawyer thing work" is both a common psychological response and, because debt and specialized career capital remain, sometimes a rational response to the new constraint set.
6. **Professional role morality:** lawyers may come to regard advancing the client's lawful interest as distinct from endorsing the client's conduct.
7. **Language and procedural expertise:** professional language can make coercion sound technical, neutral, or inaccessible to outsiders while still carrying serious consequences for the recipient.

Do not collapse these mechanisms into "law school makes people bad." Measure which ones are actually supported.

## Competition and gamesmanship

Robert Granfield's *Making Elite Lawyers: Visions of Law at Harvard and Beyond* is a central source. The publisher's description summarizes his ethnographic finding that first-year legal education can produce a legal consciousness valuing **gamesmanship over ideals** and **competition and victory over right and wrong**, followed by elite-firm recruiting that supplies a path toward status, power, and money.

This belongs beside Anh Do's account in *The Happiest Refugee* of disliking the competitive culture of law school and wanting greater emphasis on right and wrong.

The recurring law-school stories about hiding library books, lying about study habits, withholding outlines, or sabotaging shared materials should be treated carefully. They do not establish a population prevalence rate merely because they recur. But their **cultural salience is itself documentable**: schools, students, admissions officials, test-prep companies, and law-school policies repeatedly invoke "hiding books" as the recognizable example of cutthroat law-school competition. Even denials often take the form "our school is not one of those places where students hide books." See [law-school-competition-and-gamesmanship.md](law-school-competition-and-gamesmanship.md).

## Professed values versus conduct

Bryan Stevenson supplies one of the most important observations in the corpus. In *Just Mercy*, describing Steve Bright, Stevenson writes:

> "He showed none of the disconnect between what he did and what he believed that I'd seen in so many of my law professors."

That sentence should be read as an accountability test, not merely a complaint about Harvard professors. Compare professed commitments to justice, equality, public service, or professional ethics with actual work, institutional incentives, clients, and conduct.

See the Blackball Stevenson source note and Harvard pointer.

## The class question: law on paper versus usable law

A separate branch of this folder asks what law is worth to a person who cannot afford to use it.

There is a large distance between:

- constitutional theory, appellate doctrine, law-review argument, Supreme Court amicus work, podcasts, and carefully developed jurisprudential positions; and
- an ordinary person who needs a divorce, eviction defense, disability appeal, debt response, expungement, benefits claim, custody order, probate filing, or help understanding a court form.

The existence of sophisticated legal principles does not by itself establish practical access to those principles. Measure the cost of representation, availability of counsel, self-representation rates, form complexity, wait times, eligibility limits, unserved legal needs, and what happens when a person cannot afford the procedural expertise required to vindicate a substantive right.

This is where money and class re-enter jurisprudence.

See [access-to-law-and-class.md](access-to-law-and-class.md).

## Washtenaw County local test

Washtenaw County provides a concrete local test rather than an abstract national claim. Official sources describe both a lawyer-referral service and reduced-fee / modest-means pathways. The user's experience is that two supposedly useful routes produced no usable representation in practice. That experience should be reconstructed precisely before being generalized: date, legal problem, eligibility, advertised terms, calls/referrals made, quoted fees, whether a lawyer was actually available, and where the process failed.

The difference between **program exists on a website** and **person actually obtains usable counsel** is exactly the kind of front-door/back-door distinction Blackball should measure.

See [washtenaw-county-access-to-counsel.md](washtenaw-county-access-to-counsel.md).

## Questions for prospective law students

Do not stop at median salary, bar passage, or employment rate. Ask:

- What work do graduates actually perform five and ten years later?
- Who are their clients?
- What percentage enters public-interest, government, plaintiff, defense, corporate, prosecutor, debt-collection, insurance, compliance, transactional, academic, and non-law work?
- How strongly are those destinations associated with debt and class rank?
- What did students say they wanted before law school, and what did they actually do afterward?
- How many would choose the same path again knowing the work they eventually performed?
- How often does legal education create access to justice, and how often does it primarily create people paid to manage, contest, delay, price, or threaten around legal claims?
- What does an ordinary person have to pay to obtain the legal expertise produced by the schools that describe law as public service?

The purpose is not to tell a prospective student never to become a lawyer. It is to show enough of the actual system that the student cannot plausibly say afterward: **nobody told me this was what the job was.**
