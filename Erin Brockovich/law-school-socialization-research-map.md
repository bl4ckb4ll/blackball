# Law-school socialization research map

The recurring stories about cutthroat competition, movement away from public-interest plans, declining well-being, and corporate recruiting should not remain a pile of anecdotes. Several distinct empirical literatures address different parts of the sequence.

## Robert Granfield — *Making Elite Lawyers*

Robert Granfield studied Harvard Law School through classroom observation, questionnaires, and in-depth student interviews conducted in the late 1980s. His 1992 book asks why many students who enter with liberal or public-interest commitments move toward corporate practice.

Publisher and catalog descriptions emphasize:

- a first-year legal consciousness valuing gamesmanship over ideals;
- competition and victory over right and wrong;
- alienation between moral belief and professional work;
- the “Harvard mystique” and corporate recruiting;
- differing effects by class, race, and gender.

This is stronger than folklore because it is an institutional ethnography. It is still one elite school in a particular period. Retrieve the book and build chapter- and page-level notes rather than relying indefinitely on sales copy or reviews.

Primary citation:

Robert Granfield, *Making Elite Lawyers: Visions of Law at Harvard and Beyond* (Routledge, 1992), ISBN 9780415904087.

## Sheldon and Krieger — motivation, values, and well-being

Kennon Sheldon and Lawrence Krieger followed law students and reported substantial declines in subjective well-being and intrinsic motivation during law school. Their 2004 article’s abstract reports increasing appearance values, decreasing community-service values, and an association between high grades and movement toward more lucrative, higher-stress careers and away from service-oriented careers. The patterns persisted in later years and were partly replicated in another school.

A 2007 longitudinal article reports declining psychological need satisfaction and well-being across three years at two schools, while greater perceived faculty autonomy support predicted less severe declines and better later outcomes.

These studies do not show that every student becomes selfish or that every law school has the same effect. They provide measured evidence that values and well-being can change during legal education and that institutional conditions matter.

## John Bliss — timing and identity work

John Bliss complicates the simple story that the first-year classroom directly converts idealists into corporate lawyers.

His five-year, multi-method study follows elite-school students who initially expressed public-interest preferences but moved toward large-firm work. Bliss argues that major shifts often occur during the second-year recruiting period. Law-firm interviews and career narratives help students reconstruct identities compatible with corporate work, including work they previously viewed negatively.

This matters because it locates the mechanism more precisely:

- the 1L curriculum may omit serious education about career paths and professional identity;
- grades, debt, prestige, and uncertainty prepare the ground;
- organized large-firm recruiting arrives with a legible, paid route;
- students perform narrative work that makes the route acceptable;
- later corporate-bound students may distance the professional role from personal and political identity.

Citations:

- John Bliss, “From Idealists to Hired Guns? An Empirical Analysis of ‘Public Interest Drift’ in Law School,” 51 *U.C. Davis Law Review* 1973 (2018): https://digitalcommons.du.edu/law_facpub/963/
- Harvard Law School Center on the Legal Profession project summary: https://clp.law.harvard.edu/research/research-projects/professional-identity-formation/

## Research design for Blackball

Keep the following stages separate:

1. values and occupational expectations before law school;
2. selection into schools;
3. 1L classroom and grading experience;
4. competition, collaboration, and sabotage;
5. debt accumulation and sunk-cost commitment;
6. career information actually supplied;
7. 2L recruiting and interview narratives;
8. job choice;
9. actual clients and work products;
10. later satisfaction, role distancing, exit, and regret.

The question is not simply whether law school changes people. It is **which institution, at which stage, through which incentive or representation, changes the feasible and psychologically acceptable work available to them**.
