# Before law school: what does a successful corporate lawyer do?

**For someone considering law school, roughly ages 19–27.** You should not have to learn the economic consequences of legal practice years after enrolling.

Law school can lead to public defense, legal aid, civil-rights work, representation of workers or landowners, or prosecution. But it can also lead to well-paid work helping companies, banks, insurers, or landlords enforce their interests against people with much less money.

The same word—**success**—can mean very different things depending on who hired the lawyer.

## An example: a gas company goes bankrupt

Suppose you work for a law firm representing an oil-and-gas company that cannot meet its obligations. The client wants a plan that lets the business or its remaining assets continue without paying every previous claim in full.

Your tasks could include drafting the plan and supporting documents; arguing which creditors have priority; negotiating with secured lenders; contesting a claim or an objection; and obtaining a court order that binds the parties. These are not movie courtroom speeches. They can be technical, time-consuming, highly paid tasks.

If you help the client achieve a legally valid plan under which certain creditors receive nothing, **your client may count your work as a success while those creditors experience a loss**. Court authority turns papers, classifications, and deadlines into enforceable consequences. You need not personally threaten a creditor or use physical force.

A real example: in the 2018 **Rex Energy** bankruptcy, the [confirmed liquidation plan](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1397516/000119312518304338/d622929dex22.htm) provided **no distribution** for *Class 4 general unsecured claims*, while providing different treatment for secured creditors. That is an actual court-approved outcome. It does **not** establish that small royalty owners were members of Class 4, or that every landowner was unpaid. The [asset-sale documents](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1397516/000119312518258199/d614840dex21.htm) identify a category of contested royalties, which needs a separate claim-by-claim investigation.

A second real example points the other way. The [2021 Gulfport Energy confirmation order](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/874499/000121390021023524/ea140017ex2-1_gulfportenergy.htm), paragraphs 64–69, requires continued payment of undisputed royalties and expressly preserves specified royalty claimants' rights. Legal representation can also be used to **resist** the company and retain claims. Whether those claimants ultimately collected is a separate question.

## Four questions you should ask before enrolling

1. **Who will hire me?** A corporation, an insurer, a creditor, a landlord, a worker, a tenant, an accused person, a government, a landowner?
2. **What will I be paid to accomplish?** Collect money, defend a claim, contest liability, negotiate a settlement, change creditor priority, preserve someone's property, or prevent someone's loss?
3. **Who experiences the consequences if I win?** A business owner, employees, lessors, small creditors, policyholders, tenants, injured people, or other parties?
4. **What are my actual choices?** What types of law jobs do graduates from *this school* obtain? Which clients do those employers represent? What are the debts, salaries, recruitment conditions, and constraints on choosing different clients?

The point is not to claim every lawyer works for wealthy clients, or that representing a company is automatically misconduct. It is to make the **real work and the distribution of its benefits and harms** visible before someone buys an expensive education.

### Why this disclosure comes first

**Do not require evidence that a particular applicant is ignorant before explaining the profession to them.** A young applicant does not need to know bankruptcy law to deserve a clear account of the ordinary duties and consequences of a high-paying bankruptcy practice. Surveys about what incoming students already know could inform further research, but they are **not prerequisites** for giving the explanation.

The characterization “a small cut” is about professional reward relative to the sums being fought over, not a claim that all lawyers receive a fixed percentage of money taken from creditors. In Chapter 11, fees can depend on arrangements and court-approved fee claims. Trace actual fees, clients, orders and payments instead of inventing percentages.
