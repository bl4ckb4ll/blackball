# Law-school competition and gamesmanship

This note separates two claims that should not be conflated:

1. **How common is deliberate sabotage?** — currently unknown without better prevalence data.
2. **Is sabotage / strategic deception a recurrent and recognizable part of law-school culture and folklore?** — yes, clearly enough to be repeatedly discussed by students, schools, admissions officials, and commercial law-school guidance.

The second claim matters even before the first is settled. A behavior can be culturally important because people anticipate it, warn against it, regulate against it, or define their own school in opposition to it.

## User-provided example

A September 2026 screenshot supplied to this project showed a law-school discussion in which students warned that classmates may lie about grades or study habits, described historical book-hiding, and recounted a story about a shared digital outline allegedly being altered before a final examination. Preserve this as first-person/community testimony, not as a measured prevalence estimate.

The important feature is not any single anecdote. It is the recurrence of the same recognizable forms:

- hiding or monopolizing scarce study materials;
- misleading classmates about preparation;
- withholding useful outlines;
- supplying defective or altered shared study material;
- treating rank on a curve as a reason to disadvantage classmates.

## Independent corroboration of cultural salience

The phrase **"hiding books"** appears repeatedly as the stock image of cutthroat law-school competition even in sources denying that their own school has such a culture.

Examples to retain:

- A Washington & Lee admissions interview says its honor system prevents behavior "associated with law school and law students generally" and specifically says students do not hide books or rip pages from books. This is a denial of local prevalence but evidence that the behavior is a well-known reference point.
- A Baylor / Princeton Review profile quotes students distinguishing strong competition from "hiding-books-in-the-library" competition.
- A Kaplan law-school competitiveness guide invokes the familiar image of students hiding books or making fake outlines, while arguing that most law schools are not actually that extreme.
- Indiana University's Maurer School of Law academic rules explicitly prohibit knowingly stealing, destroying, damaging, or hiding library materials and intentionally impeding another student's academic work.

This pattern is analytically useful. **The opposite account often still presupposes the trope.** "Our students are competitive but do not hide books" does not establish widespread book-hiding; it does establish that law schools expect the audience to understand what "hiding books" means as a form of competitive sabotage.

## Granfield

Robert Granfield's *Making Elite Lawyers: Visions of Law at Harvard and Beyond* is stronger evidence than folklore because it is an ethnographic study of elite legal education. The publisher's summary describes a first-year legal consciousness that comes to value gamesmanship over ideals and competition/victory over right/wrong, followed by a path toward elite corporate employment.

Do not use the publisher summary as a substitute for the book. Retrieve and inspect the relevant chapters and preserve page-specific notes.

## Measurement plan

Build a corpus rather than arguing from whichever anecdote is vividest:

- student handbooks and honor codes prohibiting sabotage;
- law-library policies concerning hiding, mutilating, or monopolizing materials;
- memoirs and first-person accounts;
- student surveys on competition and collaboration;
- grading-curve policies;
- anonymous outline-sharing practices;
- interviews with faculty, librarians, and students;
- evidence of actual disciplinary cases where available;
- contrary evidence from schools with explicitly cooperative cultures.

The target is not "prove law students are bad people." It is to measure whether the institutional design **creates incentives for peers to become obstacles to one another**, and how students respond to those incentives.
