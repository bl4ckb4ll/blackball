# Law-school competition and gamesmanship

The evidence already supports more than “a few colorful anecdotes.” The same behaviors recur in memoirs, student accounts, school warnings, honor codes, library rules, admissions denials, and commercial law-school guidance. That is evidence that competitive deception and sabotage are a persistent, recognizable feature of law-school culture. What remains unmeasured is the exact population rate, not whether the phenomenon exists.

Keep three claims separate:

1. **Specific incidents occurred.** — supported where a named source describes or documents a particular act.
2. **Strategic deception and sabotage are recurrent and culturally prevalent.** — strongly supported by repeated independent testimony, institutional rules, warnings, and even school-specific denials.
3. **A known percentage of students or schools engage in each behavior.** — not yet supported without a suitable survey or administrative dataset.

Blackball has evidence for the first two. It does not yet have the third. Do not let the absence of a population estimate erase the existence or recurrence of the conduct.

The contrary account is rarely “this never happens and the trope was invented.” It is usually local and qualified: “not at our school,” “our students collaborate,” or “we are competitive without hiding books.” Those accounts may show meaningful variation among schools, but they also demonstrate that the audience is expected to recognize the underlying pattern.

## User-provided example

A September 2026 screenshot supplied to this project showed a discussion of law-school culture in which students warned that classmates may lie about grades or study habits, described historical book-hiding, and recounted a story about a shared digital outline allegedly being altered before a final examination. Preserve this as first-person/community testimony. It contributes to the recurring-pattern evidence even though it does not supply a denominator.

The important feature is not any single anecdote. It is the repetition of the same recognizable forms:

- hiding or monopolizing scarce study materials;
- misleading classmates about preparation;
- falsely claiming not to study while preparing privately;
- withholding useful outlines;
- supplying defective or altered shared study material;
- damaging, removing, or deliberately misshelving library material;
- treating rank on a curve as a reason to disadvantage classmates.

The user’s Arnold Schwarzenegger/Lou Ferrigno analogy captures one form of strategic misrepresentation: publicly claim that one will relax or party while privately continuing to train, so a competitor lowers effort. Whether any particular story is true must still be checked, but the strategy itself is widely legible because rank makes classmates’ performance affect one another.

## Independent corroboration of prevalence and cultural recognition

The phrase **“hiding books”** appears repeatedly as the stock image of cutthroat law-school competition, including in sources denying that their own school has such a culture.

Examples to retain:

- A Washington & Lee admissions interview says its honor system prevents behavior “associated with law school and law students generally” and specifically says students do not hide books or rip pages from books. This is a denial of local prevalence and simultaneous evidence that the behavior is a well-known reference point.
- A Baylor / Princeton Review profile quotes students distinguishing strong competition from “hiding-books-in-the-library” competition.
- A Kaplan law-school competitiveness guide invokes the familiar image of students hiding books or making fake outlines while arguing that most schools are not always that extreme.
- Indiana University’s Maurer School of Law academic rules explicitly prohibit knowingly stealing, destroying, damaging, or hiding library materials and intentionally impeding another student’s academic work.
- Law-school memoirs and guides repeatedly warn about withholding outlines, misleading peers about preparation, and manipulating access to shared materials.

Formal prohibitions do not prove a high violation rate. But institutions do not independently converge on detailed prohibitions and warnings for a wholly unintelligible behavior. The rules, stories, denials, and advice together establish that competitive obstruction is persistent enough to organize expectations and institutional responses.

## What “prevalent” should mean here

Do not use *prevalent* as a fake numerical claim. Use it to mean that the phenomenon is widespread enough across time, institutions, and source types to be a recurring part of how law-school competition is described and anticipated.

A precise future estimate would require a denominator and careful definitions. It should distinguish:

- direct physical sabotage;
- deliberate misinformation;
- refusal to share privately produced work;
- ordinary competitive secrecy;
- rumors or folklore;
- conduct witnessed directly versus heard secondhand;
- isolated actors versus school-wide climate.

The absence of that estimate does not require Blackball to retreat to “maybe one person once hid a book.” The current evidence supports a recurrent cultural and behavioral pattern while leaving its exact rate open.

## Granfield

Robert Granfield’s *Making Elite Lawyers: Visions of Law at Harvard and Beyond* is stronger evidence than folklore because it is an ethnographic study of elite legal education. The publisher’s summary describes a first-year legal consciousness that comes to value gamesmanship over ideals and competition/victory over right/wrong, followed by a path toward elite corporate employment.

Do not use the publisher summary as a substitute for the book. Retrieve and inspect the relevant chapters and preserve page-specific notes.

Granfield’s larger importance is that petty sabotage is not the main issue. The institutional structure can reward the deeper habit of treating every situation as a contest to be won, separating professional argument from ordinary judgments of right and wrong, and directing successful students toward clients able to pay for that skill.

## Measurement plan

Build a corpus rather than arguing from whichever anecdote is vividest:

- student handbooks and honor codes prohibiting sabotage;
- law-library policies concerning hiding, mutilating, or monopolizing materials;
- memoirs and first-person accounts;
- student surveys on competition and collaboration;
- grading-curve policies;
- anonymous outline-sharing practices;
- interviews with faculty, librarians, and students;
- actual disciplinary cases where available;
- contrary evidence from schools with explicitly cooperative cultures;
- longitudinal evidence on whether competitive conduct predicts later practice choices or professional role morality.

The target is not merely “prove law students are bad people.” It is to measure whether the institutional design **creates incentives for peers to become obstacles to one another**, whether students respond through deception or obstruction, and whether the same victory-over-right-and-wrong orientation later appears in paid legal work.
