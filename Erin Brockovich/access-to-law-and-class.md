# Access to law and class

This note asks a basic question that can disappear when legal discussion stays at the level of doctrine:

**What is a legal right worth to a person who cannot afford to invoke it effectively?**

Blackball should keep the distance visible between high legal theory and ordinary legal use.

## Two different legal worlds

One world contains:

- constitutional theory;
- appellate doctrine;
- law-review articles;
- Supreme Court amicus briefs;
- podcasts and public intellectual argument;
- carefully developed positions about institutional design, separation of powers, rights, and jurisprudence.

The other contains:

- an eviction notice;
- a disability denial;
- a debt-collection complaint;
- a custody dispute;
- an expungement application;
- a probate form;
- a workplace injury;
- a benefits appeal;
- a landlord, employer, insurer, creditor, prosecutor, or government agency represented by repeat players;
- a person who does not know which form to file, what deadline matters, what facts are legally relevant, or what language will be taken seriously.

The first world can be genuinely valuable. It does not answer whether the second world can use the law.

## Representation is itself a class resource

Measure legal access as an economic good:

- hourly rates and retainers;
- contingency-fee availability and exclusions;
- legal-aid income limits;
- modest-means eligibility limits;
- geographic lawyer availability;
- representation rates by case type;
- default judgments and unrepresented parties;
- time required to navigate forms and hearings;
- court fees, expert fees, transcripts, service fees, transportation, missed work, and child care;
- whether the opposing party is a repeat institutional litigant;
- whether a nominal referral actually produces a lawyer willing and able to take the matter.

A constitutional or statutory entitlement can coexist with practical inability to enforce it.

## Professional language and asymmetric legibility

Legal language often carries consequences that are clearer to insiders than outsiders. Blackball should collect examples where lawyers themselves describe communications as deliberately calibrated threats: language that signals "comply or we escalate" without stating the threat in ordinary speech.

Likewise, legal insiders may say that a regulator has used unusually strong language while the public sees only restrained bureaucratic prose. This is not necessarily deception; professional communities develop conventions. But those conventions create an **accessibility asymmetry** when the person whose rights or money are at stake cannot read the signal.

The same problem appears in court. A self-represented person may believe that a morally powerful speech about justice is the important thing, while the court may care about jurisdiction, pleadings, evidentiary foundations, preservation, deadlines, burdens, and requested relief. The person's moral intuition can be sound while their procedural performance fails.

## The prospective-lawyer question

For someone considering law school, ask not only whether legal work is prestigious or remunerative but what role the eventual job occupies in these class relations.

Possible outcomes include:

- helping ordinary people access legal remedies;
- helping corporations, insurers, creditors, landlords, employers, or governments manage claims against them;
- prosecuting;
- defending accused people;
- collecting debts;
- negotiating transactions;
- regulatory work;
- academic work largely removed from ordinary representation;
- non-law work after an expensive legal education.

A young person attracted by the idea of "justice" should see the distribution before borrowing heavily to enter the profession.

Tamanaha is an important lead because *Failing Law Schools* directly addresses the economics of legal education: tuition, debt, employment outcomes, institutional incentives, and the mismatch between what schools cost and what many graduates can earn. Do not attribute a narrower access-to-justice claim to Tamanaha without pinning the exact text. Search his broader work separately for discussions of access, ordinary legal needs, and the gap between elite legal education and routine practice.

## Blackball test

For every grand claim about the rule of law, rights, constitutional principle, or legal professionalism, add a mundane companion question:

**How does a low- or middle-income person actually obtain the lawyer, time, forms, expertise, and money needed to make this principle operational?**

That is not an argument against jurisprudence. It is a demand that jurisprudence remain answerable to class and implementation.
