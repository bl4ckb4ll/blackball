# Where would I read *Shakespeare, Einstein, and the Bottom Line*, and who with?

Back to the book note: [David L. Kirp — *Shakespeare, Einstein, and the Bottom Line*](../David%20L.%20Kirp%20%E2%80%93%20Shakespeare,%20Einstein,%20and%20the%20Bottom%20Line%20%E2%80%93%20$6%20paper%20%E2%80%93%20JSTOR%20(not%20open%20access).md)

This file records **course provenance**, not merely citations to the book. Strong evidence means a syllabus actually places the book or chapter in the course readings. Supplemental evidence is kept, but labeled as such rather than silently promoted to “assigned.”

## Cornell University — Ronald G. Ehrenberg — *Economic Analysis of the University*

**School:** Cornell University, ILR School  
**Course:** ILR 3480 / ILR 6480 / ECON 3410, *Economic Analysis of the University*  
**Instructor:** Ronald G. Ehrenberg  
**Term:** Fall 2017  
**Status:** **Primary course reading**  
**Syllabus:** https://courses.cit.cornell.edu/ilrle648/ILR%20648F2017ReadingList.pdf

This is the cleanest provenance found in the first pass. Ehrenberg lists only two books under “Primary Course Readings”:

1. his own *Tuition Rising: Why College Costs So Much*;
2. David L. Kirp, *Shakespeare, Einstein, and the Bottom Line*.

Kirp is then assigned throughout the course rather than appearing as a decorative bibliography entry. The syllabus includes, among other assignments:

- chapters 1, 6, and 7 for the introduction to the American higher-education system, prestige, governance, and budgetary issues;
- chapters 2, 3, and 5 for the unit on prestige and university behavior;
- chapter 4 for faculty compensation and employment;
- further chapters elsewhere in the semester as the course moves through university economics and administration.

This pairing is particularly useful because Ehrenberg was also a serious reviewer of Kirp's book in the *Journal of Economic Literature*. He did not merely praise it in print; he later made it a primary text in his own economics-of-the-university course.

## New York University — Joan Malczewski — *How Colleges Work*

**School:** NYU Steinhardt  
**Course:** E98.2011, *How Colleges Work*  
**Instructor:** Joan Malczewski  
**Term:** Spring 2009  
**Status:** **Required course material, with chapters assigned repeatedly**  
**Surviving syllabus copy:** https://studylib.net/doc/7738176/exxxxxx---nyu-steinhardt

The syllabus describes a course on the organizational dynamics of colleges and universities: finance, enrollment management, academic affairs, student services, governance, management, and strategic planning. Kirp is included in the course materials, and specific chapters recur in the schedule:

- chapters 4 and 9 — faculty rights and responsibilities;
- chapters 6 and 7 — financial management;
- chapter 10 — curriculum and retention;
- chapter 1 — strategic planning;
- chapters 2 and 3 — strategic planning, missions, and markets.

The neighboring readings are revealing too: Robert Birnbaum on how colleges and academic leadership work, Margaret Barr on budgets and financial management, Mitchell Stevens on admissions, Roger L. Geiger on politics/markets/university costs, Burton Clark on academic organizations, Karl Weick on loosely coupled systems, and others. In an actual higher-education-organization course, Kirp is therefore being used as connective institutional anatomy rather than as a free-standing polemic.

## UCLA — Jean-François Blanchette — *Academic Work*

**School:** University of California, Los Angeles, Department of Information Studies  
**Course:** INF STD 298-C, *Academic Work*  
**Instructor:** Jean-François Blanchette  
**Term:** Spring 2016  
**Status:** **Additional reading; not one of the required textbooks**  
**Syllabus:** https://pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/blanchette/291c/syllabus.pdf

The syllabus is about the institutional structures surrounding scholarship: tenure, faculty governance, peer review, scholarly communication, public funding, doctoral training, contingent faculty, knowledge economies, and the organization of academic labor.

Kirp's chapter 9, **“The Market in Ideas: Columbia University and MIT,”** appears in the additional readings. The placement is important even though the syllabus does not list Kirp among the required textbooks: the Fathom / MIT OpenCourseWare case is being put alongside readings on education, academic institutions, and the organization of scholarly work.

Blackball should preserve the distinction: this is genuine course provenance for a chapter, but the surviving syllabus labels the relevant section “Additional Readings,” so it should not be represented as a required purchase.

## Florida Atlantic University — *Higher Education Business and Finance*

**School:** Florida Atlantic University, College of Education, Department of Educational Leadership  
**Course:** EDH 7505, *Higher Education Business and Finance* (renumbered from EDA 6244)  
**Document context:** syllabus attached to a 2011 graduate-program course-change request  
**Instructor:** TBA in the surviving syllabus  
**Status:** **Recommended / supplemental / optional text**  
**Syllabus packet:** https://www.fau.edu/graduate/documents/graduate/faculty-and-staff/programs-committee/docs/032311/EDA_6244-Higher_Education_Business_and_Finance.pdf

The course covers financing, cost, price, expenditures, revenue, budgeting, and financial-management policy in postsecondary education. Kirp appears as item 4 under **“Recommended/Supplemental Texts (Optional)”**, near Derek Bok's *Universities in the Marketplace* and other higher-education finance texts.

Again, this belongs in the provenance graph, but not in the same evidentiary bucket as Cornell or NYU. The surviving document proves that the book was recommended for the course, not that every student was required to read or buy it.

## Provenance ranking from this pass

1. **Cornell / Ronald G. Ehrenberg** — strongest: whole book is one of two primary course texts, with chapter-level assignments.
2. **NYU Steinhardt / Joan Malczewski** — strong: book in course materials and chapters explicitly assigned across multiple weeks.
3. **UCLA / Jean-François Blanchette** — useful but weaker: chapter 9 in additional readings, not required-text list.
4. **Florida Atlantic / EDH 7505** — useful but weaker: book is explicitly optional supplemental reading; professor is TBA in the surviving document.

These are enough to establish that Kirp was not merely reviewed by higher-education scholars: it entered the teaching literature of higher-education economics, administration, organization, and academic-work courses.
