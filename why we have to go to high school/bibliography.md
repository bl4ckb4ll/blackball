# Bibliography

This bibliography is deliberately redundant. The point is not merely to show that sources exist, but to preserve **which source supports which claim** so later Blackball work can distinguish primary evidence, statistical series, and historical interpretation.

## Primary and official statistical sources

### U.S. Bureau of Education. *Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education*. 1918.

**Full citation:**

Department of the Interior, Bureau of Education. *Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education: A Report of the Commission on the Reorganization of Secondary Education, Appointed by the National Education Association*. Bulletin, 1918, No. 35. Washington, D.C.: Government Printing Office, 1918.

ERIC record and downloadable full text:  
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED541063

**Used here for:**

- the seven objectives of secondary education;
- evidence that educational leaders were reconceiving secondary education for a mass population;
- the language and institutional purpose of the comprehensive secondary school;
- the proposition that the 1918 report codified an already developing mass-secondary-school movement rather than creating the American high school from nothing.

**Important chronology:** Boston's public high school dated to 1821, and the high-school movement was already underway before 1918. Do not describe the report as the event that "opened" American high school.

### National Center for Education Statistics. Digest table 219.10.

**Title:** "High school graduates, by sex and control of school; public high school averaged freshman graduation rate (AFGR); public high school 4-year adjusted cohort graduation rate (ACGR); and total graduates as a ratio of 17-year-old population: Selected school years, 1869-70 through 2031-32."

https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_219.10.asp

**Key historical values used:**

- 1869–70: 16,000 graduates; ratio to age-17 population 2.0%.
- 1879–80: 23,634; 2.5%.
- 1889–90: 43,731; 3.5%.
- 1899–1900: 94,883; 6.4%.
- 1909–10: 156,429; 8.8%.
- 1919–20: 311,266; 16.8%.
- 1929–30: 666,904; 29.0%.
- 1939–40: 1,221,475; **50.8%**.
- 1949–50: 1,199,700; 59.0%.
- 1959–60: 1,858,023; 69.5%.
- 1969–70: 2,888,639; 76.9%.

**Critical methodological note:** NCES says the graduate/17-year-old ratio is not a cohort graduation rate. It divides graduates of all ages in a year by the 17-year-old population in that year. It is useful as a long historical comparison series but should be named accurately.

**Underlying historical source families named by NCES include:**

- *Annual Report of the Commissioner of Education*, 1870–1910;
- *Statistics of Public High Schools*, 1889–90 through 1929–30;
- *Biennial Survey of Education in the United States*, 1919–20 through 1949–50;
- later NCES administrative and survey series.

### National Center for Education Statistics. Digest table 104.20.

**Title:** "Percentage of persons 25 to 29 years old with selected levels of educational attainment, by race/ethnicity and sex: Selected years, 1920 through 2023."

https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_104.20.asp

**Key values used, high-school completion or higher, age 25–29:**

- 1940: 38.1% total; 41.2% white; 12.3% Black.
- 1950: **52.8% total**; 56.3% white; 23.6% Black.
- 1960: 60.7% total; 63.7% white; 38.6% Black.
- 1970: 75.4% total; 77.8% white; 58.4% Black.

**Used here for:** the young-adult attainment threshold. This is the source for saying that a majority of 25–29-year-olds had completed high school by 1950.

**Caution:** historical racial categories and measurement conventions change over time. Use the table's categories as historical statistical categories, not as timeless classifications.

## Core secondary scholarship: the high school movement

### Goldin, Claudia. "How America Graduated from High School: 1910 to 1960." 1994.

NBER Working Paper 4762, June 1994.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w4762  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3386/w4762

Published, with changes, as:

Goldin, Claudia. "America's Graduation from High School: The Evolution and Spread of Secondary Schooling in the Twentieth Century." *Journal of Economic History* 58, no. 2 (June 1998): 345–374.

**Used here for:**

- timing of the mass expansion of secondary schooling;
- especially rapid non-South expansion from about 1920 to 1935;
- the role of the Great Depression in raising attendance and graduation;
- regional differences;
- the argument that high-school expansion was central to twentieth-century U.S. human-capital growth.

Goldin's article is one of the most important single sources for this folder.

---

### Goldin, Claudia. Appendix to "How America Graduated from High School, 1910 to 1960": Construction of State-Level Secondary School Data. 1994.

NBER Historical Working Paper 57.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/h0057  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3386/h0057

**Used here for:** understanding how the state-level historical enrollment and graduation series were constructed.

The abstract also gives a useful intuitive statement: an 18-year-old male in 1910 had roughly a 10 percent chance of holding a high-school diploma, while by the mid-1930s the median 18-year-old male was a high-school graduate.

---

### Goldin, Claudia, and Lawrence F. Katz. "Why the United States Led in Education: Lessons from Secondary School Expansion, 1910 to 1940." 1997.

NBER Working Paper 6144, August 1997.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w6144  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3386/w6144

**Key summary:** the share of U.S. youths with high-school diplomas rose from about 9 percent in 1910 to more than 50 percent by 1940.

**Used here for:**

- framing the high-school movement as an unusually early American mass-secondary-school transition;
- explaining regional variation;
- the importance of income/wealth, community stability, and social composition in local adoption;
- comparison with later secondary-school expansion in other countries.

---

### Goldin, Claudia, and Lawrence F. Katz. "Human Capital and Social Capital: The Rise of Secondary Schooling in America, 1910 to 1940." 1998.

NBER Working Paper 6439, March 1998.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w6439  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3386/w6439

Published as:

Goldin, Claudia, and Lawrence F. Katz. "Human Capital and Social Capital: The Rise of Secondary Schooling in America, 1910 to 1940." *Journal of Interdisciplinary History* 29 (Spring 1999): 683–723.

**Used here for:**

- the phrase and concept of the "high school movement";
- the importance of small towns and the Midwestern/Great Plains "education belt";
- local wealth, community stability, and social organization;
- the estimated high private return to secondary schooling around the beginning of the movement.

### Goldin, Claudia, and Lawrence F. Katz. "Mass Secondary Schooling and the State." 2003.

NBER Working Paper 10075, November 2003.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w10075  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3386/w10075

Later book-chapter version:

Goldin, Claudia, and Lawrence F. Katz. "Mass Secondary Schooling and the State: The Role of State Compulsion in the High School Movement." In *Understanding Long-Run Economic Growth: Geography, Institutions, and the Knowledge Economy*, edited by Dora L. Costa and Naomi R. Lamoreaux. University of Chicago Press.  
https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/understanding-long-run-economic-growth-geography-institutions-and-knowledge-economy/mass-secondary-schooling-and-state-role-state-compulsion-high-school-movement

**This is the key source for two claims in this folder:**

1. From 1910 to 1940, U.S. secondary enrollment rose from about **18 to 71 percent**, while graduation rose from about **9 to 51 percent**.
2. Changes in compulsory-schooling and child-labor laws can account for **at most about 5 percent** of the increase in high-school enrollment and about the same portion of the increase in eventual attainment for affected cohorts.

This source is essential because it prevents the history from becoming a simple compulsory-schooling story.

NBER Working Paper 7217, July 1999.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w7217  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3386/w7217

Published as:

Goldin, Claudia, and Lawrence F. Katz. "Education and Income in the Early Twentieth Century: Evidence from the Prairies." *Journal of Economic History* 60, no. 3 (September 2000): 782–818.

### Goldin, Claudia, and Lawrence F. Katz. "The Decline of Non-Competing Groups: Changes in the Premium to Education, 1890 to 1940." 1995.

NBER Working Paper 5202.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w5202  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3386/w5202

**Potential use for the next stage of Blackball research:**

This paper traces changes in the wage premium associated with high-school education. It is especially relevant to the question of how a credential's labor-market value changes when the supply of credentialed workers expands quickly.

The paper reports rapid increases in the relative supply of high-school graduates in the early twentieth century and a substantial decline in educational wage differentials before the 1930s.

## Nineteenth-century institutional history

### Reese, William J. *The Origins of the American High School*. 1995.

New Haven: Yale University Press, 1995. Paperback edition 1999.

Publisher page:  
https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300079432/the-origins-of-the-american-high-school/

**Used here for:**

- the nineteenth-century origins of the public high school;
- Boston's 1821 English Classical School / English High School;
- debates over whether high schools were elite or broadly useful institutions;
- the relationship between public high schools and private academies;
- the spread of high schools by the late nineteenth century;
- the point that high schools could become the dominant *type of secondary school* before secondary education itself became a majority experience.

This is the main book-length source for the pre-1910 portion of the story.

## Sources worth adding in a later research pass

These are not necessary to establish the 50-percent dates, but they would deepen the answer to the title question.

### Compulsory-schooling law datasets

The Goldin/Katz papers themselves contain state-law coding and references. A later Blackball pass should preserve the underlying state-by-state compulsory-attendance and child-labor series so legal minimum school-leaving ages can be compared directly with enrollment and graduation.

### Historical job advertisements

To demonstrate when "high school diploma required" became normal in employment, we need evidence from the demand side rather than inferring employer requirements from attainment statistics.

Potential corpora:

- Chronicling America / Library of Congress newspaper advertisements;
- ProQuest historical newspapers where accessible;
- Google Books and HathiTrust trade journals;
- historical civil-service announcements;
- company employment manuals and personnel journals;
- classified-ad datasets by city and occupation.

### Occupational education distributions

IPUMS historical Census microdata can potentially be used to estimate, by occupation and cohort, when workers without high-school completion disappeared from particular jobs. This would turn "the diploma became required" into an empirical occupational series rather than a generalization.

### Race and segregation

The NCES aggregate tables show huge differences in attainment, but they do not explain them. A fuller history needs sources on segregated schooling, school term length, school expenditures, access to secondary schools, Rosenwald schools, migration, and the regional political economy of Black education.

### Sex differences

NCES's historical graduation counts show that female graduates outnumbered male graduates in many early years. A later note should examine whether the high-school movement had different timing and labor-market meanings for women and men.

## Citation discipline for this folder

Use the source closest to the claim:

- **1821 / nineteenth-century institutional origins:** Reese plus primary Boston records.
- **1874 tax-supported high school:** *Stuart* in *Michigan Reports*.
- **1918 educational philosophy:** *Cardinal Principles* primary source.
- **1910–1940 enrollment/graduation transformation:** Goldin/Katz.
- **historical annual graduate ratios:** NCES 219.10.
- **young-adult attainment:** NCES 104.20.
- **all-adult attainment:** NCES 104.10.
- **causal effect of compulsory-schooling/child-labor laws:** Goldin/Katz 2003, not a generic history page.
- **labor-market returns:** Goldin/Katz Iowa and wage-premium papers.

Do not use a modern general-interest history page to support a quantitative claim when the underlying NCES/NBER/primary source is available.
