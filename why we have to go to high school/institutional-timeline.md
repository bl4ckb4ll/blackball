# Institutional timeline: from optional secondary school to normal life stage

This timeline separates three things that are often conflated:

1. the existence of public high schools;
2. the emergence of mass attendance and graduation;
3. explanations for why that transition happened.

The first two can be dated with reasonable confidence. The third remains a causal research question.

## Before the public high school

For much of the early United States, publicly supported common schools supplied elementary education while secondary education was less standardized. Latin grammar schools, private academies, seminaries, and college-preparatory institutions served limited populations. There was no national expectation that every adolescent would remain in publicly funded school through something equivalent to grade 12.

William J. Reese, *The Origins of the American High School*.  
https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300079432/the-origins-of-the-american-high-school/

## 1821 — Boston English Classical School

Boston established the English Classical School in 1821; it soon became the English High School. Reese treats it as the first public high school in the United States.

Its importance is partly that the school was not simply a classical college-preparatory institution. The founding rationale included advanced practical education for young people who would not necessarily follow the traditional collegiate route.

Sources:

- Reese, *The Origins of the American High School*.
- *Records Relating to the Early History of Boston*, Library of Congress scan:  
  https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/public/gdcmassbookdig/recordsrelatingt23bosto/recordsrelatingt23bosto_bw.pdf

## 1820s–1880s — high schools spread, but secondary schooling remains uncommon

Public high schools spread gradually during the nineteenth century. Reese notes that by the 1880s a majority of secondary students in the North attended high schools rather than academies.

That does **not** mean most adolescents attended secondary school.

NCES historical graduation ratios show the scale:

- 1869–70: graduates equal about 2.0 percent of the 17-year-old population;
- 1889–90: 3.5 percent;
- 1899–1900: 6.4 percent;
- 1909–10: 8.8 percent.

NCES Digest table 219.10:  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_219.10.asp

The high school became an established institutional form long before it became a majority experience.

## 1874 — the Kalamazoo School Case

**Stuart v. School District No. 1 of the Village of Kalamazoo, 30 Mich. 69 (1874)** concerned whether the Kalamazoo school district could levy taxes to support its high school.

The Michigan Supreme Court upheld the district. The decision is useful evidence that tax-supported secondary education was a contested political and legal extension of the common-school system rather than an automatic assumption.

- *Michigan Reports*, vol. 30:

## 1890–1910 — the institution exists; mass participation has not arrived

By 1910 the United States had thousands of public high schools, but graduation remained uncommon. The NCES graduate-to-age-17 ratio was only 8.8 percent in 1909–10.

Goldin and Katz estimate secondary-school enrollment at about 18 percent of U.S. youths in 1910 and graduation at about 9 percent.

Sources:

- Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz, "Why the United States Led in Education: Lessons from Secondary School Expansion, 1910 to 1940."  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w6144
- Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz, "Mass Secondary Schooling and the State."  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w10075

## 1910–1940 — the high school movement

This is the decisive descriptive transition.

Goldin and Katz summarize it as:

- **1910:** about 18 percent enrolled; about 9 percent graduated;
- **1940:** about 71 percent enrolled; about 51 percent graduated.

The expansion was geographically uneven. Their work emphasizes earlier adoption in the Far West, Great Plains, and parts of New England, with the South lagging. Small towns in the Midwest and Great Plains were important early sites of mass secondary schooling.

Goldin's state-level work places especially rapid expansion outside the South roughly between 1920 and 1935 and argues that the Depression further increased attendance and graduation.

Sources:

- Claudia Goldin, "How America Graduated from High School: 1910 to 1960."  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w4762
- Claudia Goldin, "America's Graduation from High School: The Evolution and Spread of Secondary Schooling in the Twentieth Century," *Journal of Economic History* 58, no. 2 (1998): 345–374.
- Goldin and Katz, "Human Capital and Social Capital: The Rise of Secondary Schooling in America, 1910 to 1940."  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w6439

These sources are important both for data and interpretation, but their causal interpretation should be kept distinct from the historical series themselves.

## 1918 — *Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education*

In 1918 the National Education Association's Commission on the Reorganization of Secondary Education issued *Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education*, published by the U.S. Bureau of Education as Bulletin No. 35.

Its seven objectives were:

1. health;
2. command of fundamental processes;
3. worthy home membership;
4. vocation;
5. citizenship;
6. worthy use of leisure;
7. ethical character.

The report is important evidence that educational policymakers were articulating a broad social purpose for secondary education and treating it as relevant to the whole youth population.

It did **not** create the public high school. Public high schools had existed for nearly a century, and mass expansion was already underway.

But chronology alone does not tell us whether the report merely codified the transition, accelerated it, helped coordinate it, or had little independent effect. Those are separate causal questions.

Primary source:

- U.S. Bureau of Education, *Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education*, Bulletin 1918, No. 35:  
  https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED541063

## 1910s–1930s — compulsory education and child-labor rules tighten

The same decades saw states strengthen compulsory-attendance and child-labor laws. That temporal overlap makes legal compulsion an obvious candidate explanation for rising school attendance.

Goldin and Katz attempt to estimate its contribution using cross-state variation in the laws. They conclude that measured changes in compulsory-schooling and child-labor laws from 1910 to 1939 can account for **at most about 5 percent** of the increase in high-school enrollment, with a similar share of eventual attainment for the affected cohorts.

Source:

- Goldin and Katz, "Mass Secondary Schooling and the State":  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w10075

### Status of that claim in this project

Treat the 5-percent result as **Goldin and Katz's estimate**, not as the settled answer.

A proper audit should ask:

- how their state legal variables are coded;
- whether statutes are good measures of actual enforcement;
- how exemptions are handled;
- whether legal changes are endogenous to rising demand for schooling;
- whether laws interact with school availability or youth labor demand;
- whether indirect effects are omitted by the empirical design;
- whether other historical studies obtain larger or smaller effects.

The existence of a low estimated direct effect in one design does not justify replacing the empirical question with the sentence "compulsory schooling did not matter much."

## 1915 — estimated private returns to high school

Goldin and Katz use the Iowa State Census of 1915 to estimate returns to schooling. Their reported return to a year of high school is roughly 11 percent for males overall and above 12 percent for young males.

Source:

- Goldin and Katz, "Education and Income in the Early 20th Century: Evidence from the Prairies":  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w7217

These estimates are relevant to family incentives, but they are not by themselves a causal explanation of institutional expansion. Selection into schooling, occupational sorting, expectations, credential supply, and labor demand all need separate treatment.

## 1929–1940 — Depression-era acceleration

The NCES historical graduate ratio rises sharply during the 1930s:

- 1929–30: 29.0 percent;
- 1939–40: 50.8 percent.

Goldin interprets the Depression as increasing enrollment and graduation partly by reducing teenage employment opportunities and therefore the opportunity cost of school.

That interpretation should be tested against other evidence rather than treated as self-validating.

Sources:

- NCES table 219.10:  
  https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_219.10.asp
- Goldin, "How America Graduated from High School":  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w4762

## 1939–40 — current graduation crosses the majority line

NCES reports 1,221,475 high-school graduates and an age-17 population of 2,403,074, a ratio of **50.8 percent**.

Goldin and Katz summarize the 1940 graduation fraction as **51 percent**.

This is the cleanest date for saying that graduating from high school had become a majority outcome for the current youth generation.

## 1940 — young-adult attainment still below one half

Among people age 25–29, only **38.1 percent** had high-school completion or higher in 1940.

Among all people age 25+, the figure was **24.5 percent**.

Sources:

- NCES table 104.20:  
  https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_104.20.asp
- NCES table 104.10:  
  https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d15/tables/dt15_104.10.asp

## 1950 — majority of young adults have completed high school

By 1950, **52.8 percent of people age 25–29** had completed high school or more.

The aggregate hides large racial differences. NCES reports 56.3 percent for white 25–29-year-olds and 23.6 percent for Black 25–29-year-olds under the historical categories used in the source.

Source:

- NCES table 104.20:  
  https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_104.20.asp

## 1960 — most adults still did not have the credential

Even in 1960, only **41.1 percent of all adults age 25+** had completed high school or higher.

This shows how long cohort replacement takes. A credential can become normal among new entrants before it becomes normal among the entire workforce.

## 1970 — majority of the whole adult population

By 1970, **55.2 percent of all people age 25+** had high-school completion or higher.

Source:

- NCES table 104.10:  
  https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d15/tables/dt15_104.10.asp

## What this timeline establishes

The evidence here establishes a broad chronology:

- public high schools existed in the nineteenth century;
- public financing of secondary education was contested and legally established over time;
- mass secondary-school participation arrived mainly in the early twentieth century;
- current graduation crossed the majority line around 1940;
- majority young-adult attainment followed by 1950;
- majority attainment among all adults followed by 1970.

## What it does not establish

It does **not** yet tell us the relative causal weight of:

- compulsory-attendance laws;
- child-labor restrictions;
- enforcement;
- local tax capacity;
- school construction and consolidation;
- changes in curriculum;
- changes in teenage labor demand;
- parental demand;
- employer credential preferences;
- technological or occupational change;
- political organization;
- regional racial regimes;
- migration;
- the Depression;
- interactions among those mechanisms.

It also does not establish exactly when employers began writing "high school diploma required" into ordinary job requirements.

That should become a separate evidence track using historical classified advertisements, civil-service rules, company personnel manuals, apprenticeship rules, union records, military requirements, occupational microdata, and employer surveys.

The goal is not to pick a favored story first. It is to explain how an institution that served a small minority became something most Americans were expected to complete.
