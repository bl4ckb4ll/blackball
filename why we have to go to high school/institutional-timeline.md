# Institutional timeline: from optional secondary school to normal life stage

This timeline separates the existence of high schools from the later emergence of **mass high-school attendance and completion**.

## Before the public high school

For much of the early United States, publicly supported common schools supplied elementary education, while secondary education was much less standardized. Latin grammar schools, private academies, seminaries, and college-preparatory institutions served limited populations. There was no national assumption that every adolescent would remain in a publicly funded school through something equivalent to grade 12.

The important historical distinction is therefore not "school versus no school." Elementary schooling was already much more widespread. The nineteenth-century controversy was partly over whether **public responsibility should extend beyond elementary education**.

For the nineteenth-century development, see William J. Reese, *The Origins of the American High School* (Yale University Press, 1995; paperback edition 1999).  
https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300079432/the-origins-of-the-american-high-school/

## 1821 — Boston English Classical School

Boston established the English Classical School in 1821; it soon became the English High School. Reese treats it as the first public high school in the United States.

The school is useful because its original purpose was not simply to imitate a classical college-preparatory academy. The founding argument emphasized advanced practical education for boys who would not necessarily pursue the conventional classical route to college. The institution therefore illustrates an early idea that publicly supported secondary education could serve commercial, mechanical, and civic life as well as college preparation.

A contemporary historical source reproducing the 1820–21 founding material is available through Boston's early city records and later centennial histories. A modern summary appears in Reese.

Useful sources:

- William J. Reese, *The Origins of the American High School*.

## 1820s–1880s — high schools spread, but secondary schooling remains a minority experience

Public high schools spread gradually through cities and towns during the nineteenth century. Reese's account emphasizes that by the 1880s a majority of *secondary students in the North* attended high schools rather than academies.

That should not be confused with saying that most adolescents attended secondary school. NCES historical graduation data make the distinction obvious:

- 1869–70: graduates equal about 2.0 percent of the 17-year-old population.
- 1889–90: 3.5 percent.
- 1899–1900: 6.4 percent.
- 1909–10: 8.8 percent.

Source: NCES Digest table 219.10.  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_219.10.asp

The high school had become an established institutional form before it had become the expected destination of most teenagers.

## 1874 — the Kalamazoo School Case

**Stuart v. School District No. 1 of the Village of Kalamazoo, 30 Mich. 69 (1874)** concerned whether the Kalamazoo school district could levy taxes to support its high school.

The complainants were not arguing that education itself was illegitimate. The dispute concerned the boundary of publicly financed education: did the public-school taxing authority extend to advanced instruction beyond the ordinary common-school curriculum?

The Michigan Supreme Court upheld the district. The decision became influential in legitimating tax-supported public high schools in Michigan and beyond.

Primary legal source:

- *Michigan Reports*, vol. 30, pp. 69 ff.  
  https://www.courts.michigan.gov/49595c/siteassets/publications/msc-bound-volumes/1-100/bv_mi30_final.pdf

Context:

- Kalamazoo Public Library, "The Kalamazoo School Case."  
  https://www.kpl.gov/local-history/kalamazoo-history/kps/kalamazoo-school-case/

The case matters for the question "why do we have to go to high school?" because the familiar K–12 public system depended on an earlier political decision that secondary education was a legitimate public expenditure rather than a private purchase for families who wanted more than elementary schooling.

## 1890–1910 — the institution is established; the mass movement has not yet happened

By 1910 the United States had thousands of public high schools, but graduation remained uncommon. The NCES graduate-to-age-17 ratio was only 8.8 percent in 1909–10.

Goldin and Katz estimate secondary-school enrollment at about 18 percent of U.S. youths in 1910 and graduation at about 9 percent.

This is the baseline for the high school movement.

Sources:

- Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz, "Why the United States Led in Education: Lessons from Secondary School Expansion, 1910 to 1940," NBER Working Paper 6144 (1997).  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w6144
- Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz, "Mass Secondary Schooling and the State," NBER Working Paper 10075 (2003).  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w10075

## 1910–1940 — the high school movement

This is the decisive period.

Goldin and Katz call the growth of secondary schooling the "second transformation" of American education. In roughly three decades the United States moved from minority secondary schooling to mass secondary schooling.

Their summary:

- **1910:** about 18 percent enrolled; about 9 percent graduated.
- **1940:** about 71 percent enrolled; about 51 percent graduated.

The expansion was geographically uneven. The Far West, Great Plains, and parts of New England tended to lead. The South lagged. Small towns in the "education belt," particularly the Midwest and Great Plains, were important early sites of mass high-school development.

Goldin's state-level work finds especially rapid expansion outside the South from about 1920 to 1935. The 1920s produced a major attendance surge, and the Great Depression further increased enrollment and graduation by reducing youth employment opportunities and altering the opportunity cost of staying in school.

Sources:

- Claudia Goldin, "How America Graduated from High School: 1910 to 1960," NBER Working Paper 4762 (1994).

- Claudia Goldin, "America's Graduation from High School: The Evolution and Spread of Secondary Schooling in the Twentieth Century," *Journal of Economic History* 58, no. 2 (1998): 345–374.

## 1918 — *Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education*

In 1918 the National Education Association's Commission on the Reorganization of Secondary Education issued *Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education*, published by the U.S. Bureau of Education as Bulletin No. 35.

Its seven objectives were:

1. health;
2. command of fundamental processes;
3. worthy home membership;
4. vocation;
5. citizenship;
6. worthy use of leisure;
7. ethical character.

The report is often treated as a landmark because it articulated a broad social mission for secondary education and explicitly discussed secondary education as necessary for all youth.

But its chronology matters. It did **not** create the public high school. The Boston high school dated to 1821, public high schools had spread during the nineteenth century, and the 1910–1940 expansion was already beginning. The report is better read as an important act of **codification, reorganization, and legitimation** during an ongoing transition toward mass secondary schooling.

Primary source:

- Department of the Interior, Bureau of Education, *Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education: A Report of the Commission on the Reorganization of Secondary Education*, Bulletin 1918, No. 35.  
  ERIC record and full text: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED541063

## 1910s–1930s — compulsory education and child-labor law tighten

The same decades saw states strengthen compulsory-attendance and child-labor rules. A superficial timeline can therefore make it look as though the laws caused the high-school movement.

Goldin and Katz test this interpretation using cross-state changes in the laws. Their estimate is deliberately sobering: the changes in compulsory-schooling and child-labor laws between 1910 and 1939 can account for **at most about 5 percent** of the increase in high-school enrollment, and about the same proportion of the increase in eventual educational attainment for affected cohorts.

Source:

- Goldin and Katz, "Mass Secondary Schooling and the State."  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w10075

This is one of the most important causal warnings in this folder. "The state made children attend" is part of the history, but it is not an adequate explanation of the mass transition.

## 1915 — high estimated private returns to secondary schooling

Goldin and Katz use the unusually detailed Iowa State Census of 1915 to estimate returns to schooling before modern wage surveys existed.

They estimate returns to a year of high school at roughly 11 percent for males overall and above 12 percent for young males. The returns arose partly because more education opened access to better-paid white-collar work, but educational wage differences also existed within broad occupational sectors.

Source:

- Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz, "Education and Income in the Early 20th Century: Evidence from the Prairies," NBER Working Paper 7217 (1999); published in *Journal of Economic History* 60, no. 3 (2000): 782–818.  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w7217

These estimates supply one reason families and communities might voluntarily support additional schooling even when the law did not force four years of high school.

## 1929–1940 — Depression-era acceleration

The NCES historical graduate ratio nearly doubles during the 1930s:

- 1929–30: 29.0 percent.
- 1939–40: 50.8 percent.

The increase is too large to treat the 1940 majority threshold as a slow continuation of nineteenth-century trends. It belongs to a genuine institutional transformation.

Goldin emphasizes that the Depression increased high-school enrollment and graduation. Reduced demand for teenage labor lowered the opportunity cost of staying in school.

- NCES table 219.10.  
  https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_219.10.asp
- Goldin, "How America Graduated from High School."  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w4762

## 1939–40 — annual graduation crosses the majority line

NCES reports 1,221,475 high-school graduates and an age-17 population of 2,403,074, a ratio of **50.8 percent**.

Goldin and Katz summarize the 1940 graduation fraction as **51 percent**.

This is the cleanest date for saying that graduating from high school had become a majority outcome for the current youth generation.

It was not yet a majority characteristic of the adult population.

## 1940 — young-adult attainment still below one half

Among people age 25–29, only **38.1 percent** had high-school completion or higher in 1940.

Among all people age 25+, the figure was only **24.5 percent**.

The high-school movement had transformed the flow of new graduates faster than it could transform the stock of adults.

Sources:

- NCES table 104.20.  
  https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_104.20.asp
- NCES table 104.10.  
  https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d15/tables/dt15_104.10.asp

## 1950 — majority of young adults have completed high school

By 1950, **52.8 percent of people age 25–29** had completed high school or more.

This is a second important threshold. At this point the credential is no longer merely being earned by a majority of current adolescents; it is held by a majority of young adults entering the central years of the labor market.

The aggregate hides major racial inequality. NCES reports 56.3 percent for white 25–29-year-olds and 23.6 percent for Black 25–29-year-olds.

Source: NCES table 104.20.  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_104.20.asp

## 1960 — most adults still did not have the credential

Even in 1960, only **41.1 percent of all adults age 25+** had completed high school or higher.

This is another reason to distinguish the youth norm from the population norm. Institutions can begin treating a credential as standard among new entrants long before most older workers possess it.

Source: NCES table 104.10.  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d15/tables/dt15_104.10.asp

## 1970 — majority of the whole adult population

By 1970, **55.2 percent of all people age 25+** had high-school completion or higher.

The mass-high-school regime that had emerged for youths by 1940 had finally diffused far enough through successive cohorts that the credential became a majority characteristic of the entire adult population.

Source: NCES table 104.10.  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d15/tables/dt15_104.10.asp

## What this timeline does not yet establish

This timeline tells us when the public high school appeared, when it became politically supportable, when mass enrollment and graduation arrived, and when the credential became common in the population.

It does **not yet establish exactly when employers began writing "high school diploma required" into ordinary job requirements**.

That should become a separate evidence track. Useful sources would include:

- digitized classified job advertisements by decade;
- civil-service eligibility rules;
- large-employer personnel manuals;
- apprenticeship entrance requirements;
- military enlistment and occupational rules;
- union constitutions and training standards;
- Census occupational microdata linked to education;
- historical surveys of employer hiring practices.

The hypothesis to test is not merely that schooling rose, but that **the spread of the credential changed the labor-market penalty for lacking it**. That is the bridge from "more people went to high school" to "you have to go to high school."
