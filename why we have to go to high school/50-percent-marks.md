# The 50 percent mark: which one?

The phrase "high-school completion passed 50 percent" is ambiguous. At least three different historical quantities are often collapsed into that sentence.

## 1. Annual graduates relative to the 17-year-old population

NCES publishes a long-run series that divides the number of high-school graduates in a school year by the estimated population age 17 in that year.

| school year | high-school graduates | 17-year-old population | graduates / age-17 population |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1869–70 | 16,000 | 815,000 | 2.0% |
| 1879–80 | 23,634 | 946,026 | 2.5% |
| 1889–90 | 43,731 | 1,259,177 | 3.5% |
| 1899–1900 | 94,883 | 1,489,146 | 6.4% |
| 1909–10 | 156,429 | 1,786,240 | 8.8% |
| 1919–20 | 311,266 | 1,855,173 | 16.8% |
| 1929–30 | 666,904 | 2,295,822 | 29.0% |
| **1939–40** | **1,221,475** | **2,403,074** | **50.8%** |
| 1949–50 | 1,199,700 | 2,034,450 | 59.0% |
| 1959–60 | 1,858,023 | 2,672,000 | 69.5% |
| 1969–70 | 2,888,639 | 3,757,000 | 76.9% |

Source: National Center for Education Statistics, Digest of Education Statistics, table 219.10, "High school graduates, by sex and control of school; public high school averaged freshman graduation rate (AFGR); public high school 4-year adjusted cohort graduation rate (ACGR); and total graduates as a ratio of 17-year-old population: Selected school years, 1869-70 through 2031-32."  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_219.10.asp

### Important NCES caveat

NCES explicitly says this ratio is **not a cohort graduation rate**. The numerator contains people of all ages graduating in that year, while the denominator is the 17-year-old population. It is designed to permit historical comparisons, not to estimate the probability that an entering ninth-grader will graduate four years later.

That caveat is not a reason to discard the series. It is a reason to name the series correctly.

### Relation to Goldin and Katz

Goldin and Katz summarize the same transformation by saying that the fraction of U.S. youths graduating from secondary school rose from roughly **9 percent in 1910 to 51 percent in 1940**.

Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz, "Mass Secondary Schooling and the State," NBER Working Paper 10075, 2003.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w10075

So when we previously discussed "high-school completion passing 50 percent," **1939–40 / 1940 is the appropriate date if the intended object is the contemporary flow of graduates.**

## 2. Educational attainment of young adults, age 25–29

A different question is: what share of young adults had completed high school by the time the Census measured them?

NCES reports:

| year | age 25–29: high-school completion or higher |
|---|---:|
| 1940 | 38.1% |
| **1950** | **52.8%** |
| 1960 | 60.7% |
| 1970 | 75.4% |
| 1980 | 85.4% |

Source: NCES Digest, table 104.20.  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_104.20.asp

Thus **1950** is the first reported census year in this series in which a majority of 25–29-year-olds had completed high school.

This lag relative to the 1940 graduate-flow statistic is expected. A stock measure of people age 25–29 averages several birth cohorts and reflects schooling decisions made years earlier.

## 3. Educational attainment of all adults age 25+

A third question is when high-school completion became the majority status of the entire adult population.

NCES reports:

| year | age 25+: high-school completion or higher |
|---|---:|
| 1910 | 13.5% |
| 1920 | 16.4% |
| 1930 | 19.1% |
| 1940 | 24.5% |
| 1950 | 34.3% |
| 1960 | 41.1% |
| **1970** | **55.2%** |
| 1975 | 62.5% |
| 1980 | 68.6% |

Source: NCES Digest, table 104.10.  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d15/tables/dt15_104.10.asp

Thus **1970** is the first listed year in which a majority of the entire 25+ population had completed high school.

This is much later than 1940 because the adult population in 1940, 1950, and 1960 still contained many people who had reached adolescence before the high-school movement.

## Why all three dates are correct

They answer different questions:

| question | majority threshold in these historical series |
|---|---|
| Were most members of the current youth generation graduating? | **1939–40: 50.8% graduate / age-17 ratio** |
| Did most young adults possess the credential? | **1950: 52.8% of ages 25–29** |
| Did most adults of all ages possess the credential? | **1970: 55.2% of ages 25+** |

The sequence itself is historically revealing:

**mass youth behavior → young-adult norm → whole-population norm**.

It took about thirty years for the new mass-schooling regime to work its way from teenage graduation into the educational stock of the entire adult population.

## Enrollment is yet another statistic

Goldin and Katz report that secondary-school enrollment rose from roughly **18 percent of U.S. youths in 1910 to 71 percent in 1940**. Enrollment therefore crossed 50 percent before graduation did.

That should not be reported as "half of Americans completed high school." It means that a majority of youths were participating in secondary schooling.

Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz, "Why the United States Led in Education: Lessons from Secondary School Expansion, 1910 to 1940," NBER Working Paper 6144, 1997.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w6144

Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz, "Mass Secondary Schooling and the State," NBER Working Paper 10075, 2003.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w10075

## Racial inequality inside the national threshold

The national aggregate crossed 50 percent long before high-school completion was equally distributed.

For ages 25–29, NCES reports:

| year | total | white | Black |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1940 | 38.1% | 41.2% | 12.3% |
| 1950 | 52.8% | 56.3% | 23.6% |
| 1960 | 60.7% | 63.7% | 38.6% |
| 1970 | 75.4% | 77.8% | 58.4% |

Source: NCES Digest, table 104.20. Historical category labels are those used by the source.  
https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_104.20.asp

So the statement "most young Americans had completed high school by 1950" is true as a national aggregate and radically incomplete as a description of equal access.

## Rule for future Blackball notes

Do not write simply:

> High-school completion passed 50 percent in 1940.

Write one of the following instead:

- "The annual number of high-school graduates reached 50.8 percent of the 17-year-old population in 1939–40."
- "Goldin and Katz estimate that about 51 percent of U.S. youths graduated from secondary school by 1940."
- "The share of 25–29-year-olds with high-school completion or higher was 52.8 percent in 1950."
- "The share of all adults age 25+ with high-school completion or higher reached 55.2 percent in 1970."

That small amount of extra wording prevents three distinct historical transitions from being collapsed into one number.
