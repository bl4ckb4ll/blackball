# Sunk-cost lock-in and lies

The durable education problem is not exhausted by saying that rational people should ignore sunk costs.

People routinely **honor sunk costs**. After spending years, money, status, and effort becoming a lawyer, professor, physician, or other professional, a person may say:

> I got tricked into this whole lawyer thing. Now I have to find a way to make the lawyer thing work.

That response is descriptively common enough to matter even if a textbook labels it non-optimal under a specified decision rule. Once it is predictable, an institution can benefit from inducing the commitment before the buyer knows what the back end looks like.

## Keep three claims separate

1. **Descriptive:** what people actually do after a large irreversible commitment.
2. **Normative:** what someone should do under a stated objective.
3. **Model-conditional:** what maximizes that objective under a chosen set of assumptions and constraints.

“Do not consider sunk costs” belongs mainly to the second category. It is not a factual claim that people do not consider them. “Optimal” is incomplete unless the speaker states optimal for whom, according to which objective, counting which costs, over what period, and under whose constraints.

## Sunk expenditure and present constraint

Several things coexist after expensive professional education:

- Tuition and elapsed years already spent are sunk.
- Outstanding principal, interest, and future payments are not sunk; they are enforceable future obligations.
- The résumé, knowledge, professional network, identity, age, family expectations, and lost experience in other occupations are current state variables.
- Starting over may now be more expensive than it was before enrollment.
- A high-paying but morally objectionable job may dominate the remaining alternatives because the education helped create the debt and narrowed the practical choice set.

It is therefore too easy to say that the graduate remains “free to do anything.” Formally possible is not economically equivalent to feasible.

## The commitment trap

The recurring transaction has this shape:

**attractive representation → front-loaded payment and years of commitment → contrary evidence arrives gradually → exit now carries psychological, financial, social, and occupational costs → seller has already been paid**

The seller does not need to force continued participation directly. The buyer’s debt, identity, embarrassment, family expectations, and desire not to admit the loss can help enforce the path.

Measure whether institutions know and use this mechanism through:

- escalating credentials and prerequisite chains;
- “you are almost there” messaging;
- advice to obtain another degree after the first one fails to convert;
- selective alumni stories;
- status identities that make ordinary work feel like personal defeat;
- career services that redirect the graduate toward any job that preserves the credential’s appearance of value;
- loan structures that make the best-paid buyer of the graduate’s labor the only viable buyer.

## A lie is not merely imperfect information

Uncertainty, noisy signals, asymmetric information, optimism, mistake, concealment, and lying are not interchangeable.

A useful account of a lie or strategically misleading representation needs at least:

- a speaker or institution;
- a proposition, image, omission, or induced inference;
- knowledge, reckless disregard, or privileged access to contrary evidence;
- an audience expected to act;
- an incentive attached to the audience’s action;
- a difference between what the audience reasonably inferred and what the speaker was willing to guarantee.

A statement can be literally defensible and still be designed to produce a false conclusion. “Graduates work in many fields,” “the degree opens doors,” “our alumni include,” and “students have opportunities to” may be used so that a young person hears a much stronger occupational promise than the school will put into a contract.

Probability language alone does not preserve the moral structure. The student can hold the same numerical belief after an honest mistake, an incomplete disclosure, selective upper-tail advertising, or an intentional lie. Those are different transactions.

## Written in blood versus invisible ink

The user’s formulation should be preserved:

- repayment, interest, fees, and collection are written in blood;
- predicted success is written in invisible ink.

The institution can legally enforce the student’s side of the transaction while disclaiming the occupational inference that justified the purchase. This asymmetry belongs beside every calculation of educational return.

## Downstream moral effects

Debt and sunk investment can change whom a professional serves.

- A law graduate may abandon public-interest plans for corporate or creditor work.
- A professor may optimize for publications, grants, promotion, and the next appointment rather than students.
- A physician may enter a high-paying specialty or institutional role needed to service debt.
- An MBA graduate may accept work that makes financial sense for the graduate while imposing costs on workers or communities.

Do not assume the moral compromise in advance. Record the intended role before training, the actual role afterward, the debt and income constraints, available alternatives, and the person’s own explanation.

## Evidence standard

For each pathway, preserve separately:

- what was represented before enrollment;
- what the institution knew about ordinary and disappointing outcomes;
- what the student reasonably inferred;
- what was contractually guaranteed;
- what obligations remained after failure;
- when contrary information became available;
- what made exit difficult;
- what work the graduate ultimately performed and for whom.

This turns “bad information” into an accountable sequence of actors, representations, commitments, and losses.
