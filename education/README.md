# Education

This folder evaluates education as a transaction imposed on real people with limited money, time, and alternatives.

It does not ask only whether education is culturally admirable or whether some graduates become successful. It asks what was represented, what the student paid, what the institution guaranteed, what the student actually received, and who kept the loss when the expected result failed.

## Core notes

- [Truth before hope](truth-before-hope.md) — do not use optimism or exceptional success stories as substitutes for a feasible path; discouraging evidence does not require a reassuring correction.
- [Sunk-cost lock-in and lies](sunk-cost-lock-in-and-lies.md) — people honor sunk costs; commitment changes later behavior; distinguish uncertainty from strategic deception; inspect the friendly front end and asymmetric back end separately.
- [Where are the students’ jobs?](where-are-the-students-jobs.md) — seller success is not customer success; separate employment from target-job conversion and inspect the actual work.
- [Retrospective regret and attention cost](retrospective-regret-and-attention-cost.md) — count long-run regret, lost trust, and time consumed by the transaction rather than allowing earnings alone to declare success.
- [When the seller controls the outcome story](seller-controls-the-outcome-story.md) — institutional reports, alumni selection, moving definitions of value, and the disappearance of retail, unrelated, and disappointing outcomes.

Related economics note:

- [Optimal for whom, and what did the model omit?](../economics/optimal-for-whom-and-what-the-model-omits.md) — distinguishes description, private optimization, social-welfare claims, and moral justification; makes excluded people and costs explicit in NPV and education-return calculations.

## Recurring tests

For every program or degree:

1. What exact result was represented before enrollment?
2. Was the representation descriptive, aspirational, conditional, induced by implication, or guaranteed?
3. What did the institution know about ordinary, disappointing, and delayed outcomes?
4. What were the full costs in money, interest, time, foregone earnings, lost work experience, housing, family formation, and alternatives?
5. What percentage reached the specific target occupation?
6. What happened to the rest?
7. Did the graduate need another degree, unpaid work, relocation, family money, or elite sponsorship?
8. Was the eventual job inside education itself?
9. What ordinary tasks did the graduate perform, for whom, and against whom?
10. What obligations could the institution, lender, or collector enforce against the student?
11. What obligation did the institution accept if the promised or implied result failed?
12. Did debt or sunk investment push the graduate toward work they previously rejected?
13. Would the graduate knowingly purchase the same transaction again?
14. What evidence is missing because institutions do not collect or publish disappointing outcomes?
15. Does the seller dismiss negative cases as anecdotal while refusing to construct the denominator that would test them?
