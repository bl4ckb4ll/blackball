# If you mean economic empowerment, put it in the contract

## The claim under test

Education can be valuable as knowledge, culture, pleasure, social experience, or access to a tradition.

That is different from the claim that **education itself economically empowers the buyer**.

When a university, school, policymaker, employer, or advocate moves from cultural value to economic language — “opens doors,” “changes your life,” “creates opportunity,” “raises lifetime earning potential,” “gives you freedom,” “lets you set your own terms” — Blackball should stop treating “education” as one undifferentiated good.

Ask what exactly is being claimed to cause what.

Possible mechanisms include:

- knowledge or skill learned;
- the credential as a hiring screen;
- family money and ability to wait;
- inherited social position;
- professional networks;
- geographic mobility;
- unpaid or low-paid internships;
- institutional prestige;
- employer selection of people who were already advantaged;
- additional graduate credentials;
- luck and scarce openings.

The statement “education empowers” does not identify which mechanism did the work.

## The contract test

If the economic claim is stated with certainty, translate it into terms that could in principle be written into a contract.

Specify:

1. **outcome** — which occupation, earnings level, hours, stability, autonomy, or other concrete result?
2. **population** — which students does the claim cover?
3. **deadline** — by when after enrollment or graduation?
4. **conditions** — geography, further degrees, internships, family support, relocation, licensing, networking, or other requirements;
5. **denominator** — what fraction of comparable entrants actually receive the outcome?
6. **counterfactual** — what would comparable people have earned or done without this education?
7. **liability** — what does the seller owe when the promised result does not occur?

This is not a demand that every uncertain forecast become a guarantee. It is a demand that **uncertain statistical or aspirational claims not be spoken in the grammar of guarantees**.

If the institution will not accept liability for an outcome, the claim should be presented with the corresponding uncertainty.

## Michigan's useful contrast: a guarantee that actually says what it guarantees

The University of Michigan's **Go Blue Guarantee** is useful because it shows that the university knows how to state an enforceable-looking commitment precisely.

For 2026–27 on the Ann Arbor campus, U-M states that qualifying Michigan residents with family income of $125,000 or less and assets below $125,000 receive undergraduate tuition and mandatory-fee coverage for up to four years, subject to stated eligibility conditions.

Official current sources:

- https://goblueguarantee.umich.edu/ann-arbor/
- https://finaid.umich.edu/apply-aid/new-undergraduates/michigan-residents/go-blue-guarantee-eligibility

That is a real, bounded promise about **price**.

The same admissions material also markets Michigan using language about graduation, a global alumni network, and “strong lifetime earning potential”:

- https://admissions.umich.edu/costs-aid/michigan-residents

Those are different types of statement. The tuition promise has explicit eligibility rules and a specified benefit. “Strong lifetime earning potential” is not an individual earnings guarantee.

Blackball should never allow the precision of the first claim to leak into the second.

## The class/cultivation problem

The cases of Olive Bray, Paul Klee's art-school contemporaries, Gauss, Tycho Brahe, patrons, and donor families point toward a different causal possibility.

For some people, years spent reading, painting, translating, doing mathematics, traveling, or studying are **uses of pre-existing freedom** rather than the cause of that freedom.

A household with substantial property, income, position, or patronage can rationally purchase years of cultivation for a child without requiring the activity itself to produce wages.

A household without that buffer faces a different constraint. Paid work may not be a character-building exercise; it may simply be necessary for subsistence.

Therefore do not infer:

**educated person has freedom → education caused the freedom.**

Test the direction of causation.

## What this argument does not require

It does not require any of these claims:

- money is evil;
- wealthy parents are wrong to educate their children;
- scholarship is fraudulent;
- profit is inherently immoral;
- a worker should receive the same income as an owner or founder;
- a judge's child should be sent to a factory;
- art and mathematics have no value;
- every university graduate is already rich.

The narrower claim is enough:

**material freedom and cultural education are distinct variables, and the first can finance the second.**

When institutions sell the second as the cause of the first, demand evidence.

## Pamphleteering test

A successful public argument does not need to convert people to a comprehensive theory of class.

For a Detroit or Michigan audience considering an expensive educational path, a short intervention can ask:

- What exactly is guaranteed?
- What is merely advertised?
- Which costs remain with me?
- Which job or earnings outcome is actually promised?
- What happens if that outcome does not occur?
- Did successful examples already possess money, networks, mobility, or family support that I would not receive with the degree?

The aim is not to tell people not to learn. It is to prevent a cultural good, a status good, and an economic guarantee from being sold as though they were the same product.
