# When the seller controls the outcome story

A university usually has much more power than an individual graduate to define publicly what the transaction meant.

The institution controls admissions language, program pages, career reports, alumni magazines, development campaigns, commencement speeches, employer partnerships, and many of the categories used in first-destination surveys. A disappointed graduate controls a private memory, an awkward résumé, and perhaps an anonymous post that may never be indexed or preserved.

That difference affects what later students can see.

## Talking one’s own book

A university is not a neutral observer of whether university education is valuable. Tuition, employment, status, real estate, research infrastructure, donations, and political legitimacy all depend on continued belief in the institution.

Therefore separate:

- an institution’s claims about its own value;
- administrative data with inspectable definitions and denominators;
- independent outcome data;
- graduate testimony;
- employer testimony about actual task performance;
- comparative evidence from people who entered the same workplace without the degree.

Institutional self-interest does not make every statement false. It means the statement is interested evidence and should be treated accordingly.

## How disappointing outcomes disappear

Common reporting choices can make weak conversion difficult to see:

- combining employment with further education;
- reporting the percentage of respondents rather than the graduating class;
- reporting a college-wide figure instead of major-specific destinations;
- counting any employment without stating whether the job required the degree;
- using broad industry categories rather than occupations and ordinary tasks;
- publishing salary data only for graduates who report salary;
- omitting part-time, temporary, retail, food-service, warehouse, and unrelated work from prominent stories;
- using a short post-graduation window that misses instability and later regret;
- highlighting famous alumni without a denominator;
- treating academic employment as independent confirmation of outside demand;
- describing continued education as success without counting its additional price and delay.

The result need not be a fabricated number. A technically accurate presentation can still cause the audience to infer that the ordinary outcome is stronger than the underlying distribution.

## The status-preserving reinterpretation

When the occupational result is weak, the seller can change what the buyer supposedly purchased.

Before enrollment, the emphasis may be jobs, earnings, opportunity, mobility, and doors opened. After a graduate enters retail or unrelated work, the explanation may become dignity, breadth, critical thinking, exposure to Shakespeare, personal growth, citizenship, or an ineffable transformation.

Those goods can be evaluated. They are not interchangeable with the occupational and financial claims that helped justify the price. Do not allow the institution to move from an economic promise to a non-falsifiable cultural defense only after the economic conversion fails.

Ask:

- Was this non-occupational value stated as the main product before payment?
- Could it have been obtained much more cheaply?
- Did the graduate knowingly choose it over housing, earnings, work experience, or another path?
- Does the school refund any part of the price when the occupational inference fails?
- Would the school market the program just as successfully if it removed all career and earnings language?

## Negative cases are structurally hard to observe

The University of Michigan psychology-retail interview lead illustrates the problem. A manager with less credential prestige reportedly supervised several U-M psychology graduates, found some poorly suited to the actual work, and advanced through experience into a corporate role. The graduates’ prestigious credential remained socially visible; the manager’s demonstrated superiority in that workplace did not automatically reverse the perceived status ranking.

No conclusion about the population follows from one interview. But if cases like this are not deliberately collected, the public record will be dominated by the institution’s selected success stories. Absence from the alumni magazine is not absence from the labor market.

## Seller response to criticism

Measure how institutions respond when presented with poor outcomes:

- publish a major-specific distribution;
- explain definitions and missing respondents;
- acknowledge prior representations that were too strong;
- lower price or enrollment;
- alter curriculum or occupational claims;
- accept financial responsibility;
- dismiss the case as anecdotal while refusing to collect the data that would establish prevalence;
- shift blame to the graduate’s effort, networking, attitude, major choice, geography, or failure to obtain another credential;
- substitute the institution’s own survival or cultural importance for customer value.

A system that rejects every negative case as anecdotal and declines to build a denominator has designed itself not to learn.

## Required counter-record

For every school and program, seek:

- complete occupation distributions;
- jobs that do and do not require the credential;
- related and unrelated work;
- pay and hours;
- debt and repayment;
- further education and its cost;
- time to stable employment;
- graduates who omit the degree from applications;
- retail, service, warehouse, administrative, temporary, and contingent outcomes;
- managers’ evaluations of graduates’ actual work;
- non-graduate comparators in the same workplace;
- long-run willingness to purchase the degree again;
- anger, regret, and loss of trust;
- people who no longer answer alumni surveys or identify with the institution.

The purpose is not to reverse selection and publish only disasters. It is to stop the seller’s communications operation from standing in for the customer distribution.
