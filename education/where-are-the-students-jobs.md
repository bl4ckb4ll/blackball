# Where are the students’ jobs?

This is the education version of *Where Are the Customers’ Yachts?*

When a university answers criticism by pointing to professors’ salaries, faculty publications, grants, rankings, buildings, endowment, famous alumni, or the institution’s own continued growth, it is describing the seller’s success. The customer asked what the institution would do for the customer.

The first question is therefore:

> Where are the students’ jobs?

The complete question is harder:

> Where are the jobs, what jobs are they, what did it cost to reach them, who does the graduate serve, and would the student knowingly buy the same transaction again?

## Seller outcomes are not customer outcomes

Do not use any of the following as substitutes for student value:

- a professor obtained a desirable academic position;
- the department publishes influential research;
- the school attracts grants or donations;
- the institution’s graduates include unusually successful people;
- the school can hire some of its own or other schools’ graduates;
- the credential remains prestigious among people who already possess it.

These may be real achievements. They do not establish that a paying student received a relevant job, adequate income, useful knowledge, or a transaction worth its price.

## The professor's opportunity is not the student's

A professor educated at Harvard can teach serious mathematics at a Midwestern college. The mathematical content may be excellent. But the students have not thereby been admitted to Harvard or granted access to its campus recruiting, alumni networks, employer interviews, or reputation. **A teacher's biography is not a student's job opportunity.**

The student's question is: **“What exactly are you going to do for me, Professor?”** The student contributes money, time, attention, and foregone alternatives. Instruction is a service to examine, not a favor for which gratitude substitutes for results. Keep the obligations of the individual teacher, department, university, and prospective employer distinct; do not assign a teacher a placement guarantee that nobody made.

For an actual department, program, or instructor, investigate:

1. **Delivered teaching:** What did students learn, make, prove, or practice? What individualized feedback and assessment did they receive? Do not assume that valuable education must result in a high-paying job.
2. **Career representations:** What jobs were mentioned in advising, lectures, admissions material, or recruiting? Were they described as possible, probable, accessible to these students, or guaranteed? Preserve original dated statements and context.
3. **Actual access:** Which named employers came to *this campus*, accepted applications from *these students*, interviewed them, offered work, or recruited through a verified referral? A prestigious professor's previous school or career is not evidence that a student receives the same access.
4. **Concrete faculty actions:** Were students given introductions, recommendations, industry contacts, portfolios, research placements, or actual employer conversations? Record offers and refusals, not merely the existence of a relationship.
5. **Student conversion:** Of the relevant entrants, how many received the specific advertised occupation, when, for what pay and work, and after what further expenses? Preserve missing respondents, non-completion, unrelated work, and students who left the path. Do not infer a rate from conspicuous winners.
6. **Opportunity cost:** What wages, work experience, alternative training, savings, housing, or family responsibilities did the student forgo? Compare plausible options available to that student, not to the professor at an elite institution.
7. **Accountability:** When actual recruiting access is absent, was that disclosed before students paid? Did the instructor or institution correct inflated claims rather than appealing to inspirational examples?

### Falsifiers and evidence boundaries

- A non-elite college may have strong recruiting or personally effective faculty referrals; record such examples when observed.
- A professor can teach valuable material honestly without promising employment. Evaluate the teaching on what was actually offered, not on an invented job guarantee.
- The fact that a prestigious employer visits one campus does not prove that every other campus is excluded. Check original employer eligibility rules, recruiting calendars, applications, interviews, and offers for named firms, schools, occupations, and years.
- A professor's elite credential, a student's mathematical ability, recruiting access, and eventual career outcome are four separate variables. Do not deduce one from another.

The college-specific recruiting asymmetry is a **research hypothesis** until evidenced for the institutions and employers being compared.

## Separate employment from target-job conversion

Record:

1. the occupation the student sought or was led to expect;
2. the courses, laboratory work, internships, and credentials represented as preparation;
3. whether the student obtained that occupation;
4. time from enrollment to stable work;
5. every additional credential or contingent stage required;
6. debt, interest, foregone earnings, relocation, and unpaid work;
7. whether employment was inside education itself;
8. compensation, hours, schedule, geographic constraint, and stability;
9. actual clients, employer, and ordinary tasks;
10. whether the graduate would choose the path again with full knowledge.

“Employed” can include retail work, temporary work, unrelated work, another degree, a postdoc, or a job created by the education system for its own output. Those outcomes must not be silently made equivalent.

## Academic self-reproduction

A graduate becoming a professor is genuine employment, but it is partly endogenous evidence. The institution is using the education system’s ability to employ some of its own output as evidence that its output has general occupational value.

Report separately:

- faculty;
- postdoctoral appointments;
- academic research staff;
- teaching-only or contingent appointments;
- administration, admissions, career services, and other education-sector employment;
- employment by an outside organization purchasing the trained skill for a non-educational purpose.

Elite institutions also sit at the top of the academic hiring hierarchy. Their unusually strong access to professor jobs cannot be generalized to students at ordinary institutions without evidence.

## A job has moral and practical content

A salary number is not enough. Compare, for example:

- representing injured workers versus defeating their claims;
- repairing people’s cars versus helping a company externalize costs;
- teaching students clearly versus treating teaching as an obstacle to research;
- having control over hours versus being continuously subordinate inside a corporate hierarchy;
- earning the same nominal income in radically different housing, commuting, family, and geographic conditions.

The question is not whether Blackball approves of a profession. It is whether an outcome table hides the actual work and the compromises needed to obtain the income.

## Strongest-case ceiling

An unusually advantaged candidate is useful as a stress test. If a person with an elite credential, strong mentors, specialized work, exceptional effort, and access to the top of the hierarchy still cannot convert training into the advertised occupation without a long detour, debt pressure, or an internal academic job, that weakens easy explanations based on insufficient effort or school prestige.

One case does not establish a population rate. It can nevertheless expose the ceiling of the pathway and change the prior for someone with fewer advantages.

## Retrospective customer test

Institutions usually ask whether graduates are employed shortly after completion. Also ask years later:

- Did the institution deserve the money?
- Was the education necessary for the eventual work?
- How much of the training is used?
- Would the graduate purchase it again at the same price?
- Would the graduate pay to undo the decision if that were possible?
- How many years of resentment, regret, or preoccupation did the transaction produce?
- Did the graduate postpone housing, children, retirement saving, or another occupation?
- Did the path require work contrary to the values used to attract the student?

A person can earn an acceptable salary and still judge the transaction a failure. Earnings alone cannot overrule the customer’s informed retrospective judgment.

## Front-door question

Before payment, require a direct answer:

- Do you guarantee a relevant job?
- Do you guarantee a salary range?
- Will you refund the price if the occupational result does not occur?
- What percentage of comparable students obtained the specific result, not merely “employment or further study”?
- What happened to everyone else?

When the answers are no, record no. Do not let prestige language supply a guarantee that the contract withholds.
