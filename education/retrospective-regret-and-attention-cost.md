# Retrospective regret and attention cost

Educational-outcome analysis should count more than earnings, employment, and debt balances.

A person may spend years repeatedly remembering that they believe an institution misled them. Resentment, humiliation, lost trust, counterfactual thinking, and the attention consumed by trying to understand what happened are part of the result of the transaction even when the person eventually earns an adequate income.

## The missing cost

The user’s formulation is direct:

> Even knowing how many hours of my life I have spent hating these people would be a good enough reason not to go to college.

This is not a claim that every disappointed graduate experiences the same thing. It identifies a class of costs that ordinary return-on-investment calculations omit:

- hours of rumination and anger;
- repeated attempts to reconstruct representations and responsibility;
- loss of trust in schools, experts, advisers, and institutions;
- shame or humiliation associated with having believed the story;
- conflict with family or peers who still believe it;
- delayed willingness to change occupations because doing so concedes the loss;
- the psychic burden of debt attached to an unwanted or unusable credential;
- the sense that years of life were taken under false premises.

Calling these feelings irrational does not make the hours reappear.

## Retrospective measures

Ask graduates at five-, ten-, twenty-, and thirty-year intervals:

- Would you enroll again at the same price?
- What is the maximum price at which you would enroll again?
- Would you choose the same field and institution?
- Would you prefer the same capital had been used for housing, a business, tools, direct training, or savings?
- How many years did the decision constrain work, family, housing, or geography?
- How much time do you still spend regretting, defending, or trying to understand it?
- Did the experience increase or destroy trust in the institution?
- Would you recommend the path to your own child with full disclosure of your outcome?

A useful summary measure is **retrospective willingness to repurchase**. It does not replace objective outcomes, but an institution should not be allowed to declare a transaction successful solely because the customer eventually survived it.

## Selection and silence

Institutions have reasons not to collect this information. Short first-destination surveys are easier to advertise than long-run customer judgments. Disappointed graduates may stop responding, avoid alumni networks, or feel ashamed to report an outcome that contradicts the prestige of the school. Graduates who benefited often remain more visible to the institution.

Blackball should therefore seek negative testimony deliberately rather than assuming that what appears in alumni magazines represents the graduate population.

## Decision use

A prospective student is not deciding only whether the expected salary exceeds tuition. The person is deciding whether to enter a transaction that may dominate decades of memory and self-understanding.

That cost is difficult to monetize. Difficulty is not permission to set it to zero.
