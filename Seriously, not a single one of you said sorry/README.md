# Seriously, not a single one of you said sorry?

## Question

After decades in which professors, departments, and universities recruited and trained students for academic and scientific careers while permanent academic employment became increasingly scarce, can we find senior professors who later said, in substance:

> I encouraged, recruited, trained, or advised people into this path. I was wrong about the prospects or about what we were doing. My actions or advice harmed some of you. I am sorry.

This dossier tests that question rather than assuming the answer.

## Strict mea-culpa test

A source counts as a **strict career/training mea culpa** only if the same identifiable professor or academic adviser does all of the following:

1. accepts first-person responsibility for recruiting, advising, training, or encouraging students into the path;
2. acknowledges that the advice/practice was wrong, materially misleading, or caused foreseeable harm;
3. identifies the student-side harm (lost years, foregone earnings, debt, delayed family/household formation, precarious employment, unusable specialization, or comparable consequences); and
4. actually apologizes — e.g. **“I am sorry,” “I apologize,”** or an unmistakable equivalent.

Merely saying that the academic hiring system is bad, that there are too many Ph.D.s, that government funding is inadequate, that administrators rely on cheap graduate labor, or that students should research the risks does **not** satisfy the test.

## First-pass result — 2026-09-03

**Strict career/training mea culpas found: 0.**

This is a negative search result, not a claim that no such document exists anywhere. The search log records the venues, years, query families, and limitations so that this headline can be falsified.

### Inspected core corpus

- **25** distinct core items were inspected and coded.
- **0** satisfy the strict career/training mea-culpa test.
- **1** is a literal professor-to-former-Ph.D.-student apology for a different harm: Martin J. Cohn says he wrote his first graduate student a letter of apology after concluding that his mentoring had driven her out of science.
- **3** are especially strong near-mea-culpas on career/training responsibility: Monica J. Harris, Matthew Gray, and Abdulkader Sinno.
- **8** inspected items propose, recommend, or enact fewer Ph.D. admissions / fewer trainees because opportunities do not justify the existing scale.
- **5** inspected items contain clear responsibility resistance, deflection, or opacity concerning outcomes. These include Jon Wakelyn’s statement that students are responsible for researching employment prospects; Jeffrey Cassisi’s concern about professors being held responsible for graduates’ outcomes; Leonard Cassuto’s 2023 claim that a Ponzi-like doctoral system is “really not the faculty’s fault”; an Inside Higher Ed argument that “the problem is the system itself” rather than out-of-touch faculty; and Paul B. Hopkins’s refusal to publish his chemistry department’s placement data unless comparable national data were published.

These counts are counts of the **coded corpus in `CORPUS.md`**, not estimates of how many such articles exist on the web.

## Strongest near-misses

### Monica J. Harris — 2010

Harris, a social-psychology professor with 23 years in the profession, wrote **“Stop Admitting Ph.D. Students.”** She explicitly rejected the idea that voluntary student enrollment absolves faculty, acknowledged that departments need graduate students to teach and professors need them to run labs, noted that she had chaired or co-chaired 13 Ph.D.s, and stopped accepting new doctoral students indefinitely.

This is unusually strong because it combines recognition of institutional/faculty self-interest, partial personal responsibility, and an actual costly change in behavior. It still does not contain an apology to former students.

Source: https://www.insidehighered.com/advice/2010/08/18/stop-admitting-phd-students

### Matthew Gray — 2015

Gray titled his essay **“Mea Culpa”** and wrote, “you’re right—I am culpable.” He said higher-education theatre programs had become academically narcissistic and complacent, with curricula badly disconnected from the economic and professional demands facing students.

This is the closest source found to the requested rhetoric. It accepts culpability for inadequate professional preparation. It does not, however, become a direct apology to former students for years/money/career harm.

Source: https://howlround.com/mea-culpa

### Abdulkader Sinno — 2022

Sinno resigned as his political-science department’s placement director because he did not want to be **“complicit”** in keeping students in a Ph.D. program that did not advance them. He said the department benefited from cheap graduate teaching and from claiming to operate a serious doctoral program, called the situation unethical, and said young people’s best years should not be squandered on a fool’s errand. He recommended that some programs, including his own, consider pausing recruitment.

Again: explicit complicity, explicit institutional benefit, explicit student harm, and an action. No apology to the people already put through the program.

Source: https://www.insidehighered.com/news/faculty/2022/12/06/faculty-member-issues-dire-warning-grad-students-about-jobs

## A real apology exists — but for a different harm

University of Florida professor Martin J. Cohn wrote that his first Ph.D. student quit science because he had driven her out by treating graduate training as a way to get more research done. He says:

> “A few years after she graduated, I wrote a letter of apology to that student, and I radically changed my approach to graduate student mentoring.”

That is important control evidence. A professor can retrospectively identify his own conduct as harmful, apologize to the student, and change his behavior. The missing object in this search is the analogous apology for recruitment/advice/training under poor career prospects.

Source: https://biomed.med.ufl.edu/2019/04/22/dr-martin-cohn-wins-the-2018-2019-uf-faculty-mentoring-award/

## Professors plainly know what an apology looks like

The broader search found unrelated teaching apologies in which educators use exactly the structure missing from the career literature: **I did X; I now understand that X caused harm; I apologize to my former students; I will do differently.** It also found higher-education teaching advice explicitly recommending that professors model sincere apologies by saying “I apologize” or “I’m sorry,” naming what happened, and committing to a different practice.

Examples:

- Dana Huff, 2020: “my ignorance caused harm ... I apologize to my former students.” https://www.huffenglish.com/tag/to-kill-a-mockingbird/
- Teaching in Higher Ed, 2015, **“How to apologize [as a professor]”**: https://teachinginhighered.com/2015/03/31/how-to-apologize/
- Patrick Hicks, 2012, **“Apology to my former student”**: https://www.salon.com/2012/08/27/apology_to_my_former_student/

So the working question is not whether academics possess the concept or vocabulary of apology. They demonstrably do.

## Responsibility-deflection examples

These are not treated as proof of bad faith. They are evidence about how responsibility is framed.

- **Jon Wakelyn (2018 Chronicle interview):** he knew the easy hiring conditions of his own 1966 cohort were gone, but said professors were not responsible for educating prospective graduate students about employment prospects; students should research that themselves. Source: https://www.chronicle.com/article/how-a-famous-academic-job-market-study-got-it-all-wrong-and-why-it-still-matters/
- **Leonard Cassuto (2023):** described a system with roughly 50 years of professorial job shortages as resembling a Ponzi scheme, conceded professorial willful ignorance, then argued that it was “really not the faculty’s fault.” Source: https://www.chronicle.com/article/how-to-empower-your-graduate-dean-and-save-the-ph-d
- **Jeffrey Cassisi (2016):** when Florida’s governor pushed universities toward graduate employment accountability, Cassisi said the idea that faculty might be responsible for young adults and their outcomes was what scared faculty, and that professors did not want to be “on the hook” for uncertain students. Source: https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2016/01/20/florida-governor-wants-know-why-all-psychology-majors-arent-employed
- **Paul B. Hopkins (2010):** declined to publish his chemistry department’s placement information on its own because he did not want his department to be the only one with data exposed. Source: https://www.chronicle.com/article/masters-in-english-will-mow-lawns/
- **“The problem is the system itself” (Inside Higher Ed, 2021):** argues that faculty generally know conditions are bad and that the underlying structure, rather than clueless faculty, explains continued Ph.D. overproduction. Source: https://www.insidehighered.com/advice/2021/06/22/how-phd-job-crisis-built-system-and-what-can-be-done-about-it-opinion

## Longstanding awareness

The problem was not discovered after 2008.

- In 1998 Leonard Cassuto was already writing in *The Chronicle* about a “murderous” academic hiring environment and faculty members observing that they themselves could not have survived contemporary hiring standards.
- Thomas H. Benton / William Pannapacker’s 2009 *Chronicle* piece **“Graduate School in the Humanities: Just Don’t Go”** says he had already been warning students in a 2003 column.
- Paula Stephan told *Nature* that calls for graduate-department “birth control” went back to **1977**.
- A 2014 PNAS paper by Bruce Alberts, Marc Kirschner, Shirley Tilghman, and Harold Varmus explicitly says successful biomedical scientists train far more scientists than are needed to replace themselves and recommends reducing entrants into biomedical Ph.D. training.

Sources:

- https://www.chronicle.com/article/pressures-to-publish-fuel-the-professionalization-of-todays-graduate-students/

- https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-019-03439-x

## Government/funding comparison

A separate comparison search looked for articles about professors/scientists describing damage to **their own careers, grants, institutions, tenure prospects, or research programs** from government funding and policy changes, especially under the Trump administrations. Such material is easy to find. The first targeted pass alone returned well over a dozen directly relevant Nature/Chronicle items from 2017 and 2025–2026, including career features on shattered scientific careers, tenure risk, layoffs, grant termination, reduced Ph.D. admissions, and a possible brain drain.

That comparison should **not** be read as saying those harms are unreal or undeserving of coverage. It is a search-asymmetry observation: first-person and institution-centered harm language is plentiful when academics themselves are losing jobs, grants, staff, or career opportunities, while the strict retrospective apology to students remains unfound in this pass.

Representative sources:

- https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-00091-0
- https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-01267-w
- https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-00608-z
- https://www.chronicle.com/article/a-lost-generation-of-researchers

## Falsification rule

The folder title is deliberately provocative; the evidence standard is not.

If a source is found that satisfies the strict four-part test, add it prominently, change the count immediately, and do not rescue the headline by moving the goalposts.

A particularly valuable find would be an older professor addressing students trained before or around the 2008 crisis and saying explicitly that the professor’s own prior advice/recruitment was wrong, that former students bore real life-course costs, and that the professor is sorry.
