# Roger L. Geiger — business schools and liberal arts

## Live baseline — 2026-08-30

**Status:** off-the-cuff answer from a live lookup, before systematic rereading of Geiger, his notes, bibliography, cited primary sources, or the secondary literature.

**Purpose:** preserve a falsifiable baseline. Later research should add a new, better account and compare it against this one rather than silently rewriting this file.

### Question

What did Roger L. Geiger say about the pairing of business schools with liberal arts?

### Answer given live

The passage I thought was being remembered is in *The History of American Higher Education*, in Geiger's discussion of business schools near the end of Chapter 11. My reading from the quick lookup was that Geiger describes the early business school as an attempt to make business into a university-level profession. The AACSB founders in 1916 wanted business to acquire something analogous to the professional standing of medicine and law. One of their basic accreditation requirements was that the business degree contain both liberal and business subjects.

Geiger identifies four arrangements universities actually used:

1. A four-year undergraduate business school, mixing liberal and business subjects throughout.
2. Especially in the Midwest, two years of liberal arts followed by two years of business.
3. Dartmouth and Michigan: three undergraduate years followed by two business years, ending in a master's degree.
4. Harvard, later Stanford: business only at the graduate level, so the student first received an ordinary college education and only afterward entered professional business education.

The institutional architecture therefore looked roughly like:

> liberal/general education → professional business education

rather than simply treating business as an undergraduate major begun immediately upon entering college.

I interpreted Geiger's Harvard discussion as especially revealing. Liberal-arts education was not merely a distribution requirement attached to business training. Harvard imagined management as a profession exercised by broadly cultivated people with institutional responsibilities. Geiger describes the ideal graduates as "gentleman stewards" of business enterprises.

So the conception was closer to:

> become an educated person → acquire professional knowledge of administration → assume responsibility for directing institutions

than to:

> learn accounting + finance + marketing → obtain job

This was part of an effort to make management itself a profession, with social responsibilities and a public role, rather than merely training students in commercially useful techniques. I contrasted this with Geiger's treatment of Wharton, which was much more heavily oriented toward technical specialties such as accounting, insurance, transportation, real estate, advertising, and finance.

A further point from the quick reading was that a Wharton study in the 1920s reportedly found that only about a third of alumni were working in the specialization they had studied, while alumni rated general business and liberal-arts courses more highly than specialized courses. I took Geiger to present this evidence as part of a reform movement toward delaying specialization and teaching general principles first.

My broader interpretation was that this fits one of Geiger's recurring historical arguments: American higher education did not simply replace liberal education with practical education. Instead it repeatedly tried to retain liberal education as a basic collegiate formation while attaching applied and professional education to it, whether during the later undergraduate years or after the bachelor's degree.

The concise formulation I gave was:

> The liberal college supplied the general intellectual and cultural formation; the business school was supposed to supply a subsequent professional formation. The early architects of collegiate business education often regarded the former as a prerequisite or necessary component of the latter—not as an unrelated obstacle students had to get out of the way.

## What later work should test

A proper research pass should at minimum ask:

- Are the four institutional arrangements summarized accurately and completely?
- Exactly what period and institutions does Geiger mean by each arrangement?
- What did the AACSB actually require, in its own contemporary language?
- What source underlies the "gentleman stewards" formulation, and how much of that is Geiger's language versus Harvard's own conception?
- What was the Wharton alumni study, who conducted it, what did it measure, and what conclusions did its authors draw?
- Did Geiger himself frame liberal education as prior "formation" and business education as subsequent professional formation, or is that my synthesis?
- How did this architecture compare with law, medicine, engineering, teacher education, and other professional schools at the same institutions?
- How did later historians of business education interpret the same institutional changes?
