## What Bush is arguing

Bush's object is not an abstract theory of “the economy.” It is an institutional design problem created by the wartime research mobilization: what should the United States keep after the emergency organization is dismantled?

The main chain is:

1. **Basic research creates a stock of new knowledge.** Bush calls this “scientific capital.” New products and processes depend on principles and conceptions that often arise before there is a specific commercial application.
2. **Private and mission agencies will not reliably maintain that stock on their own.** Industrial laboratories are pressured toward products and processes; military organizations have military missions; the country therefore needs an institutional home for research whose immediate use is unknown.
3. **Colleges, universities, and research institutes are the preferred sites for much basic work.** Bush treats their relative freedom from immediate tangible results as a feature.
4. **Research policy is inseparable from a labor/training policy.** A continuing frontier requires a continuing supply of highly trained researchers, so Bush couples research grants to scholarships and fellowships.
5. **Federal money should be stable enough for long-horizon work but should not imply detailed federal direction of individual investigations.** His proposed organization is meant to support inquiry while leaving scientific decisions substantially with researchers and institutions.
6. **The justification is national:** health, employment/industry, national security, and general welfare.

The eventual National Science Foundation did not reproduce Bush's proposed agency exactly, but NSF itself treats this report as a foundational argument for federal support of basic research at colleges and universities.

## Why it matters in Stephan's source trail

Bush supplies the policy baseline against which much of Stephan's later evidence becomes legible. The postwar arrangement expands federal research money and scientific training together. Stephan then asks questions Bush does not answer: how the grant system changes hiring; how graduate students and postdocs become inputs to research production; how scientists respond to renewal risk; whether training expands faster than stable jobs; how priority and prestige affect behavior; and who absorbs the financial risk of research.

So this node should not be treated as “Bush proved public funding works.” It records the institutional proposal that later scholarship measures, criticizes, modifies, or works inside.

## Bibliography / recursion frontier

The 1945 report does **not** end with a conventional scholarly bibliography. Its source structure is institutional. The original volume contains the presidential request and reports of committees that Bush consulted. Those documents are the correct first recursion frontier:

- **Franklin D. Roosevelt → Vannevar Bush, letter of November 17, 1944.** Roosevelt asks four questions about releasing wartime scientific knowledge, continuing medical research, government aid to public/private research, and developing scientific talent.
- **Medical Advisory Committee report.** Chair: **W. W. Palmer**.
- **Committee on Science and the Public Welfare report.** Chair: **Isaiah Bowman**.
- **Committee on Discovery and Development of Scientific Talent report.** Chair: **Henry Allen Moe**.
- **Committee on Publication of Scientific Information report.** Chair: **Irvin Stewart**.
- The appendices also identify the broader groups and people consulted by Bush and the committees; those names should be captured as provenance when this node is expanded.

This is a useful example of why the recursive repository must allow several kinds of edge. A policy report may be built from commissioned committee reports, testimony, letters, statistics, and institutional experience rather than a journal-style reference list.
