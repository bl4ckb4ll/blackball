# Alexandre Grothendieck — *Récoltes et Semailles*: anguish, vanity, and discovery

**Evidence state:** The relevant French passage and its surrounding paragraphs have been inspected directly; the English wording is identified as Roy Lisker's translation. Psychological and institutional extensions below are interpretations or research questions, not established empirical findings.

## Exact location and wording

- **Work:** Alexandre Grothendieck, *Récoltes et Semailles: Réflexions et témoignage sur un passé de mathématicien* (*Harvests and Sowings*; also rendered *Reapings and Sowings*).
- **Section:** “En guise d'avant-propos” (“By way of a preface”), **30 January 1986**.
- **Location:** the paragraph about the first person to discover and master fire, near the end of original manuscript page **A3**, continuing on **A4**; page **28** in the inspected, continuously paginated French PDF.
- **French text (short excerpt):** “la morsure de l’angoisse, et la pommade vaniteuse éprouvée, qui fait oublier la morsure”.
- **Roy Lisker's English phrasing:** “the sting of anguish” and “the poultice of vanity”. The remembered phrase “the politics of vanity” is a mishearing of **poultice**, not a separate political expression.
- **Translation note:** `morsure` is a bite or sting; `pommade` is ointment or salve, not literally a poultice; `vaniteuse` invokes vanity; `éprouvée` means tried or tested. The French emphasizes a familiar, repeatedly used *remedy* which makes the bite easier to forget. Lisker's “anaesthetize” is an interpretive rendering of “fait oublier”.

**Primary text, French:** https://www.ral-m.com/revue/IMG/pdf/grothendieck.pdf#page=28 (PDF page 28, numbered A3/A4 in the original). A second hosted French text: https://www.la-plateforme-stevenson.org/v3/_media/connaissance/comprendremulti/pdf441_grothendieck_recoltes_et_semailles.pdf

**English translation, Roy Lisker:** https://www.fermentmagazine.org/rands/preface (paragraphs on scientific ambition and discovery of fire). The translation identifies itself as begun in December 2002; **1986 dates the underlying preface, not Lisker's translation**.

Do not reproduce an entire copyrighted passage or substitute a modern translation for the original without labeling it.

## Argument in its immediate sequence

1. **The risk of the heroic scientist myth.** Grothendieck says that speaking only of mathematical work and its beauty would produce a flattering but distorted picture of scientists. He explicitly criticizes a Promethean “Myth of Science”: an image of exceptional people moved by pure noble passion.
2. **Ordinary ambition also operates in science.** He argues that scientists may be driven by career ambition and vanity much as people in other professions are. He explicitly refuses to exempt himself. This is his testimony and judgment, **not a measured population estimate**.
3. **Ambition does not itself produce discovery.** A person can be ambitious *and* encounter beauty, but ambition is not what makes the beauty perceptible or proves a mathematical statement. He compares discovery with the involuntary quality of sexual arousal: the French says `faire bander`, rendered much more softly as “score” by Lisker. It is a deliberately bodily analogy, not just a comparison with romantic success.
4. **The discoverer of fire was an ordinary person.** Grothendieck imagines the first human who mastered fire: not a demigod, but someone familiar with fear, anguish, and vanity's soothing disguise. Yet **during the decisive moment of discovery**, he says, neither fear nor vanity occupied the discoverer. This is a philosophical illustration, not a historical claim about a particular prehistoric person.
5. **Heroic myth contains truth and can conceal truth.** The myth captures the fear-free, vanity-free quality Grothendieck attributes to a moment of real discovery; it becomes an intellectual ointment of its own when it hides the discoverer's ordinary fear and self-regard.
6. **He explicitly leaves the psychological problem unresolved.** He says he knows the drive toward understanding better than he knows the relation between fear, vanity, and the subtle obstacles to creativity. He does **not** present a completed theory that explains all vanity.
7. **Two images emerge:** the *enfant* (child, also *ouvrier*, worker) and the *Patron* (Boss). The preface introduces them as images for opposing aspects of the human adventure, with further treatment elsewhere. Do not collapse these symbols into a clinical diagnosis or a one-to-one mapping onto every real worker and boss.

## Interpretation, separated from the author's direct claims

**A useful paraphrase, not Grothendieck's exact formula:** Wanting to *know something* differs from wanting to *be seen as somebody who knows*. The latter may influence commitment to a career, but it cannot replace attention to the object of inquiry.

**The ointment metaphor:** Vanity may supply short-term relief from a feeling one would rather not face. The point is not merely that vanity looks ugly to observers, but that it can be attractive to the person using it. That explanation fits Grothendieck's language, but should not be generalized into “all vanity is caused by anguish.”

**A possible feedback loop to investigate, not an established conclusion:** Seeking recognition might itself create new fears of failure, invisibility, or lost status, leading to still more reliance on self-protective narratives. The preface does not prove such a loop.

## Blackball relevance

This passage matters to Blackball's investigation of **prestige, intellectual vocation, and institutional authority** because it prevents two easy but opposing mistakes:

- Do not infer from great mathematical work that the mathematician is free of ordinary career interest, competition, or vanity.
- Do not infer from the presence of ambition or institutional prestige that the work or its intrinsic intellectual beauty is fraudulent. Grothendieck says those motives can coexist; the causal claim is narrower: ambition alone does not yield discovery.

Potential connections to [Louis Joel Mordell's access to mathematical work](../../../people/louis-joel-mordell.md), [Miles Reid's first-person account of academic career contingencies](../../miles-reid/obituary-notes/README.md), and the [professed-values-versus-conduct question](../../../PROFESSED-VALUES-VERSUS-CONDUCT.md) are **comparative research uses**, not claims made by Grothendieck about those individuals or that document.

The institutional question for Blackball: when a university praises the intrinsic nobility of intellectual work, which part of the narrative accurately describes the experience of doing the work, and which part may also serve to legitimate hierarchy, status, or unequal access to protected time? Answer using specific evidence about an institution; do not treat this literary passage alone as proof.

- Read the later treatment of *l'enfant / l'ouvrier* and *le Patron* in Part I, “Fatuité et renouvellement”; track precise section references before assigning them a fixed psychological function.
- Separate his introspective testimony from his allegations about colleagues and institutions, and inspect those allegations individually rather than importing them wholesale.
- Compare translations where the vocabulary matters: `pommade` (“ointment”) versus “poultice”; `faire bander` versus “score”.
- Ask whether institutional reward systems change what workers pursue, what they publicly say motivates them, or both. This requires independent evidence, not just a quotation.
