# Andrew Bird featuring Fiona Apple — “Left Handed Kisses” (2016)

## Why the song belongs here

Two people sing a love song that questions whether writing a love song is enough. One attempts to declare love without surrendering intellectual skepticism; the other insists that eloquent declarations are not a substitute for risking something real.

**Selection thesis — the listener's own reading, not a song lyric:** “You have to give everything!! Or else it's not worth it!”

The force of that reading is that love calls for an undivided investment rather than keeping one's deepest commitments safely in reserve. The duet stages the demand; it does not supply a definitive list of what “everything” includes.

## What is happening between the two voices?

### A skeptic who has nevertheless fallen in love

Bird rejects the doctrine that events, especially romantic meetings, were destined to happen. Yet that rejection creates his own impossible question: if there is no predestined beloved, how did he come to find this one person among so many others?

That paradox makes him write precisely the kind of uncomplicated love song he is reluctant to write. He is not emotionally empty. His feeling is strong enough to press past his skepticism; his hesitation is over how vulnerable and conventional a declaration he can bear to make.

### A romantic who rejects an evasive declaration

Apple's part is not merely an enthusiastic affirmation of romance. Her character hears the gap between the speaker's clever song and his willingness to put himself at stake.

The accusation is sharper than “write a better love song.” It is: **stop using the love song as a safe place from which to describe devotion without actually risking anything.** Poetic language can express sincerity, but it can also serve as a defense against the consequences of sincerity.

The listener's maxim — *give everything, or it is not worth it* — belongs exactly at this point in the argument. In that reading, a commitment deliberately held back makes even gorgeous words feel like counterfeit payment.

### The second voice is not disposable

The duet would be substantively different if Apple's challenge were merely summarized by a narrator. Bird's character keeps having his own reasons; Apple's character keeps forcing him to confront what those reasons avoid.

Neither an abstract lesson (“always trust love”) nor a sentiment score (“romantic”) preserves their mutual pressure. The song stages two incompatible demands within one intimate encounter: do not pretend to believe what you do not believe; do not hide behind honesty about skepticism to evade the cost of loving.

### Why “left handed” and “backhanded” matter

The title's turn toward “backhanded” changes the emotional value of a seemingly tender gesture. The kiss or song that appears affectionate may also be qualified, indirect, or withholding. The play on words is part of the diagnosis: the declaration itself can betray its speaker's effort to keep a hand free.

## Emotional and dramatic progression

| Movement | Skeptical voice | Challenging voice | Emotional significance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Premise | Denies romantic fate | Finds the denial provocative | Disagreement exists inside attraction |
| Declaration | Wonders at having found the beloved; writes a conventional love refrain | Questions the sincerity or adequacy of this gesture | Speech becomes evidence under examination |
| Accusation | Continues trying to express the remarkable encounter | Demands risk beyond words | Commitment, not verbal ingenuity, becomes the test |
| Return | The mystery of love remains | The charge of evasion remains | Neither voice has been reduced to a platitude |

This is a **listening and reading map**, not a transcription or a claim about exact bar boundaries. A model should retain the voices and their pressure on each other, not just a sequence of isolated adjectives.

## What this teaches a model about love

This entry is useful for a love-song bank precisely because the emotion is *not* reducible to happiness, longing, or heartbreak:

- **Skepticism can coexist with deep attachment.** Denying destiny does not mean denying the beloved's importance.
- **An expression may be beautiful without being sufficient.** Language and action have different costs.
- **Romantic confrontation can be a demand for genuine contact.** The charge of evasion may itself arise from wanting the other person to stop hiding.
- **Risk is central to the emotional claim.** The challenger is measuring exposure and sacrifice, not the length or artistic sophistication of the speech.
- **A declaration can be backhanded.** It may simultaneously offer tenderness and retain an escape route.
- **The two viewpoints are irreducible.** Neither should be overwritten by a generic model of male/female psychology or by invented personal claims about the singers.
- **A challenge is not its resolution.** The lyrics do not establish the couple's later conduct or define a universal threshold of sacrifice.

**Suggested retrieval questions:** “How can an eloquent love declaration avoid vulnerability?” “What kind of risk would show serious love?” “Can a skeptic be deeply romantic?” “When is an argument about love also an act of love?” “What is the difference between saying everything and giving everything?”

**Cross-cutting concepts:** love; risk; devotion; sincerity; guardedness; emotional reciprocity; speech versus action; vulnerability; commitment; the conflict between romance and skepticism.

## Artist's account of composition

Bird described the work as beginning with an **internal dialogue** about why he could not write a simple love song. When an accusing voice emerged, he thought it should become a duet and sought someone able to make the indictment credible. He characterized Apple as fully committed to the role.

In a first-person interview, Bird also explained that he started from a skeptic who did not believe in star-crossed lovers yet found himself writing to the particular person he had met. The song's formal argument therefore belongs to its composition, not merely a later listener's imposition.

## Retrieval boundaries

- **Attribution:** Bird wrote and recorded the song with Fiona Apple as featured vocalist. Do not attribute writing credit to her without separate evidence.
- **Artist intention:** an internal dialogue made into a duet is supported by Bird's own statements.
- **Interpretation:** the contrast between artistic expression and actual risk is a close reading of the song, not a measured fact about the artists' relationships.
- **Selection thesis:** “You have to give everything!! Or else it's not worth it!” is the listener's maxim, explicitly distinct from an actual lyric.
- **Unknown:** no source here establishes what actual conduct would satisfy these fictional voices, whether their relationship succeeds, or whether unconditional personal sacrifice is an appropriate universal rule.
