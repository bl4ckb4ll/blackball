# Finance-sector client-referral hiring: employment exchanged for business

**Record date:** 2026-10-13.  
**Evidence level:** SEC administrative order for JPMorgan (text and pages 2 and 5 inspected); SEC and DOJ releases for Credit Suisse; SEC enforcement summary for Deutsche Bank. Historical enforcement cases, not a representative sample of all finance hiring.

## Claim raised in conversation

> With options trading it's not even in question. Banks just give the job to some kid whose dad will do business with the bank. Business is business, unapologetic money maximizers who laugh if you talk about fairness

This is a criticism about **how firms allocate jobs**, distinct from whether an entrant has intelligence or trading ability. Treat it as a user claim and hypothesis about options-trading jobs, not a measured prevalence estimate or a verbatim quotation of bank personnel.

The mechanism is testable: an employer can prefer a candidate because hiring that candidate is expected to win business from a parent, relative, official, or client. A bank can then be acting in its own revenue interest while dispensing with its publicly described competitive process. It is possible to document this mechanism without pretending that everyone in a trading role entered this way.

## Case 1: JPMorgan, Asia-Pacific (2006–2013)

**Primary source:** SEC, *In the Matter of JPMorgan Chase & Co.*, Exchange Act Release 79335 (Nov. 17, 2016), particularly **printed pages 2, 4–6**, paragraphs 1–3, 12–16: https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2016/34-79335.pdf  
**Agency release:** https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2016-241  
**DOJ release:** https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/jpmorgan-s-investment-bank-hong-kong-agrees-pay-72-million-penalty-corrupt-hiring-scheme

The SEC found:

- Bankers operated a *Sons & Daughters Program* for candidates referred by executives of clients or prospective clients and government officials. Its stated operational purpose was to obtain or retain banking business by granting employment favors (order ¶13).
- Referral hires bypassed ordinary competitive recruitment, typically were less qualified by the bank's stated criteria, and were evaluated against prospective **client revenue**, not the standards applied to non-referral candidates (¶¶2, 12–15).
- The bank hired **approximately 200** interns and full-time employees on these APAC client/government referrals. **Nearly 100** of them were referred by foreign officials at more than 20 Chinese state-owned enterprises (¶3). The shorter SEC press release highlights approximately 100 referrals **from foreign government officials**, not the full approximately 200.
- Transactions with those referring state-owned enterprises produced **more than $100 million in revenues** for JPMorgan or affiliates during the period (¶3). This is associated business revenue, **not** a causal estimate of incremental revenue from all 200 hires.
- In 2010–2011, bank employees recorded the revenue associated with clients whose candidates were hired in spreadsheets (¶2). The SEC release calls these **“Referral Hires vs Revenue”** spreadsheets.
- Officials responsible for the program sometimes hid material facts or gave inaccurate answers in compliance review; the SEC found no referred candidate who reached this review was rejected (¶4).
- JPMorgan agreed to a combined **over $264 million** in sanctions through the SEC, DOJ, and Federal Reserve resolutions. SEC findings were made in an administrative settlement, not a jury verdict. The order records JPMorgan's consent without admitting the factual findings beyond jurisdiction (order p. 1).

This case directly contradicts the claim that all relevant entry-level investment-banking decisions were based solely on skill, credentials, or open competition. It does **not** establish this specific scheme on options-trading desks.

## Case 2: Credit Suisse, Hong Kong (2007–2013)

- SEC release and findings: https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2018-128
- DOJ announcement and account of subsidiary admissions: https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/credit-suisse-s-investment-bank-hong-kong-agrees-pay-47-million-criminal-penalty-corrupt

The SEC reported that Credit Suisse managers offered jobs to **more than 100** people referred by or connected to officials, bypassed ordinary hiring, and used employment to obtain investment-banking business. The DOJ reported that Credit Suisse Hong Kong admitted using such **“relationship hires”** in a quid pro quo and that the scheme generated **at least $46 million in profits**. The SEC settlement was approximately $30 million, with a separate **$47 million** DOJ criminal penalty. Distinguish profits from revenue and job offers from accepted hires.

## Case 3: Deutsche Bank (2006–2014)

- SEC enforcement summary, Aug. 22, 2019: https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/administrative-proceedings/34-86740-s
- Original SEC order: https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2019/34-86740.pdf

The SEC said Deutsche Bank hired relatives of foreign officials in the Asia-Pacific region and Russia to obtain or retain business. The favored candidates bypassed ordinary competitive hiring, were often less qualified, and the bank paid **more than $16 million** to settle the SEC's FCPA charges. This independently documents the mechanism at a second additional institution; it is not a measured industry-wide frequency.

## Inference boundaries

**Established:** In multiple named investment banks, personnel exchanged career opportunities for clients' or officials' business and anticipated revenue; recruitment standards and financial incentives diverged. Employment can be a business inducement and preferential access can coexist with demanding selection processes for outsiders.

**Not established:** That the same hiring mechanism explains the majority of options-trading positions, every bank, all years, or any particular person's rejection. Investment banking is not synonymous with options market making, derivatives trading, proprietary trading, or sales and trading. No denominator of total entry-level hires or controlled measure of nepotism prevalence across those distinct divisions has been supplied.

**Normative contrast:** The phrase “business is business” describes the revenue incentive behind the cases. In the documented FCPA cases it was not an accepted legal defense: enforcement imposed sanctions. Nor does documented wrongdoing prove bank executives literally laughed at fairness objections. The genuine contradiction is **public merit criteria versus private relationship-and-revenue selection**, with costs borne by otherwise competing candidates.

## Blackball question and verification queue

When a bank publicly advertises a highly competitive process, can we establish: (1) which positions went to client-family referrals; (2) the hires' actual division, desk, skill assessments and ordinary comparator pool; (3) the business relationship and internal expected-revenue ledger; (4) whether jobs were additional or displaced others; (5) the effect on qualified applicants without family clients; and (6) whether controls later changed recruitment?

Use case-specific source documents and hiring data if available; **do not silently relabel investment-banking referral cases as options-trading-desk cases**.
