# Paul Klee — *The Diaries of Paul Klee, 1898–1918*

## Status

## Why Blackball wants this source

The diaries are useful for a class-and-freedom distinction that can disappear when education and artistic cultivation are discussed only as privilege.

The remembered passage to recover is about one of Klee's art-school contemporaries whose family had enough money to finance serious artistic training — apparently including movement among schools or places — while still controlling what he was permitted or expected to paint.

The point to preserve is:

**material privilege can expand the range of possible lives while family power still constrains the life actually lived.**

That is different from either of these flattened stories:

- "wealth means freedom";
- "family control means privilege was unreal."

Both can be true at once: somebody may have access to art school, travel, instruction, time, and money that most people do not have, while remaining dependent on parents who attach conditions to that support.

## Nearby verified context

The diary itself repeatedly records Klee's own dependence on family decisions and family money. The English edition opens the Munich period with his parents' wishes having constrained when he could leave school, and later records money arriving from home during his travels.

A contemporary review of the English diaries in the *New York Review of Books* likewise describes Klee as repeatedly running short of money in his early years and waiting for remittances from home, while emphasizing that the family continued to support him.

This nearby material does **not** verify the remembered friend passage. It does show that family financing and artistic autonomy are native concerns of the diary rather than an interpretation imported from nowhere.

Review route:

- Sybille Bedford, "The Good Life of Paul Klee," *New York Review of Books*, 31 December 1964: https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1964/12/31/the-good-life-of-paul-klee/

## Blackball use

Connect this source to the broader question:

> When a family can finance education, art, travel, or scholarship, what exactly has the recipient gained — money, time, status, freedom, or some mixture — and which of those remain controlled by the family?

This matters for the current donor-class / cultivation discussion because elite cultural life should not be described as a simple paradise of unconstrained self-development. Family wealth can make unusual intellectual or artistic lives materially possible while simultaneously giving the family leverage over the recipient's choices.

The relevant contrast is therefore not simply privileged versus unprivileged. It is:

**resources, dependence, control, and freedom are separate variables.**
