## What the document is

This is unusually direct autobiographical evidence. Reid himself titled the file *Notes for obituary*. At the end he explains that the notes were prepared for **biographical memoirs of the Royal Society** and identifies historian of mathematics Jeremy Gray as a possible biographer. The file says it was last modified in July 2002.

It should therefore be read as a self-authored source prepared in anticipation of a later institutional biographical memoir, not as an obituary written after Reid’s death. It is also a dated snapshot: Reid’s current CV contains later appointments and honours that the 2002 notes naturally do not include.

## Summary

### Family and childhood

Miles Anthony Reid was born 30 January 1948 in Hoddesdon, England. His father, John Rollo Reid, was an accountant who spent roughly half his life in France; his mother, Edna Mary Reid (née Frost), was a schoolteacher. Reid was the third of five children.

He grew up in France from about age four to twelve in what he describes as a genteel British middle-class household without a scientific background. He remembers French primary-school grammar instruction as extraordinarily important to him and says the language training remained useful throughout his career.

### School mathematics

After schooling in France he attended Bournemouth School for Boys. He describes himself as disruptive and frequently in conflict with teachers, and says he was impatient to leave school for university.

His mathematical education was partly self-directed. Around age fourteen or fifteen he found his father’s copy of Hall and Knight’s *Higher Algebra* and worked through much of it, including many exercises, for pleasure. After winning a place at Trinity College, Cambridge, he bought books from the Cambridge preparatory reading list and read beyond his formal preparation, including material on Riemannian geometry. He connects some of that interest to Hermann Bondi’s televised lectures on relativity.

### Cambridge undergraduate years

Reid attended Trinity College, Cambridge from 1966 to 1969. He took the second-year Part IB material in his first year. His retrospective account is notably unsentimental about academic success: he says he did not study especially hard or effectively, got through earlier examinations largely on ability, then performed badly in Part III and only barely passed.

He says he probably would not have continued into the Cambridge PhD program without active support from senior mathematicians, naming Alan Baker, Peter Swinnerton-Dyer and W. V. D. Hodge as likely advocates. He singles out Jeffrey Goldstone and Swinnerton-Dyer as especially important intellectual influences because they encouraged independent thought rather than excessive reverence for technical detail.

He also describes Cambridge mathematics at the time as very broad. His undergraduate exposure ranged across Banach-space analysis, electromagnetism, thermodynamics, Galois theory, algebraic topology and differential geometry. He later came to regard applied mathematics—particularly physical ideas such as div, grad, curl and Green functions—as useful motivation for understanding pure mathematics.

### How he entered algebraic geometry

Reid’s description of his route into algebraic geometry is a chain of contingencies rather than a planned trajectory.

When he began postgraduate work at Cambridge in 1969, he was initially supposed to study with Frank Adams. Because Adams had not yet arrived, Reid had considerable freedom. Trying to prepare for work connected with Adams led him toward spectral sequences, Abelian categories and sheaf theory, and from there toward algebraic geometry.

Peter Swinnerton-Dyer advised him to read Mumford’s *Abelian Varieties* (“the little red book”). Reid and Jean-Louis Colliot-Thélène organized seminars on Serre’s *Corps Locaux* and algebraic geometry. Wanting to study in Paris, Reid was introduced to Jean-Pierre Serre, who told him Grothendieck was no longer taking students and advised him to work with Pierre Deligne instead.

Reid went to IHÉS in 1970. Deligne directed him toward Hodge theory and K3 surfaces and proposed the Torelli problem for K3 surfaces. Reid says the problem was much too hard for him at the time. His subsequent path into three-dimensional algebraic geometry is treated more fully in his later autobiographical essay, *25 years of 3-folds — an old person’s view* (in *Explicit Birational Geometry of 3-Folds*, 2000).

A recurrent theme in Reid’s account is the role of chance, personal introductions and senior mathematicians steering him toward viable problems. He explicitly describes the period as a sequence of near-disasters and fortunate interventions.

### Paris, mathematical networks and languages

At IHÉS and in Paris, Reid encountered an unusually dense mathematical network: Deligne’s lectures, the Bourbaki seminar, Serre’s Collège de France courses, and visits or lectures involving figures including Van de Ven, Bombieri and Hirzebruch. He also spent time at the 1971 Warwick symposium organized by Mumford, where he met Mike Artin, C. S. Seshadri, C. P. Ramanujam, Bombieri and others.

He learned German and Russian during evening classes in France. After only about six months of Russian he volunteered to translate a substantial paper by Yuri Manin. Reid says the mathematical content of the translation and detailed editorial corrections from Kurt Hirsch taught him both mathematics and standards of mathematical writing and translation.

### Postdoctoral period and employment

Reid completed his thesis in 1972. Between then and taking a permanent position at Warwick in October 1978, he held a research fellowship at Christ’s College, Cambridge and spent long periods in the Soviet Union, Japan and Erlangen.

For Blackball’s record of academic labor markets, one sentence in this section is particularly worth preserving: Reid says that, among his reasons for wanting to visit Russia, **he had no prospect of employment when he was finishing his PhD**. This is Reid’s own retrospective statement, not labor-market statistics, but it is direct evidence that even a mathematician who became an FRS and a major algebraic geometer experienced the end of the PhD as a period without an obvious job prospect.

### Career and honours as of the 2002 notes

The notes direct the reader to Reid’s CV for appointments. In the obituary file itself he records, among other distinctions, being the first holder of the University of Tokyo endowed chair in Mathematical Theory of Prediction and Control in 1990 and election as a Fellow of the Royal Society in 2002.

The separate CV is the better source for the later chronology. It records his Warwick career beginning in 1978, appointment as professor in 1992, extensive visiting positions, the 2006 LMS Senior Berwick Prize, the 2014 LMS Pólya Prize and the 2023 Royal Society Sylvester Medal.

### Education, science policy and interests

For his views on education and science policy Reid does not try to summarize his position in the obituary notes; instead he points to his “Collected Sermons,” including a 2001 piece on Warwick examinations. The homepage currently links the collection here:

- https://mreid.warwick.ac.uk/Sermons/

He lists broader interests including science and popular science, history of mathematics, current affairs and human rights, languages and cultures of countries in which he worked, and classical and contemporary music. He also describes learning piano relatively late and returning to it after his PhD while his children were growing up.

## Blackball relevance

The source is valuable because it cuts against a polished retrospective “great mathematician” career narrative. Reid’s own account contains several discontinuities:

1. substantial self-teaching before university;
2. weak Part III performance and a near failure to enter doctoral study;
3. senior academics informally helping him remain in the Cambridge pipeline;
4. an accidental route from topology toward algebraic geometry;
5. repeated dependence on introductions, seminars and unusually dense elite mathematical networks;
6. an assigned research problem at IHÉS that he considered far beyond him;
7. an explicit statement that he saw no job prospect as he finished the PhD;
8. six years of fellowships and international visits before permanent employment at Warwick.

Those are autobiographical claims and should not be generalized into population-level statements without separate evidence. They are nevertheless unusually useful first-person evidence about how one exceptionally successful mathematical career actually developed.

## Source limitations

- **Authorship:** first-person retrospective account by Reid himself.
- **Date:** last modified July 2002; it omits later honours and career developments.
- **Purpose:** prepared for a future Royal Society biographical memoir, so it is selective and consciously autobiographical.
- **Evidentiary scope:** strong evidence for what Reid says about his own recollections and career; weak evidence for general claims about Cambridge, mathematics education or academic employment unless independently corroborated.
- **Copyright:** link and summarize; do not mirror the full text without permission.
