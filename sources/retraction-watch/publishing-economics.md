# Who actually receives the money?

**Scope:** Follow each cash flow and the institution making the decision. Do not say vaguely that “academia makes money from papers”: the author, journal owner, university, funder, broker, and book publisher may be different entities with opposing financial interests.

## Business models and cash flow

| Transaction | Payer | Seller / beneficiary | What is actually bought | Evidentiary limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional journal subscription | University/consortium library, hospital, company, some individuals | Journal/publisher (and sometimes learned society) | Licensed access to a journal or portfolio | Revenue is not ordinarily one fee *per manuscript*. Bundle negotiation, usage, title reputation and coverage matter; cannot attribute a particular article's cash without a contract. |
| Read-and-publish / transformative contract | Consortium of libraries, research institution or funder | Publisher | Reading rights + specified open-access publishing entitlements, often for multiple years | Payment can be pooled; cannot infer a fee for one article simply from it appearing OA. |
| APC / open-access publication | Author, university department, grant or funder, sometimes covered by a waiver | Publisher (possibly society) | Processing and publication services for an accepted article | Revenue increases with charged accepted volume if fees held constant; variable costs and waivers prevent treating gross fees as profit. |
| Conventional page or submission charges | Author, institution, grant | Publisher | Page handling or submission/publication services, potentially even in subscription journals | Separate from APCs; hybrid economics and fees can coexist. |
| Purchased authorship/manuscript/peer review | Researcher or intermediary | Paper mill, journal impersonator, broker, corrupt participant | A name on a scholarly work or passage through editorial gate | Sales solicitation does not prove final publication. Check contracts and transaction records. |
| Books and textbooks | Students, libraries, institutions, professional readers | Commercial press, distributor, and sometimes author | Content and established authority or required course material | A professor's course adoption is a conflict-sensitive gate; revenues/royalties differ from journal APCs. Book plausibility is not experimental validation. |
| Citation metrics / indexing / recognition | Often no per-citation payment; journal owner, university and job market may benefit later | Author, journal brand, editor, institution | Prestige, rank, bargaining position; sometimes journal fees increase or openings/promotion result | The conversion into money is indirect and needs records. A highly cited paper does not demonstrate financial value or scientific truth. |
| Salary, grant, tenure and promotion | Employer, agency, taxpayers, donors | Credentialed researcher/institution | Research employment and promised knowledge production | Incentives often depend on publication counts, prestige, grants and reputation, but actual rules vary: inspect local promotion/salary decisions. |

## Direct, contemporary accounts

**Wiley's own 2026 SEC Form 10-K** identifies research-publishing revenue as: journal subscriptions and read-and-publish agreements, OA pay-to-publish, licensing and ancillary products. It reports selling subscriptions directly to thousands of research institutions through negotiated contracts. Its stated growth strategy includes publishing more articles and increasing volume-based OA revenue.

Source: [Wiley fiscal 2026 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/107140/000162828026045111/jwa-20260430.htm), sections “Research,” “Research Publishing,” “Journal Subscriptions and Transformational Agreements.” Its fiscal 2025 Form 10-K also says 64% of consolidated revenue was Research and that segment reported a 32.1% adjusted EBITDA margin; **Research includes solutions, not only research journals**. [2025 SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/107140/000010714025000081/jwa-20250430.htm).

**RELX/Elsevier's 2025 annual financial accounts** report £2.714bn in Scientific, Technical & Medical revenue and **80% subscription / 20% transactional** within that segment. STM also includes databases, decision tools and reference products; **80% is not the percentage paid by university libraries, nor exclusively journal subscription money**. They explicitly name libraries, clinicians, corporations and universities among buyers and explain both pay-to-read and pay-to-publish. [RELX SEC-filed annual report, market segments](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/929869/000110465926017277/relx-20251231xex15d2.htm).

**The Wiley/Hindawi publishing pause provides a cleaner marginal-revenue observation.** Retraction Watch reported that Wiley attributed about **$9 million of lost quarterly revenue** (relative to the comparable quarter a year earlier) to a pause in Hindawi's special issues as it addressed manipulated or compromised research. This is evidence the throughput of a journal publishing operation affects sales, *not* evidence that the whole $9m would have been fraud proceeds or net profit. [Retraction Watch, 2023](https://retractionwatch.com/2023/03/09/wiley-paused-hindawi-special-issues-amid-quality-problems-lost-9-million-in-revenue/).

**Concrete small-journal arithmetic:** Retraction Watch linked historic page charges and publication years to 1,610 retractions at *Journal of Intelligent and Fuzzy Systems*, reporting a fee-derived sum of **$427,850** from articles later retracted, with unknown fees for early years and estimates for others. The paper's owner was IOS Press before Sage acquired it in November 2023; **Sage said most fees came before acquisition**. The sum is modeled gross receipts for a particular set of articles, not an audited profit statement. [Retraction Watch, 2025](https://retractionwatch.com/2025/05/07/journal-intelligent-fuzzy-systems-jifs-sage-retracted-papers-revenue/).

**A publisher/editor power dispute with observable constraints:** *Journal of Geometric Mechanics* editors resigned when the publisher sought greater output, author-paid open access and more special issues. Publisher Shouchuan Hu disputed their characterization and disclosed the journal had fewer than ten subscriptions and roughly 20 articles per year, arguing the old model was financially untenable. That conflict means “greed” and “guardianship” cannot substitute for investigating **actual editorial decisions and costs**; the subscription shortage and attempted change are both real parts of the account. [Retraction Watch, 2023](https://retractionwatch.com/2023/09/28/after-resigning-en-masse-math-journal-editors-launch-new-publication/).

**Citation→fee→registration chain in medicine:** Retraction Watch's 2026 investigation of surgical journals found a high-impact citation flow to editor-authored guidelines, editorial instructions specifying guideline citations, article charges of $3,900 for a flagship journal, and an affiliated paid Research Registry. It calculated specific 2022 guideline citations contributing **3.4 impact-factor points**. A criticism that reporting standards are useless does *not* follow; the audit question is whether requirements were imposed despite irrelevant content and financial conflicts, and how rankings/fees changed. [Retraction Watch, March 2026](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/03/12/riaz-agha-international-journal-surgery-research-registry-wolters-kluwer/).

**Barrier to entry for less-funded authors:** The mathematics/statistics editorial resignations over roughly $3,000 APCs and economics-journal editors' argument over dependence on publisher royalties make the class question concrete. A rich institution can absorb charges that an unaffiliated researcher cannot. [Statistics and Computing, July 2026](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/07/09/the-exploitation-still-remains-stats-journal-associate-editors-resign-over-3000-publishing-charge/); [Review of Social Economy, July 2026](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/07/23/editors-of-economics-journal-resign-after-association-renews-taylor-francis-contract/). The latter journal's association explicitly disputed the resigning editors' expectations about future OA policy; retain that dispute.

## How plausible journals get library payments

A librarian need not literally have been fooled by a plausible title. Institutions acquire thousands of articles and journals through portfolios and read-and-publish contracts negotiated under budget, research-access, faculty-demand and disciplinary-coverage constraints. A particular journal may be included in a larger bundle. A journal's rank and reputation can influence the perceived indispensability of access; this requires evidence from actual contracts, not conjecture about librarian gullibility.

Another scam *does* exploit plausible names directly: websites and organizations impersonating established publishers to solicit fees or authorships. This is not the same transaction as a university library subscribing to a legitimate publisher's portfolio. [Fake “Springer” solicitation, Oct. 2026](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/10/12/springer-journals-online-authorship-for-sale/).

## Institutional economic hypotheses to investigate

1. Do salaries, funding decisions and promotion criteria actually reward more papers, higher ranks or citation counts? Collect the *written rule* **and** real decisions; compare otherwise similar scholars.
2. Do a university's subscriptions and read-and-publish deals create costs not borne by the individual professor advocating those subscriptions? Track library budget → contracts → publisher remittances → access/publishing.
3. Are peer reviewers/editorial workers paid, and by whom? Record contracted labor, uncompensated work, workload, and who profits; do not assume no editor anywhere is paid.
4. Who can demand citations, set an impact factor, certify a credential, decide a retraction or prevent criticism from appearing alongside a paper? The actor controlling the gate often isn't the one doing the research.
5. Can a professor turn a method into a paid textbook, certification, consulting product, proprietary strategy or seminar? Follow actual customers and verifiable benefits, including failures and opportunity costs.
6. Can a financially vulnerable scholar buy an authorship slot to enter the promotion process? Look at the actual net return after cost, risk, falsity and possible sanctions—not only the existence of a market.
7. Can independent researchers publish and be heard without institutional affiliation or a large APC budget? Record case-specific rejections, waivers and opportunities, rather than calling exclusion automatic.
8. What incentives does the publisher have to correct its own products? Compare customer loss, legal risks, fees already taken, investigating costs, retraction delays and treatment of critics.

**Research discipline:** absence of an audited cash flow is an open question, not a license to invent it. The point is to test whether apparently expert institutions were selling *a right to read, an opportunity to publish, or a credible-looking stamp*, and how their quality incentives aligned or conflicted with their business.
