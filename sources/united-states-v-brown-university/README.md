# United States v. Brown University — Ivy/MIT financial-aid antitrust case

## Scope

This dossier follows the federal antitrust case over the **Ivy Overlap Group**, in which MIT and the eight Ivy League institutions coordinated parts of their need-based financial-aid systems and reconciled the expected family contributions of students admitted to more than one participating school.

The point here is to keep four things separate:

1. what the schools actually agreed to do;
2. what the Department of Justice alleged and argued;
3. what MIT argued in response; and
4. what the district court, the Third Circuit majority, and the Third Circuit dissent actually decided.

The legal record is especially important because a shorthand such as “the colleges were convicted of price fixing” is wrong. This was a **civil Sherman Act case**. The eight Ivy defendants settled by consent decree; MIT litigated. MIT lost after a bench trial, obtained reversal and remand on appeal, and then settled before the remanded merits analysis occurred.

- **United States v. Brown University**, Civil Action No. 91-3274, U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania.

## What the Overlap Group did

MIT and the eight Ivy League schools — Brown, Columbia, Cornell, Dartmouth, Harvard, Princeton, Pennsylvania, and Yale — participated in the Ivy Overlap Group.

The district court found that the schools:

- agreed to award institutional financial aid on the basis of need rather than merit;
- developed and used common principles for measuring financial need;
- exchanged information about financial-aid applicants;
- met in the spring to compare the expected family contributions calculated for students admitted to multiple participating schools; and
- adjusted discrepant figures so that a family would generally be asked to contribute approximately the same amount whichever participating institution the student chose.

The schools therefore did not merely happen to use similar formulas independently. The case concerned **actual inter-institution coordination**, including student-specific reconciliation.

The district court found that an objective of the process was to remove price competition among participating institutions for aided common admits. MIT did not deny the coordination; it disputed the government's characterization and legal consequences of it.

## Chronology

- **1958:** the Ivy institutions and MIT formed the Ivy Overlap Group.
- **1989:** DOJ began the investigation that the district court later described as a two-year investigation.
- **May 1991:** DOJ filed its Sherman Act § 1 complaint.
- **May 22, 1991:** the eight Ivy defendants entered a consent settlement. MIT did not.
- **June–July 1992:** MIT and DOJ tried the case to the court without a jury.
- **Sept. 2, 1992:** Chief Judge Bechtle ruled for the United States.
- **Sept. 17, 1993:** the Third Circuit reversed and remanded. The two-judge majority required a fuller rule-of-reason inquiry; Judge Weis agreed that MIT should not lose but would have gone further and entered judgment for MIT because he thought the antitrust laws did not apply to this student-aid activity.
- **Dec. 22, 1993:** DOJ and MIT settled before a remand trial resolved the full rule-of-reason merits.

## The government's argument

Read the government's own trial brief rather than relying on a retrospective paraphrase:

**Government's Trial Brief:**  
https://appliedantitrust.com/07_reasonableness/brown/brown_edpa/doj_trial_brief6_22_1992.pdf

Its table of contents states the theory unusually clearly. DOJ argued that:

1. the Ivy-MIT Overlap Group was an agreement or conspiracy within Sherman Act § 1;
2. fixing expected family contributions and banning merit scholarships constituted horizontal price fixing;
3. the arrangement should therefore be condemned per se, or alternatively failed even under a rule-of-reason analysis;
4. the arrangement caused anticompetitive effects by suppressing financial competition;
5. financial aid and tuition were part of interstate commercial activity;
6. nonprofit status did not immunize MIT; and
7. MIT's educational and social-policy justifications could not legally rescue an agreement that eliminated price competition.

The government's concrete price theory was that aid functions as a discount from the price of educational services. If competing schools jointly align the family contribution, they are coordinating a component of the net price paid by the family.

The government's post-trial proposed findings are here:

https://appliedantitrust.com/07_reasonableness/brown/brown_edpa/doj_post_trial_findings7_23_1992.pdf

That document should be used when a question turns on what DOJ asked the trial judge to find as fact after hearing the evidence.

## MIT's argument

Read MIT's own post-trial filing:

**MIT's Post-Trial Memorandum:**  
https://appliedantitrust.com/07_reasonableness/brown/brown_edpa/mit_post_trial_brief7_24_1992.pdf

MIT did not principally argue that the schools had never coordinated. Its defense attacked the government's legal and economic characterization of the coordination.

MIT argued that:

1. need-based institutional aid was a charitable, educational activity rather than ordinary commercial price setting;
2. the program allocated scarce privately donated aid to students who actually needed it;
3. coordination improved the accuracy and consistency of need analysis;
4. the arrangement helped institutions sustain **need-blind admission** and a commitment to meet admitted students' full demonstrated need;
5. it widened access for needy students and therefore increased consumer choice;
6. socioeconomic diversity improved the educational product;
7. the arrangement did not increase participating schools' aggregate revenues — MIT characterized it as revenue-neutral; and
8. even if the Sherman Act applied, those asserted benefits required a full rule-of-reason analysis rather than automatic or abbreviated condemnation.

MIT also argued that ending Overlap could redirect scarce aid toward bidding for especially desirable applicants who did not need the extra subsidy, thereby reducing the number of needy students who could be funded.

A contemporaneous MIT statement gives the institution's public presentation of essentially the same defense:

https://news.mit.edu/1992/overlap-0506

Treat that statement as MIT advocacy; use the filed memorandum for the legal argument.

## District court: Judge Bechtle's reasoning

**Decision:**  
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/805/288/2593152/

Judge Bechtle rejected a blanket “charity” or nonprofit exemption. His basic reasoning was that universities sell educational services for money and that financial aid changes the amount a student and family actually pay. Coordinating the expected family contribution therefore affected a commercial price even though the institutions were nonprofit and even if their broader educational purposes were charitable.

He did **not** simply apply the ordinary per se rule to end the case immediately. Because the restraint operated in a nonprofit/professional/educational setting, he gave MIT an opportunity to justify it under an abbreviated rule-of-reason inquiry.

### What he found anticompetitive

The court found “abundant and uncontroverted evidence” that a fundamental objective of Overlap was eliminating price competition among members. It pointed specifically to:

- the common needs methodology;
- exchanged budget and self-help information;
- the agreement not to award merit scholarships; and
- the annual comparison and adjustment of family contributions for common admits.

The judge reasoned that whether Overlap raised aggregate tuition revenue was not decisive. Even accepting MIT's claim of revenue neutrality, an agreement intentionally removing price as a dimension of competition was itself competitive harm requiring justification.

### Why he rejected MIT's justification

MIT argued that the system helped preserve need-blind admission, full-need aid, access, and socioeconomic diversity. Judge Bechtle treated those claimed social benefits as insufficient to justify competitors jointly eliminating price competition. He relied heavily on Supreme Court cases such as *Professional Engineers* and *Indiana Federation of Dentists*, which reject defenses premised on the proposition that competition itself would produce socially undesirable decisions.

His final institutional point was explicit: Congress could choose to authorize such cooperation as a matter of national education policy, but absent that authorization the court was bound by the antitrust law enacted by Congress.

### District-court conclusion

The court entered judgment for the United States and enjoined MIT's participation in the challenged practices.

That was **not the last judicial word**.

## Third Circuit majority: Judge Cowen's reasoning

**Opinion:**  
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/5/658/626013/

The Third Circuit majority agreed with two important pieces of the district court's analysis:

1. financial aid was sufficiently connected to the price of education to implicate “trade or commerce” under Sherman Act § 1; and
2. Overlap had facially anticompetitive characteristics because it restrained financial bidding for common admits.

But the majority rejected both DOJ's request for per se condemnation and the district court's abbreviated treatment of MIT's defenses.

### Why the majority required a full rule of reason

Judge Cowen emphasized the nonprofit educational context and the uncertainty of the economic evidence. The district court had assumed for purposes of decision that the arrangement was revenue-neutral and had not made a finding of higher aggregate prices or lower output.

The majority held that MIT had identified claims that, if factually supported, were not merely “competition is bad” arguments:

- **quality:** socioeconomic diversity could improve the educational product;
- **consumer choice/access:** concentrating aid on need could allow more financially needy students to choose an Overlap institution; and
- **social welfare intertwined with competition:** equality of educational access could not simply be discarded without determining how the restraint actually operated.

The court rejected one MIT theory: merely shifting competition away from price and toward curriculum, campus life, or other non-price attributes is **not itself** a procompetitive virtue. Any cartel could say that suppressing one form of competition leaves other forms.

But the majority held that the access, choice, and quality theories warranted actual factual evaluation. If MIT established a persuasive procompetitive justification, DOJ would then have to show that a reasonable **less restrictive alternative** could achieve the same benefits.

### Majority conclusion

The majority did **not** hold that Overlap was lawful.

It held that the district court used too abbreviated an analysis and therefore:

- **reversed** the judgment; and
- **remanded** for a full-scale rule-of-reason analysis.

That distinction is important. “MIT won the appeal” is true procedurally; “the Third Circuit held that the Overlap system complied with the Sherman Act” is not.

## Judge Weis's dissent

Judge **Joseph F. Weis Jr.** agreed that the judgment against MIT should not stand, but for a different and broader reason.

He would have held that, in these circumstances, the student-aid activity was outside the commercial domain to which the Sherman Act was meant to apply. He emphasized the charitable character of the aid program, the absence of an ordinary profit-seeking exchange in the grant of aid, and judicial caution about extending antitrust doctrine into educational activity.

Unlike the majority, he would not merely have remanded for a fuller rule-of-reason inquiry. His conclusion was that judgment should be entered **for MIT**.

The searchable opinion contains both Judge Cowen's opinion and Judge Weis's dissent:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/5/658/626013/

## Settlement: what was and was not resolved

The parties settled in December 1993 rather than returning to district court for the full rule-of-reason trial the Third Circuit ordered.

Settlement letter:

https://appliedantitrust.com/07_reasonableness/brown/litan_thane_settlement12_22_1993.pdf

MIT's contemporaneous description:

https://news.mit.edu/1994/settlement-0105

The settlement allowed defined forms of cooperation among qualifying nonprofit institutions, including common principles of need analysis, while barring the old practice of discussing or agreeing on the individual family contribution or aid mix for a particular applicant.

Because the case settled, the courts never produced a final merits judgment answering whether the complete Overlap system would survive the **full** rule-of-reason analysis required by the Third Circuit.

## What later answers should say carefully

Safe compressed description:

> DOJ challenged the Ivy League/MIT Overlap financial-aid system as horizontal price coordination. The eight Ivies settled. MIT litigated and lost in district court, but the Third Circuit reversed because the district court had not fully evaluated MIT's asserted access, consumer-choice, educational-quality, and social-welfare justifications under the rule of reason. The appellate court did not declare the system lawful; the case settled before that full merits analysis occurred.

Avoid these shortcuts:

- **“The colleges were convicted.”** Wrong: civil case, not a criminal conviction.
- **“The Third Circuit cleared MIT of price fixing.”** Too strong: it reversed and remanded for fuller analysis.
- **“MIT denied coordinating aid.”** Misleading: the dispute was centrally about the legal and economic meaning of acknowledged coordination.
- **“The court held social welfare overrides antitrust.”** Wrong: the majority required those arguments to be considered in the particular higher-education rule-of-reason analysis; it did not announce a general social-welfare exemption.
