# Charles Booth — *Life and Labour of the People in London*

## Why this source is here

Charles Booth gives Blackball a genuinely contemporary economic control for late-Victorian London.

Instead of projecting present-day tuition, salaries, or class categories backward onto Sherlock Holmes, Booth and his research team actually investigated London households, occupations, wages, working conditions, housing, poverty, and social position between 1886 and 1903.

The result was *Life and Labour of the People in London*, ultimately published in 17 volumes.

## Booth's own material position

Booth was not an outside observer with no economic stake or resources.

LSE's Booth archive records that:

- his father was a merchant;
- after his father's death Booth used inherited money;
- he joined his brother Alfred in a commission business dealing principally in skins and leather;
- the family firm operated in Liverpool and New York;
- Booth also ran steam-shipping interests;
- he later moved the merchandising arm of Alfred Booth & Company to London;
- he devised, organized, and substantially funded the London inquiry himself.

That background belongs beside the survey. A person with inherited capital, an international leather business, and shipping interests had a different margin for unpaid investigation than the households being investigated.

This does not invalidate the survey. It tells us who could afford to produce it and what social position the investigator occupied.

## The A–H classification

Booth divided the population into eight classes.

In the final London summary, the groupings were:

- **A** — lowest;
- **B** — very poor;
- **C and D** — poor;
- **E and F** — working class in comfortable circumstances;
- **G and H** — middle class and above.

For all London, Booth reported approximately:

| Group | People | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | 37,610 | 0.9% |
| B | 316,834 | 7.5% |
| C + D | 938,293 | 22.3% |
| E + F | 2,166,503 | 51.5% |
| G + H | 749,930 | 17.8% |

Booth grouped A–D as **30.7% in poverty** and E–H as **69.3% in comfort** in that summary.

The categories were not merely a modern notion of “class.” They drew on earnings, regularity of employment, occupation, household circumstances, and status. The underlying notebooks and maps preserve much more detail than the compressed A–H letters.

## Why this matters for Watson

Watson's situation in *A Study in Scarlet* should not be translated into a fake modern price tag.

The better historical questions are:

- What income did a medically trained former Army doctor actually have?
- Was it regular?
- What housing could it support?
- Was he living alone or supporting a family?
- What assets or family support did he have?
- How did his resources compare with Booth's poor, comfortable working-class, and middle-class London households?
- How long could someone in that position remain outside civilian medical practice?

Watson states that he had eleven shillings and sixpence per day, initially stayed at a private hotel in the Strand, spent beyond his means, and then sought cheaper shared rooms.

Those facts can be put beside Booth's London without pretending the categories line up perfectly by date or household type.

## Why this matters for Victor Hatherley

Hatherley is an even cleaner asset-versus-income case.

He has:

- seven years of technical apprenticeship;
- inherited money from his father;
- enough capital to establish an independent engineering office;
- almost no demand for his services during the next two years.

A current earnings measure alone would make him look poor. An asset/inheritance measure changes the picture. Booth's broader social classification makes that distinction easier to see.

## Method warning

Do not use A–H as if Booth had discovered eight natural kinds of human beings.

The categories were constructed for a particular investigation, with judgment calls, information gathered by investigators and School Board visitors, and explicit contemporary assumptions about respectability, employment, household structure, and poverty.

Use them as historical measurement, then inspect how the measurement was made.
