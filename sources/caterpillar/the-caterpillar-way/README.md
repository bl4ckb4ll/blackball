## Why the book gets its own source node

This book belongs in the hopper even when it is not on a syllabus.

The existing Koch dossier records that the authors' work included substantial access to Caterpillar executives, managers, dealers, customers, labor figures, and analysts. That access can make the book valuable. It can also shape which questions are easy to answer.

Treat it as a corporate strategy/history source whose claims should be decomposed.

For any Caterpillar episode record:

- who is being credited;
- whether the claim concerns engineering, management, finance, manufacturing, labor, dealers, or customers;
- whether evidence comes from an interview, company document, public filing, union source, outside analyst, or author interpretation;
- what independent source can control the claim;
- who bears the downside of the decision described.

## Engineering-career use

The book is especially useful for testing the familiar Michigan/industrial narrative that engineers once ran industrial companies and were later displaced by “money people.”

Do not decide that story from nostalgia or from one management book.

Build a chronology of:

- CEO and senior-leadership educational/occupational backgrounds;
- authority of engineering, finance, sales, operations, and manufacturing functions;
- product-development organization;
- capital allocation and ownership pressure;
- labor relations;
- changes in hiring and promotion routes for technical staff.

The question is historical and organizational, not “engineers good / finance bad.”
