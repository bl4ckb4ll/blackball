# Paula E. Stephan — public source map behind *How Economics Shapes Science*

This is a reconstruction of the intellectual/source base behind Paula E. Stephan's 2012 book, not a transcription of the copyrighted 2012 bibliography.

## Provenance and confidence

The strongest public source is Stephan's chapter **“The Economics of Science”** in *Handbook of the Economics of Innovation*, vol. 1 (2010), pp. 217–273. Its references run through the end of the chapter, and Stephan explicitly says the chapter **draws extensively from Stephan (1996)**. The chain is therefore documented:

**Stephan 1996 JEL survey → Stephan 2010 handbook chapter → Stephan 2012 book.**

A 2012 review of the finished book independently says that its opening account of puzzles and recognition draws on **Thomas Kuhn, Robert K. Merton, Warren Hagstrom, and Derek de Solla Price**, and that only about **13% of the book's references are economics literature**. Sociology, management, education, science studies, policy reports, and other literatures matter heavily.

Labels below:

- **BOOK-CONFIRMED** — independently identified as used in the 2012 book.
- **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR** — directly cited in Stephan's public 2010 chapter.
- **2012-COMPANION** — directly used in Stephan's 2012 *Nature* essay “Perverse Incentives,” published alongside the book.

## 1. What motivates scientists: puzzles, priority, recognition, reputation

- **Thomas S. Kuhn** — **BOOK-CONFIRMED**. Historical structure of scientific problem solving and paradigms.
- **Robert K. Merton** — **BOOK-CONFIRMED / PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Stephan repeatedly relies on his work on priority, recognition, multiples in discovery, and the Matthew effect. Her 2010 chapter says economists owe a substantial debt to Merton for establishing the importance of priority.
- **Warren O. Hagstrom, *The Scientific Community* (1965)** — **BOOK-CONFIRMED / PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Science as puzzle solving and as a community in which recognition, exchange, sharing, citations, and coauthorship matter.
- **Derek J. de Solla Price, *Little Science, Big Science … and Beyond*** — **BOOK-CONFIRMED / PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Growth, scale, publication, and organization of scientific work.
- **David L. Hull, *Science as a Process* (1988)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Scientific behavior, curiosity, competition, and social process.
- **Richard Feynman, *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out*** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Stephan uses Feynman as direct evidence that discovery itself can be a reward.
- **Jonathan R. Cole and Stephen Cole, *Social Stratification in Science* (1973)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Unequal distributions of recognition, resources, and careers.
- **Allison & Stewart (1974)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Cumulative advantage and productivity differences among scientists.
- **Harriet Zuckerman (1992)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Proliferation of prizes and the scientific reward system.
- **John Ziman, *Public Knowledge* (1968)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Public communication and communal evaluation as constitutive features of science.
- **Michael Polanyi, “The Republic of Science” (1962)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Coordination and autonomy in scientific work.

## 2. Why scientific knowledge is difficult to treat as ordinary property

- **Kenneth Arrow (1962)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Information, uncertainty, disclosure, and the case for public support of invention/research.
- **Richard R. Nelson (1959)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Why private firms will tend to underfund basic research when benefits escape the funder.
- **Partha Dasgupta & Paul David (1987, 1994)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. They join the public-good problem to scientific priority, openness, and disclosure; Stephan uses their treatment of priority as a kind of property right.
- **Partha Dasgupta & Eric Maskin (1987)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Parallel research portfolios and duplication under uncertainty.
- **Michel Callon (1994)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Directly questions the simple claim that science is a public good.
- **Dominique Foray, *The Economics of Knowledge* (2004)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Knowledge production, transmission, and appropriation.

## 3. Scientists as workers: labor markets, training, postdocs, and bad shortage forecasts

- **David Blank & George Stigler, *The Demand and Supply of Scientific Personnel* (1957)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Stephan says this and Arrow–Capron “set the stage” for later scientific-labor-market work.
- **Kenneth Arrow & William Capron (1959)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Dynamic shortages and price adjustment in the engineer/scientist market.
- **Richard Freeman (1975)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Science manpower, salaries, enrollment, and delayed supply responses.
- **Ronald Ehrenberg (1991, 1992)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Academic labor supply and doctorate flows.
- **Ehrenberg, Rizzo & Jakubson (2003)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Who bears the growing cost of university science.
- **Ehrenberg & Zhang (2005)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Changing faculty employment structure.
- **Mary Frank Fox & Paula Stephan (2001)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. What young scientists want from careers versus the jobs they expect to obtain.
- **Scott Stern (2004)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Evidence that scientists accept lower pay for jobs that let them publish and participate in scientific reputation systems.
- **National Research Council, *Forecasting Demand and Supply of Doctoral Scientists and Engineers* (2000)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Stephan calls this report “mandatory reading” for anyone attempting scientist-supply forecasts; it analyzes misspecified models, bad data, lags, and unpredictable shocks.
- **National Academies (2005), National Science Board (2003/2004/2006), NSF workforce and doctorate reports** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Empirical backbone for training, migration, employment, and workforce claims.
- **Leslie & Oaxaca (1993)** and **Bowen & Sosa (1989)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Scientist/engineer supply-demand and faculty-market forecasting.

## 4. Why research moved toward teams and larger collaborations

- **James Adams, Grant Black, Roger Clemmons & Paula Stephan (2005)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Growth of scientific teams and inter-institutional collaboration in U.S. universities.
- **Benjamin Jones (2005)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. “Burden of knowledge”: as the frontier requires more accumulated knowledge, researchers narrow expertise and rely more heavily on teams.
- **Stefan Wuchty, Benjamin Jones & Brian Uzzi (2007)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Increasing dominance of teams in scientific knowledge production.
- **Ajay Agrawal & Avi Goldfarb (2008)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Falling communication costs and collaboration across institutions.
- **Cockburn & Henderson (1998)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Research organization, coauthorship, and absorptive capacity in pharmaceutical discovery.
- **Frank Andrews, *Scientific Productivity* (1979)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Comparative study of research groups.

## 5. Patents, ownership, research materials, and commercialization

- **Rebecca Eisenberg (1987)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Proprietary rights versus scientific norms in biotechnology.
- **Michael Heller & Rebecca Eisenberg (1998)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. The biomedical “anticommons” hypothesis: fragmented upstream rights may impede downstream research.
- **John Walsh, Charlene Cho & Wesley Cohen (2005, 2007)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Empirical work on patents and, importantly, access to physical research materials. Stephan uses this to distinguish patent barriers from material-transfer barriers.
- **Fiona Murray (2006, 2007)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Oncomouse, stem-cell patents, and accommodation/resistance to property claims in academic science.
- **Pierre Azoulay, Waverly Ding & Toby Stuart (2009)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. How academic patenting changes publication output, quality, and research direction.
- **Saul Lach & Mark Schankerman (2008)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. University royalty-sharing incentives and invention/licensing.
- **Jerry Thursby & Marie Thursby, with collaborators (2005–2009)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Licensing, faculty research behavior, and university patenting.
- **Agrawal & Henderson (2002)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Patents as only one channel of knowledge transfer from a university such as MIT.
- **Jaffe & Lerner, *Innovation and Its Discontents* (2004)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Patent-system institutions and innovation.

## 6. Public research, firms, technological change, and spillovers

- **Vannevar Bush, *Science—The Endless Frontier* (1945)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. The postwar institutional case for federal support of basic research and training.
- **Nathan Rosenberg (1974, 1982, 1990)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Science–technology feedback, industrial basic research, and technological change; a major recurring influence on Stephan.
- **Zvi Griliches (1979)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Measuring R&D's contribution to productivity.
- **Edwin Mansfield (1991, 1995)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Direct empirical work on academic research underlying industrial innovation.
- **Adam Jaffe (1989)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Real effects of academic research.
- **Jaffe, Trajtenberg & Henderson (1993)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Geographic localization of knowledge spillovers using patent citations.
- **Wesley Cohen, Richard Nelson & John Walsh (2002)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. How public research reaches industrial R&D through multiple channels, not patents alone.
- **David Mowery & Nathan Rosenberg, *Paths of Innovation* (1998)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Historical development of technological change in the United States.
- **Giovanni Dosi-style evolutionary work enters indirectly through Richard Nelson & Sidney Winter, *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change* (1982)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Stephan explicitly suggests using evolutionary models to understand scientific careers and selection.
- **Paul Romer (1994)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Endogenous technological change and growth.
- **Schmookler (1966)** and **F. M. Scherer** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Demand, invention, uncertainty, and R&D timing.

## 7. Gender, inequality, age, and cumulative advantage

- **Mary Frank Fox (1983, 1991, 2005)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Productivity, gender, family, and scientific environments.
- **Yu Xie & Kimberlee Shauman (1998; 2003, *Women in Science*)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Gender differences in scientific careers and productivity.
- **Waverly Ding, Fiona Murray & Toby Stuart (2006)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Gender differences in patenting and commercial science.
- **Sharon Levin & Paula Stephan (1991)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Research productivity over the life cycle.
- **Benjamin Jones (2005)** and **Hall, Mairesse & Turner (2007)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Age/cohort effects and the age at which important work is produced.
- **Lotka (1926)** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Highly skewed distributions of scientific productivity.
- **Merton / Allison–Stewart** — **PUBLIC-PRECURSOR**. Cumulative advantage and the Matthew effect.

## 8. Funding design, risk, and perverse incentives

The public 2010 chapter ends by asking directly how funding changes outcomes, whether grant systems encourage excessive reliance on graduate students and postdocs, and whether risk-averse funding agencies deter risky research.

A contemporaneous 2012 companion article makes the bridge to the book especially visible:

- **Paula Stephan, “Perverse Incentives” (Nature, 2012)** — **2012-COMPANION**.
- **Pierre Azoulay, Joshua Graff Zivin & Gustavo Manso, work comparing HHMI-style investigator funding with conventional project funding** — **2012-COMPANION**. Long horizons and tolerance for early failure are used to study whether funding design affects novelty and high-impact research.
- **Chiara Franzoni, Giuseppe Scellato & Paula Stephan (2011)** — **2012-COMPANION**. International mobility and scientific careers.
- **Mallon & Korn (2004)** plus U.S. workforce statistics and NIH/NIGMS grant-output evidence — **2012-COMPANION**. These support Stephan's critique of grant dependence and the structure of academic research employment.

## A short “read these first” chain

If the goal is to understand where Stephan's conclusions actually came from rather than to read every reference, the highest-yield upstream chain is:

**Merton → Hagstrom → Price → Ziman → Arrow → Nelson → Dasgupta & David → Blank & Stigler → Arrow & Capron → Freeman → NRC 2000 → Fox & Stephan → Stern → Jones → Adams/Black/Clemmons/Stephan → Wuchty/Jones/Uzzi → Eisenberg → Heller & Eisenberg → Walsh/Cho/Cohen → Murray → Rosenberg → Mansfield → Jaffe → Cohen/Nelson/Walsh → Azoulay/Graff Zivin/Manso.**

That chain makes the book look much less like a product of one academic department. It is a synthesis of sociology of science, science studies, labor-market evidence, university policy, patent law, histories of technological change, bibliometrics, and empirical studies of laboratories and scientific careers.
