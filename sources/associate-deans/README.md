# Associate Deans (@ass_deans)

## Why this belongs in Blackball

`@ass_deans` is an **insider-view corpus about university management**.

The important contrast is not “academics versus universities.” A more useful working model is:

**faculty and other academic workers produce much of the teaching, research, and disciplinary substance → managerial layers allocate resources, impose procedures, define priorities, measure performance, and increasingly mediate the institution**

The account's jokes repeatedly target associate deans, vice provosts, committees, strategic plans, assessment systems, branding language, restructuring, faculty governance, adjunctification, and administrative career ladders. That makes it a compact corpus for examining what management looks like from the productive academic layer.

For people who have worked inside universities, much of this managerial culture is familiar enough to be immediately legible. Blackball should not manufacture skepticism about that insider perspective merely because the account expresses it through satire.

Blackball should use the account to generate and organize questions such as:

- What work is actually done by expanding dean/provost/administrative layers?
- Which administrative functions are required, useful, duplicative, or self-perpetuating?
- Who has authority over teaching and research priorities, and who bears the consequences of those decisions?
- How have faculty governance and managerial control changed over time?
- When institutions say they are cutting costs, where do cuts actually fall?
- Do new administrative layers reduce faculty workload, or create additional reporting, assessment, compliance, and meeting work?
- What incentives govern administrative promotion and expansion?
- How do compensation, headcount, and job security differ between faculty, contingent instructors, staff, and managerial layers?
- When a university performs badly, who is accountable?

## Interpretive frame

Treat the account as a recurring **management-versus-production** lens.

A useful analogy is a firm in which skilled workers make the product while an expanding management hierarchy controls capital, procedures, metrics, and organizational priorities. In a university, faculty are not the only workers and not every administrator is managerial, but the distinction helps formulate empirical questions that aggregate labels such as “university spending” can hide.

This also creates an **insider/outsider information asymmetry**. People who work inside an institution can see routine dysfunction, incentive problems, pointless procedures, management fashions, and organizational absurdities that outsiders mostly encounter only through branding, rankings, credentials, public relations, and the finished product. The comparison is similar to what factory workers, engineers, or managers at a large industrial firm may know about the internal organization that customers and the general public do not.

The importance of `@ass_deans` is therefore not merely that it supplies isolated jokes. It makes part of that insider knowledge legible outside the institution and gives Blackball a vocabulary for collecting the underlying patterns.

## Evidence boundary

Treat `@ass_deans` as a primary source for the insider academic-management perspective it expresses.

Do **not** add a generic credibility warning simply because the source is satire or pseudonymous. Satire can accurately encode institutional knowledge, recurring practices, incentives, and recognizable managerial language.

A narrower provenance rule still applies when Blackball turns a post into a claim about a specific external fact. For example:

- the post itself is sufficient evidence of the account's observation, criticism, joke, or characterization;
- if a post names a particular salary, budget, appointment, policy, reorganization, enrollment figure, or event and Blackball wants to state that factual detail independently, attach the relevant institutional record or other direct source;
- the need to verify that external detail is not a reason to question the account's broader insider status or usefulness.

The account is pseudonymous. That means the author's biographical identity should not be invented. It does **not** mean the institutional perspective should be downgraded to an untrusted lead.
