# Associate Deans (@ass_deans)

## Why this belongs in Blackball

`@ass_deans` is useful here as an **insider-view corpus about university management**.

The important contrast is not “academics versus universities.” A more useful working model is:

**faculty and other academic workers produce much of the teaching, research, and disciplinary substance → managerial layers allocate resources, impose procedures, define priorities, measure performance, and increasingly mediate the institution**

The account's jokes repeatedly target associate deans, vice provosts, committees, strategic plans, assessment systems, branding language, restructuring, faculty governance, adjunctification, and administrative career ladders. That makes it a compact corpus for examining what management looks like from the productive academic layer.

Blackball should use the account to generate and organize questions such as:

- What work is actually done by expanding dean/provost/administrative layers?
- Which administrative functions are required, useful, duplicative, or self-perpetuating?
- Who has authority over teaching and research priorities, and who bears the consequences of those decisions?
- How have faculty governance and managerial control changed over time?
- When institutions say they are cutting costs, where do cuts actually fall?
- Do new administrative layers reduce faculty workload, or create additional reporting, assessment, compliance, and meeting work?
- What incentives govern administrative promotion and expansion?
- How do compensation, headcount, and job security differ between faculty, contingent instructors, staff, and managerial layers?
- When a university performs badly, who is accountable?

## Interpretive frame

Treat the account as a recurring **management-versus-production** lens.

A useful analogy is a firm in which skilled workers make the product while an expanding management hierarchy controls capital, procedures, metrics, and organizational priorities. In a university, faculty are not the only workers and not every administrator is managerial, but the distinction helps formulate empirical questions that aggregate labels such as “university spending” can hide.

The strongest Blackball use is therefore not “this joke proves administrative bloat.” It is:

**the joke identifies a managerial practice, contradiction, incentive, or institutional vocabulary worth tracing to budgets, organizational charts, job descriptions, faculty-handbook rules, meeting minutes, accreditation requirements, compensation data, and historical headcounts.**

The satire is also evidence in its own right when the question is about how academics describe or perceive university management.

## Evidence boundary

Preserve three separate layers:

1. **The tweet itself** — primary evidence that the account made a particular joke or criticism at a particular time.
2. **The managerial phenomenon referenced by the tweet** — verify independently where a factual claim is wanted.
3. **Interpretation** — whether the example demonstrates administrative bloat, managerial capture, necessary coordination, compliance burden, or some other explanation remains a question for comparison with stronger institutional evidence.

Do not flatten the account into generic “higher-education humor.” Its value is specifically the sustained view of universities through the relationship between academic production and managerial authority.

The account is pseudonymous. Use it as an insider-view corpus because of the institutional vocabulary and recurring management perspective expressed in the posts; do not silently convert that into a verified biographical claim about the author's employment or identity without independent evidence.
