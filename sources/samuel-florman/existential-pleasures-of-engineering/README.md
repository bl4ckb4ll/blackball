# Samuel C. Florman — *The Existential Pleasures of Engineering*

## Source position

Samuel C. Florman was a civil engineer, contractor, and writer about engineering culture. *The Existential Pleasures of Engineering* was first published by St. Martin's Press in 1976; a second edition appeared in 1994.

The book is useful as an engineer's explicit defense and description of the profession. It should not be treated as an employment-outcomes study.

Publisher metadata describes it as a response to anti-technology criticism and as an account of how engineers think and feel about their work.

## Jack Ganssle / *The Embedded Muse* lead

Jack Ganssle's book-review page recommends Florman's book. In Ganssle's summary, Florman places a “golden age of engineering” roughly from 1850 to 1950 and treats nineteenth-century engineers as unusually celebrated public figures.

That is **Ganssle's report of Florman's periodization** until the relevant passage in Florman is inspected directly.

This is almost certainly the book behind the remembered *Embedded Muse* recommendation about an earlier age when engineers had greater public status.

## Blackball questions

Do not ask only whether engineering once had more prestige.

Ask:

- Which engineers supplied the examples behind the prestige narrative?
- Were they salaried employees, partners, family-firm owners, military engineers, public appointees, patentees, contractors, or financiers?
- How did they obtain their first consequential work?
- What family money, inheritance, apprenticeship, patronage, government procurement, or company ownership appears upstream?
- Who performed the construction and production labor beneath the celebrated engineering work?
- What happened to ordinary engineering graduates in the same periods?
- Did the social status of a few famous builders track pay, autonomy, and employment security for the occupation generally?

A history of “the engineer” built only from famous bridge builders and industrial leaders risks sampling on success.
