## What the book contains

Cobbs, a psychiatrist and management consultant, and Turnock, an attorney and civil-rights advocate, draw on the experiences of 32 African-American executives who rose through major American corporations. Reviews and catalog descriptions identify recurring subjects including racial and gender bias, isolation, unwritten or difficult-to-read workplace signals, achievement, competition, leadership, power, mentors, and the organizational rules surrounding advancement.

The source is useful precisely because the endpoint — senior corporate success — does not erase the route taken to get there.

## Blackball use: success is not a complete moral metric

Do not code this book merely as another set of success stories.

Use it to separate at least three questions:

1. **Did the person obtain status, money, authority, or a senior title?**
2. **What did the institution require the person to learn, tolerate, suppress, navigate, or accommodate in order to obtain it?**
3. **Were those requirements good, merely survivable, or morally damaging?**

The third question cannot be answered by pointing back to the first.

A person can win a contest whose rules are ugly. A person can acquire power while also describing isolation, racial hostility, pressure to conform, or compromises that should not be euphemized as simply “growing up” or “learning how business works.” Conversely, do not invent a moral compromise where the executive does not report one. Preserve the speaker's own account and distinguish description from Blackball's later evaluation.

## Questions for machine reading

For each executive/profile, extract where possible:

- employer, role, industry, and career sequence;
- family and class background when stated;
- explicit incidents of racial or gender bias;
- isolation or tokenization;
- mentors, patrons, sponsors, and informal networks;
- rules or signals that were not formally stated;
- behavior the executive says they changed in order to advance;
- conflicts they confronted versus conflicts they chose not to confront;
- examples of speaking up, strategic silence, accommodation, exit, or resistance;
- descriptions of power and how one learns to use it;
- costs to family, community, identity, health, friendship, conscience, or other non-career goods when the source actually documents them;
- whether the executive regards a given accommodation as regrettable, necessary, neutral, or positive;
- whether later independence/entrepreneurship is described as escape from corporate gamesmanship.

Do not flatten all of these into a scalar “success” field.

## Link to education and college evaluation

This book belongs beside college outcome claims because colleges often treat admission to prestigious corporations, finance, consulting, management, or executive tracks as self-validating evidence that an education “worked.”

Blackball should instead preserve both sides:

- the financial and occupational outcome may be real and worth reporting;
- the life inside the institution, and the moral/social costs required to obtain or keep that outcome, remain separate evidence questions.

A placement report that says a graduate became an executive does not tell us whether the work was good, whether the institution was humane, what compromises were demanded, or whether the graduate would recommend the route knowing its full costs.
