# Cruikshank and Sicilia — *The Engine That Could*

## Bibliography

Jeffrey L. Cruikshank and David B. Sicilia, *The Engine That Could: Seventy-five Years of Values-driven Change at Cummins Engine Company*. Harvard Business School Press, copyright 1997; Google Books lists a 1998 edition, 589 pages.

Google Books metadata also lists **Cummins Engine Company** as compiler.

WorldCat records Cummins Engine Company alongside Cruikshank and Sicilia in the bibliographic record.

## Source position

This is a company history with unusually deep corporate access, not a neutral view from nowhere.

That is a reason to preserve it carefully, not a reason to discard it.

Publisher/catalog descriptions say the authors draw extensively on interviews and archival research and trace Cummins from a small Indiana machine shop through its growth as a diesel-engine manufacturer.

Before using a claim from the book, Blackball should identify:

- whether the underlying evidence is a company archive, interview, public record, or author interpretation;
- whether Cummins selected or constrained archival access;
- what role the company had in initiating, compiling, funding, reviewing, or distributing the book;
- whether workers, unions, dealers, suppliers, customers, managers, engineers, and owners receive comparable evidentiary treatment;
- which favorable management claims have independent controls.

Do not infer from “compiled by Cummins Engine Company” alone a particular editorial-control arrangement. Recover the preface/acknowledgments and contract history if available.

## Why it belongs in the engineering-career corpus

Cummins supplies a long industrial setting in which engineering, manufacturing, management, family/company history, capital, labor, and corporate values can be separated instead of compressed into “engineers built engines.”

Use it to ask:

- Who got hired as engineers, from which schools, and through which channels?
- Which technical leaders came from family or company networks?
- How much authority did engineers have relative to finance, sales, manufacturing, ownership, and the board in different eras?
- Which celebrated technical decisions depended on machinists, production workers, suppliers, field service, or customers?
- When management language says “values-driven,” what events and outcomes make that claim testable?
