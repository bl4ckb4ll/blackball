## Summary

The discussion follows Gothic writing from its eighteenth-century beginnings through its literary and architectural afterlives. It asks why ruined castles, persecution, supernatural threat and buried histories became such durable ways of staging anxieties about religion, reason, sexuality and social order.

Rather than treating “Gothic” as one fixed genre, the episode distinguishes an evolving set of forms: a reaction to Enlightenment confidence, a commercial fiction associated strongly with women writers and readers, and later a respectable cultural style. The guests connect the novels’ machinery of fear to the historical pressures that made it intelligible.

## Guests

- **[Chris Baldick](../../../people/Chris Baldick.md):** traces the literary genealogy of Gothic fiction and the historical fears organized by works such as *Frankenstein*.
- **[A. N. Wilson](../../../people/A. N. Wilson.md):** places Gothic writing within wider changes in religion, belief and nineteenth-century culture.
- **[Emma Clery](../../../people/Emma Clery.md):** examines the eighteenth-century rise of supernatural fiction, including its readership and women’s role in the genre.

## Analytical hooks

- genre as a historical container for social and religious anxiety
- commercial readership and the later conversion of popular forms into respectable culture
- women's production and readership inside literary-history narratives
