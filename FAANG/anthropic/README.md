# Anthropic employment map

This is a second employer case in the FAANG working area. The purpose is not to tell a particular person whether Anthropic is a good fit. It is to make the employer's actual demand, geography, and access conditions inspectable enough that a worker can decide whether spending time moving toward one of its kinds of work has any evidence behind it.

Use the same order as the Amazon case:

1. map Anthropic's public job families and named teams;
2. attach dated requisitions to those areas;
3. record what each role says the worker will actually do;
4. record location and in-office requirements;
5. record qualifications and hiring gates;
6. separately investigate whether people with nontraditional backgrounds are actually hired.

Do not turn employer recruiting language into evidence of realized hiring behavior.

## Geography and remote work

A repeated statement in current Anthropic job postings says staff are generally expected to be in an Anthropic office at least 25% of the time, with some roles requiring more. Many postings are centered on San Francisco, New York City, and Seattle. Other current locations include London, Dublin, Zürich, Washington DC, Tokyo, Singapore, Sydney, Seoul, Paris, Munich, Bangalore, Mumbai, and others.

There are genuinely remote-friendly postings, including some US-wide roles, but many are explicitly `Remote-Friendly (Travel-Required)`. Do not collapse "remote-friendly" into "work from anywhere with no travel or relocation constraint."

- https://www.anthropic.com/careers/jobs
- current Greenhouse postings linked from that page

## What Anthropic says about nontraditional technical backgrounds

Anthropic's careers material says that about half of its technical staff arrived without prior machine-learning experience, that about half have PhDs but some colleagues did not attend college, and that independent research, technical writing, and substantial open-source contributions are useful evidence to put prominently in an application. It also tells engineering-background candidates to apply as engineers rather than assuming research requires the research-scientist route.

Source:
- https://www.anthropic.com/careers

This is useful evidence about Anthropic's stated hiring philosophy. It is **not yet evidence about the probability that a worker without conventional credentials will be hired**. Blackball should test the claim against observable employee backgrounds where that can be done responsibly and against the actual requirements in requisitions.

## Worker-access question

For Blackball the important question is not "is Anthropic prestigious?" and not merely "what skills appear in its postings?" It is:

> **Is there a real path from a low-power job into any part of this employer's demand surface, and what evidence would a worker have to produce before applying?**

Do not assume anyone will pay for the transition. Measure whether the path can plausibly be built while the worker is still earning a living.

For each role family, eventually record:

- current openings and persistence over repeated snapshots;
- location and in-office requirements;
- minimum education wording and whether equivalent experience is explicitly accepted;
- experience level actually requested;
- required versus preferred technical background;
- interview gates;
- artifacts that could be produced independently: open-source work, public research, certifications, deployed systems, portfolios, writing, or other direct evidence;
- whether the route requires full-time school, unpaid work, relocation before offer, or other large sunk costs;
- whether there are lower-risk adjacent steps or side-income paths that build the same evidence;
- any public evidence about backgrounds of actual hires;
- unknowns and failure cases.

The point is to avoid telling someone to spend years on a route merely because the destination pays well.

## Current evidence boundary

This first pass establishes only Anthropic's **current advertised demand and stated hiring/location policies**. It does not establish:

- the probability of being hired;
- the size or quality of the applicant pool;
- whether "equivalent experience" is accepted in practice;
- whether independent work substitutes for professional experience at a given level;
- whether a remote-friendly role is practically accessible from every US location;
- whether a requisition results in a hire;
- how long a requisition stays open;
- how employees are actually allocated after joining.

Those are follow-up questions, not blanks to fill with optimism or pessimism.

3. Separate staff/senior-heavy demand from genuinely accessible entry routes.
4. Build a technical organization map under the recruiting categories: pretraining, RL, interpretability, research productivity, inference/infrastructure, product, compute, security/safeguards, applied AI, and other source-supported nodes.
5. Sample actual employee backgrounds where public professional biographies permit it; keep employer recruiting claims separate from observed hiring.
6. Repeat the snapshot so persistence and churn can be measured instead of inferred from one day.
