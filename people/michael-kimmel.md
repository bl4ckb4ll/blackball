# Michael S. Kimmel (1951–)

**Field:** sociology; gender studies; masculinity studies; social movements; comparative and historical sociology.

**University / institutional affiliation:** formerly **SUNY Distinguished Professor of Sociology and Gender Studies at Stony Brook University**. He founded and directed Stony Brook's Center for the Study of Men and Masculinities. He retired after public allegations of sexual harassment and professional misconduct emerged in 2018. In a 2021 statement, Stony Brook said, **“Dr. Michael Kimmel is no longer associated with Stony Brook University.”**

**Status checked:** 2026-08-31.

## Why this dossier is here

Kimmel is a useful Blackball case because his scholarly and public reputation was built heavily around masculinity, feminism, gender equality, and men's responsibility for sexism and violence. In 2018 former graduate students publicly alleged sexual harassment, sexually explicit and degrading workplace behavior, discriminatory treatment, and exploitation of graduate labor.

The case also illustrates an evidentiary problem that appears repeatedly in academic misconduct stories: there is extensive public testimony and a rapid professional fallout, but the publicly available record does not contain a completed institutional adjudication comparable to NYU's finding in the Avital Ronell case or Harvard's published policy finding in the John Comaroff case. The dossier therefore keeps allegations, Kimmel's response, association action, and employment outcome separate.

## Academic position and reputation

Stony Brook described Kimmel before the controversy as a SUNY Distinguished Professor of sociology and gender studies and one of the leading scholars of men and masculinities. He founded the Center for the Study of Men and Masculinities at Stony Brook and was founding editor of the journal *Men and Masculinities*.

His books include *Manhood in America*, *The Gendered Society*, *Guyland*, *Angry White Men*, and *Healing from Hate*. Stony Brook publicized him as an advocate for gender equality, and in March 2018 — only months before the allegations became public — announced that UN Women had named him a “Champion of Change.”

## 2018 allegations

In August 2018 *The Chronicle of Higher Education* reported allegations from a former graduate student who requested anonymity because she feared retaliation. She alleged that Kimmel suggested they have sex about six weeks into her graduate study and again later in her career. She also described comments about her appearance and the possibility that others would think she had advanced by sleeping with someone.

Soon afterward, former Stony Brook doctoral student **Bethany M. Coston** publicly described their experience working under Kimmel. Coston alleged inappropriate sexual conversation, homophobic and transphobic comments, unequal treatment of graduate students, uncompensated or non-academic labor demands, and a broader environment in which Kimmel treated cisgender heterosexual men more favorably. Coston explicitly connected their reluctance to leave the advising relationship with the fear that doing so might make finishing the doctorate harder.

These are allegations by former students, not findings by a court or a completed institutional tribunal.

## Kimmel's response

Before the allegations were fully public, Kimmel told the American Sociological Association that he had learned of “rumors” suggesting unethical professional conduct. He asked ASA to delay presenting him with the Jessie Bernard Award for six months so concerns could be vetted.

He said he took the concerns seriously, wanted to hear the charges, and wanted to make amends to people who believed he had harmed them. He also said he believed he had been professional and respectful in his relationships with women.

The wording matters. Kimmel did not, in the public statement reproduced by Inside Higher Ed, admit the specific allegations later made by former students. Nor should his stated willingness to hear complaints be represented as an institutional finding.

Source:

- Inside Higher Ed, **“Sociologist Facing Harassment ‘Rumors’ Defers Award,” 5 August 2018**: https://www.insidehighered.com/quicktakes/2018/08/06/sociologist-facing-harassment-rumors-defers-award

## American Sociological Association response

Kimmel had been selected to receive the **2018 Jessie Bernard Award**, an ASA prize honoring work that enlarges sociology's understanding of women's roles in society. Once the allegations became public, ASA Council voted unanimously to defer delivery of the award.

Members of the Jessie Bernard Award committee later said they had unanimously recommended rescinding the award, though ASA at the time did not yet have a settled procedure for revoking awards. This distinction matters: the public record shows association action concerning the award, but that action is not equivalent to an adjudication of the underlying harassment claims.

## Retirement and Stony Brook status

Kimmel retired from Stony Brook in the period following the 2018 allegations. Public secondary accounts say an institutional Title IX process was pending when he filed for retirement, but a final public finding from that process has not been located in this research pass.

The strongest later institutional statement located is Stony Brook's June 2021 response to the *Ivory Tower Boiler Room* podcast. The University described its Title IX/OEA procedures and concluded: **“Dr. Michael Kimmel is no longer associated with Stony Brook University. The University will not comment on specific personnel matters.”**

That is enough to establish current institutional status. It is **not** enough to infer what the University concluded about the factual allegations.

Sources:

## A useful counter-source: a former student describing a different experience

Not every former student publicly described Kimmel in the same way. **Cliff Leek**, who had worked closely with Kimmel, wrote in August 2018 that his own experience had been “mostly positive,” while also saying that the accounts from Coston and others forced him to reconsider his own complicity and the possibility that people working around Kimmel had experienced him very differently.

This is not evidence disproving the allegations. It is useful because it shows why a prestige network can persist without every member having witnessed the same behavior.

Source:

- Cliff Leek, **“Coming to Terms with Complicity,” Medium, August 2018**: https://medium.com/@cliffleek/coming-to-terms-with-complicity-348c851d4846

## Blackball relevance

Kimmel's case should be stored with several distinctions intact:

1. he was a major sociologist of gender and masculinity at Stony Brook, not merely a public commentator;
2. former graduate students made allegations involving both sexual conduct and the ordinary mechanics of academic dependency — advising, labor, status, and fear of retaliation;
3. the American Sociological Association deferred an award and members of its award committee later sought rescission;
4. Kimmel retired and Stony Brook later said he was no longer associated with the University;
5. the public materials reviewed here do **not** provide a final institutional merits finding, so the allegations should remain attributed;
6. at least one former student who worked closely with Kimmel publicly described his own experience as mostly positive while taking other students' allegations seriously.

For Blackball this is a case about how the same senior academic can be experienced very differently by different students and how dependence on one adviser can make even ambiguous or informal conduct consequential.

- Preserve the full ASA Council and Jessie Bernard Award committee records if they are publicly available.
