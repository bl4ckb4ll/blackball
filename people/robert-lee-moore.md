# Robert Lee Moore (1882–1974)

American mathematician and long-serving University of Texas at Austin professor, important in point-set topology and in the history of inquiry-based mathematics teaching. He is also important to this repository because named Black mathematicians who passed through Texas left specific accounts of his racist treatment of Black students.

See also:

- [Mathematicians of the African Diaspora](mathematicians-of-the-african-diaspora.md)

## Career and mathematics

- Born November 14, 1882, in Dallas; died October 4, 1974, in Austin.
- B.S. and M.A., University of Texas, 1901.
- Ph.D., University of Chicago, 1905, for *Sets of Metrical Hypotheses for Geometry*; his doctoral work was associated with Oswald Veblen and E. H. Moore.
- Taught at Tennessee, Princeton, Northwestern, and Pennsylvania before returning to the University of Texas in 1920.
- Professor at Texas from 1923; retired from teaching in 1969.
- Elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1931.
- President of the American Mathematical Society in 1937–1938.
- Worked principally in foundations and point-set topology. His 1932 AMS monograph was *Foundations of Point Set Theory*.
- Supervised a large and influential group of doctoral students, including R. H. Bing, F. Burton Jones, Mary Ellen Rudin, Richard D. Anderson, Edwin Moise, Gordon Whyburn, and Raymond Louis Wilder.

## The Moore method

Moore developed a style of teaching in which students were expected to build mathematics themselves from definitions and stated problems rather than learn it primarily through lectures or reading established solutions. In its strongest form students were discouraged or forbidden from consulting outside texts and from collaborating on the assigned problems. The classroom centered on students presenting their own arguments at the board and being questioned by the class.

The method became unusually influential in American mathematics education and survives in modified forms under names such as inquiry-based learning. Its reputation should not be separated from the question of who Moore permitted to participate in his classroom in the first place.

- F. Burton Jones, “The Moore Method,” *American Mathematical Monthly* 84 (1977), republished by Celebratio Mathematica: https://celebratio.org/Moore_RL/article/105/

## Racism and exclusion of Black mathematicians

Scott W. Williams's **Mathematicians of the African Diaspora (MAD)** archive collects several named accounts rather than merely applying a retrospective label to Moore.

### Walker E. Hunt

Walker E. Hunt, a Black University of Texas mathematics student who later earned a Ph.D. there, recalled asking to take Moore's celebrated topology course. According to Hunt, Moore told him he could enroll but would begin with a C and could only go down from there.

### Vivienne Malone Mayes

Vivienne Malone Mayes recalled that she could not enroll in Moore's class because Moore did not teach Black students. Her MAD biography also describes broader segregation at Texas: isolation in classes, denial of a teaching assistantship despite prior teaching experience, and exclusion from informal mathematical discussions held in a segregated cafe.

### Raymond L. Johnson

Raymond L. Johnson, then a University of Texas undergraduate and later a Rice Ph.D., wrote that the image of Moore he retained was of Moore attending a topology lecture by a student of R. H. Bing and walking out after discovering that the speaker was Black. Johnson's account is autobiographical testimony; the repository should attribute it to Johnson rather than silently turn it into an independently witnessed fact.

Primary source cluster:

- Scott W. Williams, “R. L. Moore — racist mathematician unveiled,” MAD: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/special/RLMoore-racist-math.html
- Raymond L. Johnson, personal history, University of Maryland: https://www.math.umd.edu/~rlj/RJ.html

The importance of these sources is that they preserve names, settings, and reported words. They make it possible to distinguish evidence of particular conduct from a vague claim that an old institution was simply “of its time.”

## R. H. Bing as contrast

Johnson explicitly contrasted Moore with Moore's student R. H. Bing, saying that he had “a very different image” of Bing. Johnson reported a story that Bing would not return from Wisconsin to Texas while Moore remained there.

That story has a chronology problem. Bing accepted a permanent position at UT in 1973; Moore died in October 1974. The claim about Bing's motive should therefore remain identified as a report circulating in the mathematical community, not as established chronology. Bing nevertheless has an independently documented reputation for judging people on their own merits and treating people kindly.

See [R. H. Bing](r-h-bing.md) for the chronology and sources.

## Institutional memory

The University of Texas named its physics, mathematics and astronomy building for Moore in the 1970s. In July 2020 the university announced that it would remove Moore's name and return the building to the descriptive name **Physics, Math and Astronomy Building (PMA)** as part of a broader reconsideration of campus symbols and support for Black students.

## Why this dossier is here

Moore is simultaneously a major figure in twentieth-century American topology, a highly influential teacher, and a documented participant in racial exclusion inside one of the country's important mathematics departments. The MAD archive is valuable precisely because the conventional record of awards, students, publications, and teaching influence can otherwise make the people who were kept outside that lineage disappear.
