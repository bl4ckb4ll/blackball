# Jack Temple Kirby

## Why Blackball is recording him

Jack Temple Kirby (1938–2009) was a historian of the American South whose work is directly relevant to the migration story J. D. Vance tells in *Hillbilly Elegy*. But the citation trail needs to be kept exact: Vance does **not** cite a Kirby book in this passage. He cites Kirby's sixteen-page article, **“The Southern Exodus, 1910–1960: A Primer for Historians,”** published in the *Journal of Southern History* in 1983.

That distinction matters for a Blackball source audit. *Hillbilly Elegy* notes 6, 7, 8, and 11 point to Kirby's article, with notes 8 and 11 specifying page 598. The repeated references make clear that Kirby is not merely a name appearing in Vance's bibliography. But citation evidence cannot establish whether Vance read every page of the article, and it certainly does not establish that he read Kirby's much larger body of work.

Kirby is especially useful because that wider body of work puts Appalachian and southern migration inside a much larger structural history: agricultural transformation, mechanization, collapsing rural economies, New Deal programs, industrial labor demand, racial inequality, mass migration, environmental change, and the cultural stereotypes through which outsiders imagined the South.

## Career and position

Kirby earned his B.A. at Old Dominion and his graduate degrees at the University of Virginia. He joined Miami University in Ohio in 1965 and remained there until retiring in 2002, eventually serving as W. E. Smith Professor of History. In spring 1999 he was a Fulbright Senior Lecturer at the University of Genoa.

At his death in 2009 he was president of the Southern Historical Association. His later work was particularly important in southern agricultural and environmental history. His 2006 book *Mockingbird Song: Ecological Landscapes of the South* won the 2007 Bancroft Prize and the Southern Historical Association's Bennett H. Wall Award.

## What Vance actually cites

Full citation:

> Jack Temple Kirby, “The Southern Exodus, 1910–1960: A Primer for Historians,” *Journal of Southern History* 49, no. 4 (November 1983): 585–600.

- JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/2208677
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/2208677

In *Hillbilly Elegy*, this appears in the historical setup to Vance's family migration from Kentucky to industrial Ohio. Vance's endnotes cite Kirby four times in that early discussion. The important bibliographic fact for Blackball is that the cited work is only sixteen journal pages, not a monograph.

### Reading-completeness audit

Do not turn the citation pattern into a claim that cannot be demonstrated.

What can be said:

- Vance cites the Kirby article repeatedly.
- Two notes identify page 598.
- The article is short enough that a complete reading would have been easy.
- The citations establish use of the article as a source for the migration discussion.

What cannot be said from the endnotes alone:

- that Vance read every page of the article;
- that he accepted Kirby's whole interpretation rather than extracting particular facts;
- that he read *Rural Worlds Lost*, *Media-Made Dixie*, or Kirby's other books.

Blackball should therefore distinguish **source cited**, **page cited**, **claim supported**, **source actually discussed**, and **evidence of broader engagement with the author's work**.

## “The Southern Exodus”

Kirby's 1983 article treats the huge twentieth-century movement of Black and white southerners out of the South as a major historical event rather than a collection of isolated family decisions. That is the scale on which it should be read: demographic movement connected to changes in agriculture, regional labor markets, race, war, industrialization, and the reorganization of rural life.

This matters for evaluating *Hillbilly Elegy*. Vance narrates an individual family story and then uses scholarship to situate that story in a broader migration. Kirby supplies part of that structural bridge. Blackball should preserve both levels instead of letting memoir stand in for population history or letting aggregate migration history erase the particular experiences of migrants.

## *Rural Worlds Lost: The American South, 1920–1960*

Kirby's *Rural Worlds Lost* is probably the most important next source for Blackball even though Vance does not cite it in the *Hillbilly Elegy* notes discussed above.

Louisiana State University Press describes the book as a study of the transformation of ordinary rural southern lives and communities under modernization. Kirby uses WPA oral histories, archival research, and federal agricultural censuses. He examines the spread of the cash economy, New Deal programs, agricultural change, migration, class relationships, family life, health, leisure, and rural culture.

That makes the book useful for resisting a false choice between “culture” and “economics.” The migration out of Appalachia and the rural South can be studied simultaneously as a change in jobs, land, technology, wages, institutions, family networks, and culture.

- LSU Press: https://lsupress.org/9780807113608/rural-worlds-lost/

## *Media-Made Dixie: The South in the American Imagination*

This book is unusually relevant to Blackball's treatment of Vance because Kirby studied not only the South but the **production of the image of the South**.

Kirby tracks depictions of the region in movies, radio, television, plays, popular histories, and music. The University of Georgia Press describes the book as an account of how mass media shaped and sometimes controlled public perceptions of the South. Kirby examined recurring representations ranging from poverty and the “cracker” or “hillbilly” image to plantation nostalgia, race, back-roads stereotypes, and modern “New South” imagery.

That creates an obvious research question around *Hillbilly Elegy*: the memoir should be studied not just as testimony about one family but as another mass-cultural object that helped define what millions of outsiders thought an Appalachian or “hillbilly” life meant.

This is a Blackball analytical extension. It should not be attributed to Kirby as a judgment on Vance, who published years after Kirby's death.

- University of Georgia Press: https://www.ugapress.org/9780820323886/media-made-dixie/

## Blackball reading frame

Kirby should not be reduced to “the historian Vance cites for hillbilly migration.” His work offers several separate measurement and comparison problems:

1. **Migration scale.** How many people left which southern/Appalachian counties, when, and for which destinations?
2. **Push factors.** Agricultural mechanization, mine and timber employment, land tenure, poverty, racial oppression, and declining or unstable rural work.
3. **Pull factors.** Industrial wages, wartime labor demand, recruiting networks, relatives already living in destination cities, and transportation corridors.
4. **Distributional effects.** Which migrants improved wages and material conditions, which did not, and what happened across the next generation?
5. **Community loss and gain.** What institutions and networks were lost, retained, rebuilt, or transformed after migration?
6. **Representation.** How did newspapers, film, television, books, politics, and memoir turn migrants into a recognizable social type such as “hillbillies”?
7. **Structure versus moral explanation.** When a later author explains outcomes through family behavior, religion, work habits, violence, or “culture,” what structural variables in Kirby's account have disappeared from the causal story?

These are not reasons to dismiss cultural explanations. They are variables that need to be measured before culture is permitted to function as a residual explanation for everything else.

## Comparison with Carl E. Feather

Kirby and Carl E. Feather form a useful paired source set for the migration passage in *Hillbilly Elegy*.

Kirby works at the level of the **southern exodus as a large historical process**. Feather's *Mountain People in a Flat Land* works at the level of **particular Appalachian migrant families in one northeast Ohio community**. Kirby supplies macro history; Feather supplies community-scale oral history and journalism.

Vance's own story sits between those scales: one family presented as representative of a much larger migration. Blackball should use Kirby and Feather to test exactly how representative it is.

## Source discipline

When using Kirby against or alongside *Hillbilly Elegy*, keep three claims separate:

- what Kirby actually wrote;
- what Vance actually cited from Kirby;
- what Blackball infers after comparing the two.

Do not say Vance “ignored Kirby's book” as though the book were the source he cited. The immediate citation is a short journal article. A more defensible question is whether Vance's narrow use of the migration scholarship captures the fuller structural history developed by Kirby and other historians.
