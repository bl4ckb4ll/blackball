# Andrew Hacker

## Why Blackball is recording him

Andrew Hacker (1929–2026) is useful to Blackball because he spent decades inside American higher education while repeatedly writing public-facing criticism of the institutions that employed him. In *Higher Education?* (2010), coauthored with Claudia Dreifus, that insider position is combined with journalistic campus reporting, institutional statistics, and an explicit attempt to ask whether colleges actually educate undergraduates in proportion to their cost and prestige.

His work is especially relevant to Blackball's questions about:

- elite-college prestige as a social and economic signal;
- selection versus the causal effect of attending a prestigious institution;
- research prestige versus undergraduate teaching;
- administrative and faculty labor structure;
- tuition, debt, and institutional spending;
- vocational training versus liberal education;
- the tendency to infer broad social value from credentials and institutional labels.

## Biographical anchor

- Born August 30, 1929.
- Died April 21, 2026.
- Political scientist and public intellectual.
- Undergraduate education: Amherst College.
- Graduate study included Oxford and the University of Michigan.
- Ph.D.: Princeton University.
- Taught at Cornell University before joining Queens College, City University of New York.
- Professor Emeritus of Political Science at Queens College.
- Longtime contributor to *The New York Review of Books*.

The New Press's 2026 memorial describes him as a scholar of political science, public intellectual, author of more than a dozen books, and writer known for provocative use of statistics.

## *Higher Education?* and the collaboration with Claudia Dreifus

Hacker's own account of the collaboration matters. Dreifus proposed the higher-education project. Hacker agreed to coauthor it in part because she was an experienced journalist and, in his account, would keep the book focused on actual people rather than letting it become only an academic argument.

That helps explain the book's mixed method:

**Hacker's long institutional/statistical criticism + Dreifus's reporting/interview practice + three years of campus research.**

Do not turn that shorthand into an unsupported division of labor. The exact authorship of interviews, visits, data work, and individual chapters remains to be reconstructed.

## 2005 precursor: “The Truth About the Colleges”

Hacker's *New York Review of Books* essay of November 3, 2005 is a major precursor to *Higher Education?*. It already contains many later themes:

- a small national prestige tier later formalized as the “Golden Dozen”;
- admission rates, yield, SAT scores, and rankings as prestige machinery;
- elite-school class composition;
- Asian and Jewish representation in selective admissions;
- Dale and Krueger's attempt to separate student quality from selective-college treatment;
- Princeton Review teaching ratings;
- Ken Bain and Patrick Allitt as contrasting accounts of college teaching;
- Carnegie/NSSE material on undergraduate education;
- NCES/IPEDS staffing figures;
- research-university expansion and medical-school research;
- AAUP salary data;
- adjunct and graduate teaching labor;
- Murray Sperber's criticism of research and athletic structures.

Because the NYRB page exposes Hacker's footnotes, it is a particularly useful source trail for the later book. It should not, however, be substituted for the book's own bibliography.

## Characteristic method

Hacker often works by taking a socially accepted prestige claim and asking for a measurable implication.

Examples:

- If a tiny group of colleges supposedly identifies and develops exceptional talent, what do their graduates later do?
- If research improves teaching, can students detect superior teaching at research-intensive universities?
- If expensive colleges provide more education, where does the extra money actually go?
- If tenure protects intellectual independence, when and how does that protection appear in real cases?

This style is valuable because it converts prestige language into testable questions. It can also become weak when the chosen measurement is too crude. The Princeton class-of-1973 / *Who's Who* exercise is a clear example: the question is useful, but “national prominence” is an inadequate scalar measure of a graduate's life.

Relevant to Hacker's broader habit of using large-scale descriptive statistics to challenge conventional accounts of social change.

Later work criticizing compulsory advanced mathematics and claims about STEM labor demand. This belongs in Blackball because it again tests educational credential requirements against what people actually need and what jobs actually exist.

## Source discipline

Do not call Hacker a sociologist. Some reviews did; his discipline was political science.

Do not treat his provocative quantitative comparisons as self-validating. For every number, recover the source owner and denominator.

Do not assume his long academic career makes his claims about universities representative. Insider access is evidence of access, not a sampling design.

Do not attribute the reporting in *Higher Education?* solely to Hacker. Dreifus is an equal coauthor and Hacker himself emphasized the importance of her journalistic role.
