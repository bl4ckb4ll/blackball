# Nell Irvin Painter

## Why Blackball is recording her

Nell Irvin Painter is a historian of the United States and Edwards Professor of American History Emerita at Princeton. For Blackball she is especially useful as a historian of **how racial categories are made, revised, naturalized, and given scientific authority**, rather than treated as timeless biological facts.

Her 2010 book *The History of White People* is directly relevant to Blackball's material on Charles Murray and IQ because Painter follows the history of racial classification through nineteenth- and twentieth-century racial science, eugenics, and intelligence testing. The book's table of contents includes chapters on "The discovery of degenerate families," "From degenerate families to sterilization," "Intelligence testing of new immigrants," and "Refuting racial science."

## *The History of White People*

Painter's central historical contribution is not a new psychometric estimate. It is a genealogy of the categories themselves. She shows that ideas about who counted as white, which European peoples were treated as distinct "races," and which physical or mental measurements were supposed to rank those groups changed substantially over time.

The book therefore belongs in any Blackball discussion of intelligence testing and racial hierarchy because it supplies historical context that a cross-sectional statistical model cannot supply by itself: measurements do not enter history without institutions, categories, prior theories, and political uses.

Painter's own book description explicitly ties racial hierarchy to scientific classification and discusses skull measurement, eugenics, and highly biased intelligence tests as historical techniques used to rank groups and exclude working people and immigrants.

The useful comparison is methodological rather than a claim that Painter's historical work mechanically refutes every psychometric result in *The Bell Curve*.

Questions for the crosslink:

- When a contemporary argument compares racial groups statistically, how historically stable are the group categories it treats as inputs?
- Which parts of a measured difference are empirical descriptions, and which parts are interpretations about the underlying cause?
- What historical institutions produced the tests, classifications, samples, and meanings attached to the results?
- When does the authority of quantification make an old hierarchy look newly objective?
- Which claims can actually be tested by the data at hand, and which are being imported from a broader story about race, heredity, class, or merit?

## Important distinction

Do not turn historical genealogy into a substitute for statistical criticism. Painter can show why racial categories and the authority of racial science require historical scrutiny. That does **not**, by itself, determine the coefficient in a modern regression, the predictive validity of a test, or the causal source of a contemporary mean difference.

The strongest Blackball treatment should put the two forms of criticism beside each other:

1. **Historical:** where did the categories and hierarchy-making practices come from?
2. **Statistical/causal:** what does this particular dataset and model actually establish?

That separation makes the critique stronger, not weaker.
