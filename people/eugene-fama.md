# Eugene F. Fama

## Why Blackball is recording him

Eugene Fama is a central University of Chicago case because his entire intellectual career is unusually tightly coupled to the institution. He entered Chicago in 1960, joined the faculty in 1963, and—apart from two years teaching in Belgium—spent his career researching and teaching there.

That gives direct chronological overlap with Sudhir Venkatesh's 1989 description of the Hyde Park/Woodlawn boundary and the low-priced informal labor visible around the university.

## Chronology

- **1960:** graduated from Tufts University and enrolled at the University of Chicago.
- **1963:** earned an MBA and joined the Chicago Graduate School of Business faculty.
- **1964:** completed a Ph.D.; dissertation on stock-market prices.
- **1970:** published "Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work."
- **2013:** received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Lars Peter Hansen and Robert Shiller.

## Documented benefit from University of Chicago affiliation

Chicago Booth's own biography is unusually useful here because it explicitly describes the affiliation as career-forming. It says Fama arrived when major finance scholars including Merton Miller, Harry Roberts, and Lester Telser were on the faculty; as a young scholar he had abundant opportunities to discuss research with them and receive their recommendations. Fama regarded Miller as a mentor, and Miller gave recurring feedback on research that led toward Fama's 1970 efficient-markets paper.

So Blackball does not need to infer that Chicago was beneficial merely from the institution's prestige. The institution itself documents mentorship, intellectual access, faculty appointment, and a long-term research platform.

## Hyde Park relation

Fama was already a Chicago faculty member for more than twenty-five years when Venkatesh arrived in 1989. Booth's current Hyde Park center is at 5807 S. Woodlawn Avenue; Fama's institutional career was therefore physically situated inside the same university-centered neighborhood whose class boundary Venkatesh described.

The point is not that efficient-markets theory is refuted by windshield washing. Securities markets and informal street labor are different objects. The research question is institutional: **how did a university that produced globally authoritative formal accounts of markets understand the highly unequal labor market immediately around itself?**

## Questions for investigation

1. What did Fama write or say about labor markets, poverty, inequality, human capital, welfare, regulation, or Chicago's South Side?
2. Did he ever use Hyde Park, Woodlawn, Chicago segregation, or informal labor as economic examples?
3. Which elements of his early research were materially enabled by Chicago faculty, computing, data, workshops, research assistance, or institutional finance?
4. How did Chicago's finance workshop and faculty network affect publication, hiring, citation, consulting, or access to market data?
5. What local labor-market conditions existed around UChicago during Fama's major theoretical work?
6. Where do Fama's own stated limits on efficient-market claims differ from popular or policy uses of his work?

## Source discipline

Do not treat Fama as responsible for every policy associated with "Chicago economics." Separate his own writings from the broader Chicago School label. Likewise, do not imply that neighborhood poverty is a direct counterexample to a theory about securities prices unless the theory actually makes a claim about that domain.
