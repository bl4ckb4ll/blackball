# Eric G. Falkenstein

## Why Blackball is recording him

Eric Falkenstein is useful to Blackball because he is not merely an academic economist commenting on finance from outside it. He has an economics Ph.D., published academic finance research, built bank risk systems and capital-allocation models, helped develop a commercial default model, managed money, and designed trading systems. He therefore provides a useful case where criticism of financial economics comes from someone who has actually had to measure risk, allocate capital, build models that other people use, and trade.

He is also useful because one of his recurring criticisms maps directly onto a Blackball labor-market question: economists often move too quickly from an equilibrium story about compensation to a claim about what workers are actually paid. Falkenstein repeatedly uses dirty or "smelly" work as an example: if unpleasantness automatically produces a compensating wage premium, then unpleasant jobs should visibly pay more. He points out that many plainly unpleasant jobs do not.

## Political and personal source discipline

Falkenstein has described himself as a libertarian and, in older posts, as a Friedmanite conservative. In August 2026 he still described himself as a libertarian while arguing that crypto markets nevertheless require some regulation.

Blackball should not turn that political disagreement into a character judgment. The user's assessment is explicitly different: the user is far to Falkenstein's left politically, disagrees with him on plenty of politics and would not necessarily want to debate all of his financial theories, but gets the impression that Falkenstein is a decent and pleasant person and would happily spend time with him. Record that as the user's assessment, not as a biographical fact.

This distinction matters for the corpus: disagreement about ideology is not evidence that somebody is stupid, dishonest, or personally contemptible.

## Education and career

- **1987:** B.A. in economics, Washington University in St. Louis.
- As an undergraduate he was a teaching assistant for Hyman Minsky and wrote a senior thesis under Laurence Meyer; he later worked for an economics department led by future Cleveland Fed president Jerry Jordan.
- **1994:** Ph.D. in economics, Northwestern University.
- His dissertation, *Mutual Funds, Idiosyncratic Variance, and Asset Returns*, documented what he describes as a negative relation between volatility and subsequent stock returns and also examined mutual-fund preferences for volatile stocks.
- **1996:** part of the dissertation appeared in the *Journal of Finance* as “Preferences for Stock Characteristics As Revealed by Mutual Fund Portfolio Holdings.” The paper reports that mutual funds preferred visible, liquid stocks and were averse to low-idiosyncratic-volatility stocks.
- **KeyCorp:** worked first as an economist, then built the bank's value-at-risk system and later worked on firm-wide economic/risk-capital allocation.
- **Moody's:** developed RiskCalc, a private-company default model.
- Later worked in hedge funds and trading as a convertible-bond analyst, long/short equity portfolio manager, and high-frequency trading algorithm designer.
- More recently he has worked on Ethereum/automated-market-maker problems and continues writing under the Falkenblog name on Substack.

The University of Minnesota's 2023 financial-mathematics workshop biography is a particularly useful compact source because it independently lists the Northwestern Ph.D., low-volatility dissertation, KeyBank risk work, Moody's RiskCalc work, hedge-fund work, and his two books.

## Dissertation, low volatility, and the missing risk premium

Falkenstein's 1994 dissertation is an early low-volatility result. Its abstract says that, over the preceding thirty years of NYSE and AMEX data, variance was negatively correlated with expected return after accounting for familiar characteristics such as size and book-to-market: more volatile stocks had lower returns, other things equal.

That empirical claim is distinct from his broader explanation for it. Falkenstein later argued that the usual positive risk-return story is badly wrong as a general description of asset markets. His broader theory emphasizes relative status and benchmarking: if investors care strongly about performance relative to others rather than only absolute wealth, deviation from the consensus portfolio becomes the relevant risk and a conventional positive risk premium need not appear.

Blackball should keep at least three propositions separate:

1. **Low-volatility anomaly:** high-volatility equities have often failed to outperform low-volatility equities and in many samples underperform them.
2. **Fama/French and factor-model criticism:** Falkenstein argues that factors often work better as empirical characteristics or benchmarking devices than as demonstrated compensation for bearing identifiable economic risk.
3. **Relative-status explanation:** Falkenstein's proposed explanation for the missing positive risk premium. This is a stronger theoretical claim and should not be treated as established merely because the low-volatility evidence is real.

If the phrase “Fama factor theory” is used in Blackball notes, check whether it means the Fama-French factor framework, Falkenstein's criticism of factor-as-risk interpretations, or his own low-volatility/relative-status theory. Do not silently collapse them.

## Criticism of economists who do not do the work

Falkenstein has repeatedly drawn a line between formal economic authority and practical knowledge.

### Chief economists versus banking

In an April 9, 2009 Falkenblog post, while criticizing Joseph Stiglitz and the IMF, he wrote that chief economists generally do public-relations work rather than “get their hands dirty actually banking.” He added that executives do not ordinarily rely on chief economists for practical operating advice.

Whether or not the particular attack on Stiglitz is accepted, the methodological distinction is useful: **having an economics title is not the same thing as bearing responsibility for underwriting, trading, allocating capital, raising money, managing a book, or surviving losses.**

### Technique versus institutional detail

In May 2010 he argued that econometric technique can let weak empirical work hide behind sophisticated methods when researchers do not understand the institutional details of the thing being measured. His example was research on file sharing, but the criticism generalizes: a clever identification strategy does not eliminate the need to know what the variables actually mean in the institution being studied.

### Credentials are noisy

In July 2010 he described an economics Ph.D. as a noisy signal because much technically difficult economics can be remote from economic practice. That is directly relevant to Blackball's distinction between educational prestige and demonstrated ability to do consequential work.

### Current continuity

This is not only an old-blog theme. In a 2024 post on an academic central-limit-order-book model, Falkenstein again criticized taking a highly stylized academic estimate and multiplying it across world trading volume to claim a huge aggregate welfare gain.

## “Smelly” jobs and compensating differentials

This is the strongest direct connection to the user's point.

Falkenstein traces the analogy to Keynes, who wrote in *The General Theory* that unpleasant or “smelly” processes should command higher rewards because workers would otherwise avoid them, and then analogized risky activity to the same logic.

Falkenstein's objection is empirical. In an April 2010 post he says septic-tank work does not pay especially well. In June 2011 he sharpened the point: “toilet cleaners do not earn more than other custodians,” and he also pointed to poorly compensated sewer labor in India. In July 2010 he made the same point more generally about “dirty jobs.”

The important Blackball lesson is **not** that compensating differentials are impossible. It is that an equilibrium diagram does not establish that a real worker is being compensated for a real disamenity. The premium has to be measured.

There is empirical literature supporting that caution. A 2012 study of compensating differentials and labor-market segmentation found evidence of preference/amenity tradeoffs in primary and intermediate labor markets but not in the secondary labor market. Experimental labor-market work also finds compensating differentials under controlled conditions while emphasizing how mobility, selection, and measurement affect the observed premium. In other words, the mechanism can exist without guaranteeing that the janitor, night-shift worker, sanitation worker, mechanic, HVAC technician, welder, or other worker is actually made whole for an unpleasant job.

## Blackball measurement program suggested by this case

Do not write “undesirable jobs pay more” as a theorem and stop. Measure it.

1. **Shift premium:** for the same employer and substantially the same job, record day-, afternoon-, and night-shift hourly wages. Store both dollars per hour and percentage premium.
2. **Dirty/hazardous premium:** compare a clearly specified dirty, dangerous, hot, cold, noisy, or otherwise unpleasant job to the closest realistic alternative available to the same labor pool.
3. **Total compensation:** record health insurance, retirement contribution, paid time, overtime rules, bonuses, tool allowances, and predictable unpaid time instead of treating base wage as the whole job.
4. **Entry wage versus advertised trade wage:** for welding, HVAC, automotive, industrial maintenance, and similar programs, compare school/teacher earnings claims with actual entry-level and median postings in the relevant geography.
5. **Instructor-selection problem:** a teacher saying that a welding certificate or degree “never did me wrong” is weak evidence about the labor-market payoff to the student. The instructor's teaching position is itself a selected outcome and may pay differently from the field being advertised.
6. **Vacancy and turnover:** if a job is unpleasant and still pays poorly, record whether employers respond with vacancies, turnover, understaffing, mandatory overtime, or lower hiring standards rather than a large wage increase.
7. **Bargaining power / labor-market segment:** test whether the unpleasantness premium is larger where workers have credible alternatives or bargaining power and smaller where workers are trapped in a secondary labor market.
8. **Claim-to-posting ratio:** when colleges, politicians, or commentators claim a trade pays $X/hour, collect a local sample of actual postings and report the fraction that reaches $X.

User-supplied examples to verify separately rather than hard-code as fact: very small night-shift premiums; janitorial work that is unpleasant without a large wage premium; an HVAC opening around $23/hour despite much larger public claims; ordinary automotive repair work involving welding; and welding programs whose promotional wage story may be based partly on instructors' own outcomes rather than graduates' field wages.

## Why this matters beyond Falkenstein

Falkenstein is useful precisely because he complicates a simple left/right sorting of sources. A libertarian finance practitioner can supply a strong empirical criticism of a textbook equilibrium story that is also useful to a left labor-market critique.

The claim to preserve is narrow and testable: **do not infer compensation from unpleasantness. Observe the wage premium, if any.**

That should become a general Blackball rule. When a textbook says a market mechanism “must” compensate workers, investors, consumers, or firms for something, look for the actual transfer in the data before treating the mechanism as an accomplished fact.
