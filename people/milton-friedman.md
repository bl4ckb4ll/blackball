# Milton Friedman

## Why Blackball is recording him

Milton Friedman belongs in the University of Chicago / Hyde Park case because he combined enormous intellectual and policy influence with a long residence and faculty career in the same university-centered neighborhood whose economic boundaries Blackball is studying.

His active Chicago career predates Sudhir Venkatesh's 1989 opening scene, so the comparison must be historical rather than pretending the two men occupied Hyde Park at the same moment.

## Chronology

- **1932:** completed undergraduate study at Rutgers.
- **1933:** earned an M.A. in economics from the University of Chicago.
- **1946:** joined the University of Chicago faculty.
- **1946–1977:** Chicago faculty career.
- **1962:** published *Capitalism and Freedom*.
- **1976:** received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.
- **1977:** left the regular Chicago faculty and moved to the Hoover Institution at Stanford.
- **2006:** died in San Francisco.

## Documented benefit from University of Chicago affiliation

Friedman's relationship to Chicago was both educational and professional. He earned his M.A. there, met Rose Director in an economics class, returned as a faculty member in 1946, and produced much of the work for which he became internationally influential during his Chicago years.

The affiliation supplied a long-term faculty position, colleagues, students, workshops, an intellectual school, publishing and institutional visibility, and a platform from which arguments about monetary policy, regulation, welfare, education, and individual choice traveled globally.

Blackball should still distinguish the benefit of the platform from the merits of any particular argument. A prestigious institution can amplify work without proving it correct.

## Hyde Park relation

University of Chicago materials explicitly identify Milton Friedman and George Stigler as Nobel-winning economists who made their home in Hyde Park. Another UChicago Hyde Park brochure identifies a building at 5825 S. Dorchester as having housed multiple major professors, including Friedman.

That makes Friedman a genuine resident, not merely a professor whose campus happened to be nearby.

The relevant reconstruction is therefore **Hyde Park and surrounding South Side conditions from 1946 through 1977**: housing, segregation, urban renewal, low-wage work, university expansion, policing, school policy, and employment. Those conditions can then be placed beside Friedman's contemporaneous writing about markets and public policy.

## Questions for investigation

1. What did Friedman write specifically about Chicago housing, segregation, schools, poverty, unemployment, or race?
2. What role did University of Chicago urban-renewal activity play in the neighborhood during his residency?
3. Did Friedman use local Hyde Park or South Side examples when discussing freedom of choice, housing, education, or welfare?
4. Which Chicago colleagues, seminars, institutions, and funders materially supported his research and public reach?
5. How did his claims about markets treat bargaining power, discrimination, inherited wealth, neighborhood segregation, and informal labor?
6. Where did Friedman explicitly acknowledge institutional or market failure, and where did later followers simplify his arguments?

## Source discipline

Do not attribute every "Chicago School" position to Friedman. Separate his own claims from those of Aaron Director, George Stigler, Gary Becker, Ronald Coase, later faculty, and political advocates.

Do not say Friedman personally observed Venkatesh's 1989 windshield-washer scene; Friedman had left the regular faculty in 1977. The legitimate claim is that he lived and worked in Hyde Park during an earlier phase of the same deeply unequal university-neighborhood geography.
