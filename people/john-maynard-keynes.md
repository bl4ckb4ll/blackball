# John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946)

British economist, civil servant, investor, writer, and Fellow of King's College, Cambridge. Keynes is central to Blackball's economics material because his work repeatedly joins technical economic questions to institutions, political choices, class, employment, money, and the social purpose of economic activity.

## Basic chronology

- Born in Cambridge on **5 June 1883**.
- Educated at Eton and King's College, Cambridge, where he studied mathematics.
- Entered the British civil service and worked at the India Office before returning to Cambridge.
- Elected a Fellow of King's College in 1909; later taught economics and served as bursar.
- Worked for the Treasury during the First World War and represented the Treasury at the Paris Peace Conference before resigning.
- Published *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), *A Treatise on Money* (1930), and *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), among many other works.
- Returned to government service during the Second World War, worked on war finance, and played a leading role in the Bretton Woods negotiations.
- Became Baron Keynes of Tilton in 1942.
- Died on **21 April 1946**.

## “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren”

Blackball's canonical note on the essay is under economics:

- [“Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren” (1930)](../economics/economic-possibilities-for-our-grandchildren-1930.md)

The essay is the source for the often-retold Keynes prediction that productivity growth could eventually reduce necessary paid work to a small fraction of life. Keynes's concrete illustration is a **fifteen-hour workweek**, not a future in which everyone literally spends all day reading books.

His deeper claim is that abundance would transform the human problem. Once basic material needs could be met with little labor, the central question would become what people do with their freedom from economic necessity and whether societies know how to convert technical abundance into a good life.

## Why Keynes matters here

Keynes is useful to Blackball for reasons broader than the usual textbook label “Keynesian economics.” His career crosses several institutions whose relationships matter to the repository's larger questions:

- elite education and Cambridge intellectual networks;
- government and Treasury service;
- journalism and public argument;
- financial markets and institutional investing;
- economic theory and policy;
- international monetary institutions;
- patronage of the arts and the question of what economic abundance is for.

The 1930 leisure essay is especially useful because it prevents productivity from being treated as an end in itself. Keynes asks what productivity gains are supposed to buy in human terms: more goods, shorter work, freedom from necessity, or some mixture of these.
