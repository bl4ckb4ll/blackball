# Boaventura de Sousa Santos (1940–)

**Field:** sociology; especially sociology of law, postcolonial theory, epistemology, globalization, social movements, democracy, and human rights.

**University / institutional affiliation:** longtime professor at the **University of Coimbra**, Portugal; professor emeritus of sociology / the Faculty of Economics. Founder and longtime director of the **Centre for Social Studies (CES), University of Coimbra**. In November 2024 he asked to cease being a CES associate and renounced the title of Director Emeritus. Older Coimbra biographies also identify him as a Distinguished Legal Scholar at the University of Wisconsin–Madison.

**Status checked:** 2026-08-31.

## Why this dossier is here

Santos is a useful Blackball case because he was not merely a professor accused of private misconduct. He was an internationally celebrated sociologist whose scholarly identity centered on decolonization, inequality, social movements, human rights, and critiques of entrenched power. The public controversy therefore generated unusually explicit claims about the power of a famous adviser over doctoral students and junior researchers, the dependence of academic careers on patronage, and the gap that can exist between an institution's public politics and its internal hierarchy.

The evidence must be kept in layers. There are public first-person allegations against Santos; there are institutional findings about patterns of harassment and abuse at CES that deliberately do **not** name the individuals responsible; there are allegations that were not established by a court; and there are later Portuguese prosecutorial decisions that turned partly on procedural deadlines rather than a merits finding that the alleged conduct did or did not occur.

## Academic position and reputation

The University of Coimbra's CES described Santos before the controversy as professor emeritus of sociology at Coimbra and Distinguished Legal Scholar at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. His research subjects included sociology of law, postcolonial theory, epistemology, democracy, globalization, social movements, and human rights.

Santos founded CES in 1978 and directed it for decades. When CES elected António Sousa Ribeiro director in 2019, the center described Santos as the retiring founder/director and a professor emeritus at Coimbra. That institutional history matters to the later allegations: the disputed relationship was not between a student and an interchangeable instructor, but between junior researchers and a founder with extraordinary symbolic and organizational authority.

## The 2023 chapter: “The walls spoke when no one else would”

In March 2023 Lieselotte Viaene, Catarina Laranjeiro, and Miye Nadya Tom published **“The walls spoke when no one else would: Autoethnographic notes on sexual-power gatekeeping within avant-garde academia”** in the Routledge volume *Sexual Misconduct in Academia: Informing an Ethics of Care in the University*.

The chapter initially anonymized both the institution and the people it described. It discussed an internationally recognized progressive research center, a “Star Professor,” a “whisper network,” unequal dependence of early-career researchers on senior academics, sexual-power gatekeeping, moral harassment, and intellectual and sexual exploitation. Subsequent public statements and reporting identified the setting as CES and associated Santos with the “Star Professor” description.

CES itself responded on 11 April 2023 by acknowledging that the chapter alleged unethical conduct at CES and announcing an institutional response. Routledge later said that after publication it received legal threats from various parties, including a UK law firm acting for one accused person; the publisher ultimately withdrew the chapter and returned its rights to the authors. The chapter nevertheless remains bibliographically identifiable and author-posted copies have circulated.

## Bella Gonçalves's public allegation

On 14 April 2023 Agência Pública published a first-person account from **Bella Gonçalves**, a Brazilian politician and former doctoral researcher at CES. She said Santos had supervised her doctoral work in 2013–2014.

Gonçalves alleged that Santos invited her to his apartment, put his hand on her leg, told her that people close to him obtained significant advantages, and suggested that they “deepened our relationship.” She said that the following day he met with her and her then-partner and humiliated them and their work. Gonçalves interpreted the sequence as evidence that affective or sexual closeness to powerful professors could yield academic advantages while refusal could bring punishment. She said she ended Santos's supervision and eventually returned to Brazil.

These are **Gonçalves's allegations and interpretation**, not a judicial finding. Agência Pública reported that Santos characterized the wider allegations as revenge and said he would take legal action.

## Independent CES commission, 2023–2024

CES created an independent commission to examine reports of harassment and abuse. Its final report is dated **29 February 2024** and was publicly presented in March.

The commission reported receiving testimony from **32 people**, involving **78 reports/complaints concerning 14 people**. It concluded that there was evidence of patterns of abuse of power and harassment by some people in senior CES positions. CES's Board publicly apologized to people who considered themselves victims of moral or sexual harassment or abuse of power and announced new reporting, prevention, and governance measures.

Crucially, the commission's public report did **not** identify Santos or any other individual as responsible for particular findings. It should therefore not be converted into the claim “the commission found Santos guilty.” The institutional finding is broader: CES had substantiated evidence of abusive patterns in its hierarchy.

## Departure from CES

On **26 November 2024**, CES announced that Santos had asked to withdraw as an associate of the center and to renounce the title of Director Emeritus. CES said he had raised objections and suspicions about the inquiry process; the Board defended the inquiry's procedures and said the external investigators were intended to identify specific facts and actors while giving accused people an opportunity to be heard.

Source:

- CES Board, **Press release, 26 November 2024**: https://ces.uc.pt/en/agenda-noticias/destaques/2024/comunicado-da-direcao-do-ces/apresentacao

## Courts and prosecutors: what later proceedings do and do not establish

The post-2024 legal history is easy to misstate.

Santos brought civil actions protecting his reputation and later a criminal defamation complaint against a group of 13 women who had accused him publicly. Reporting in January 2026 said Portuguese prosecutors archived his complaint against the 13 women; the reported grounds included insufficient evidence for false testimony and a limitations problem for insult claims. Santos's lawyer said he intended to challenge the archival decision.

Separately, the Portuguese Public Prosecutor examined material arising from the CES report and the women's collective. Reporting in April 2026 said the portion concerning alleged sexual offenses was archived because the legally required complaints had not been made within the applicable six-month period. Reporting also said other possible offenses remained under investigation. **An archival decision based on a complaint deadline is not a finding that the underlying conduct did not occur.** Santos publicly described the decision as confirmation of his position that the accusations had never been properly made in court.

In June 2026, RTP/Lusa reported that a separate defamation complaint brought by three women against Santos was archived after those complainants withdrew it. Again, that is a procedural ending, not a merits adjudication of the underlying academic-harassment allegations.

## Blackball relevance

This case is useful for questions about academic hierarchy because it provides several distinct evidence layers that can be compared rather than collapsed:

1. published autoethnography by former researchers;
2. a named former doctoral researcher's direct account;
3. an institutionally commissioned inquiry finding patterns of harassment and abuse of power without publicly naming individual perpetrators;
4. the institution's apology and governance changes;
5. Santos's denials and legal responses;
6. court/prosecutorial actions whose procedural dispositions do not answer every underlying factual question.

The central retrieval lesson is to preserve **who made each claim, what process tested it, and what that process was actually empowered to decide**.
