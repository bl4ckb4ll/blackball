# John L. Comaroff (1945–)

**Field:** anthropology; African and African American studies; legal and political anthropology; colonialism and postcolonialism; historical anthropology; crime, policing, and the state.

**University / institutional affiliation:** **Harvard University** from 2012 until retirement on **30 June 2024**, where he held appointments in Anthropology and African and African American Studies. Previously Harold H. Swift Distinguished Professor of Anthropology at the **University of Chicago**. Harvard-hosted profile pages still describe him as a professor and should be treated as stale against the later retirement record.

**Status checked:** 2026-08-31.

## Why this dossier is here

Comaroff is a particularly well-documented case about graduate-student dependence on an internationally prominent professor. Three Harvard graduate students — Margaret Czerwienski, Lilia Kilburn, and Amulya Mandava — alleged sexual harassment and professional retaliation and sued **Harvard**, not Comaroff, over the University's handling of their complaints.

The evidentiary record is unusually layered. Harvard itself found that some of Comaroff's verbal conduct violated its sexual-harassment and professional-conduct rules. The students alleged substantially more serious physical misconduct and retaliation. Comaroff denied those broader allegations. The civil suit ended in 2024 through a stipulated dismissal with prejudice rather than a public trial verdict on the allegations.

## Academic position and reputation

Harvard's profile describes Comaroff as an anthropologist working on Africa and the Global South, legal and political anthropology, crime and policing, colonialism and postcolonialism, and historical anthropology. Before Harvard he spent decades at the University of Chicago and held additional affiliations including the University of Cape Town and the American Bar Foundation.

His worldwide reputation and dense advising network are themselves part of the graduate students' complaint: the lawsuit argued that the professional power of a famous adviser and his network made the alleged threats to students' careers unusually consequential.

## Harvard investigations and sanctions

Harvard placed Comaroff on paid leave in 2020 while complaints were investigated. On **20 January 2022**, the Faculty of Arts and Sciences imposed sanctions after reviews by Harvard's Office for Dispute Resolution and FAS.

Harvard's own public statement is the safest concise description of what the institution actually found: the reviews concluded that Comaroff **“engaged in verbal conduct that violated the FAS Sexual and Gender-Based Harassment Policy and the FAS Professional Conduct Policy.”** FAS imposed unpaid administrative leave for spring 2022 and limitations on teaching and advising through at least the 2022–23 academic year.

This finding should not be inflated. Harvard's public statement does not say that every allegation made by the students was substantiated.

## The graduate students' lawsuit

On **8 February 2022**, Czerwienski, Kilburn, and Mandava filed a federal lawsuit against the President and Fellows of Harvard College. The complaint alleged that Harvard had failed to protect students from Comaroff's sexual harassment and retaliation.

Among the allegations, Kilburn said Comaroff subjected her to unwanted kissing and touching and made sexualized comments. Czerwienski and Mandava alleged that he threatened career consequences after they warned other students about him. The complaint also alleged that Harvard knew of concerns about Comaroff before hiring him from Chicago.

These are allegations in a civil complaint, not findings of fact. The complaint is nevertheless a primary source for exactly what the students alleged and how they described the professional-power mechanism.

## What the court later did

In March 2023, a federal judge allowed substantial portions of the students' amended complaint against Harvard to proceed while dismissing other portions. The court's discussion recounted Harvard's own finding that Comaroff had violated the sexual-harassment and professional-conduct policies; that recitation was not an independent judicial finding that all of the students' factual allegations were true.

On **14 August 2024**, the parties stipulated that the case be dismissed **with prejudice and without costs**. Reuters and the *Harvard Crimson* described the case as settled; Inside Higher Ed noted that no settlement agreement or terms had been made public. A dismissal with prejudice prevents the same claims from being refiled, but it is **not equivalent to a trial judgment on the truth or falsity of the underlying allegations**.

## Comaroff's response

Comaroff has repeatedly denied the broader allegations. In his 2024 retirement statement, he said Harvard had found him not responsible for the major accusations apart from one instance he characterized as “verbal impropriety” and a separate professional-conduct finding. He argued that the sexual-harassment finding arose from advice he gave a student about the risk of anti-LGBTQ sexual violence during field research abroad, and he described the later lawsuit and campus campaign as unfair and factually false.

That account is useful as a **party's own position**, not as an independent adjudication. It should be stored alongside Harvard's wording, the students' complaint, and the court record rather than substituted for them.

Source:

- John Comaroff, **“Statement of Professor John Comaroff Upon His Retirement,” 7 July 2024**: https://johncomaroff.com/statement-of-professor-john-comaroff-upon-his-retirement/

## Faculty support and retraction controversy

Before the lawsuit became public, dozens of academics signed an open letter questioning Harvard's sanctions and defending Comaroff. After the complaint revealed allegations not known to many signatories, a large group withdrew their support and acknowledged that their original letter could have intimidated students or discouraged disclosure.

This is relevant to Blackball because it is unusually explicit evidence of how prestige networks can function in real time: a famous scholar's colleagues intervened publicly before having access to the same factual record that later became public.

## Blackball relevance

This dossier preserves several distinctions that are easy to lose in compressed retellings:

1. the students sued Harvard, not Comaroff;
2. Harvard did make formal findings against Comaroff, but its public finding concerned verbal conduct under sexual-harassment and professional-conduct policies;
3. the students alleged broader physical misconduct and career retaliation;
4. Comaroff denied those broader allegations and disputed Harvard's interpretation of the conduct it did sanction;
5. the lawsuit ended by stipulated dismissal with prejudice, not by a public merits verdict;
6. Comaroff retired from Harvard in June 2024.

The case therefore belongs in a corpus about **academic power, advising dependence, professional networks, institutional process, and the difference between allegation, administrative finding, and court judgment**.
