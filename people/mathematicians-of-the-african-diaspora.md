# Mathematicians of the African Diaspora (MAD)

This is a website-level source note, not a proposal to create an individual dossier for every mathematician named by the site.

**Mathematicians of the African Diaspora (MAD)** was created and maintained by University at Buffalo mathematician **Scott W. Williams**. The site says it opened on **May 25, 1997** and describes its purpose as documenting mathematical accomplishments of people of Africa and the African diaspora, in part as a response to racist claims about Black mathematical ability.

Original site:

- https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/
- alternate/table-of-contents page: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/mad.html
- acknowledgements and project history: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS.html

The site is large and historically useful. Its sections include profiles of Black mathematicians, Black research mathematicians, Black women in mathematics, modern historical material, mathematicians outside North America, ancient mathematics in Africa, special articles, organizations, bibliographies, and related sources. Williams's acknowledgements describe the archive as an ongoing collaborative project assembled from published sources, correspondence, and contributions from many mathematicians and historians.

## Representative names in the archive

The archive contains hundreds of names. Examples useful for orientation include:

- **Elbert Frank Cox** — generally identified as the first Black person to earn a Ph.D. in mathematics, in 1925: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/PEEPS/cox_elbertf.html
- **David Harold Blackwell** — probability, statistics, game theory and dynamic programming; first African American elected to the National Academy of Sciences: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/PEEPS/blackwell_david.html
- **Albert Turner Bharucha-Reid** — probability, statistics and mathematical biology: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/PEEPS/bharucha-reid_a_t.html
- **Euphemia Lofton Haynes**, **Evelyn Boyd Granville**, **Marjorie Lee Browne**, and **Gloria Ford Gilmer**, discussed in the site's history of Black women in mathematics: https://math.buffalo.edu/mad/wohist
- **Vivienne Malone Mayes**, whose page contains unusually concrete testimony about segregation and racism while studying at the University of Texas at Austin: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/PEEPS/mayes_vivienne_malone.html
- **Raymond L. Johnson**, a University of Texas undergraduate who later earned his Ph.D. at Rice University and whose autobiographical page records his memories of R. L. Moore and R. H. Bing: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/PEEPS/johnson_raymondl.html

## Raymond L. Johnson on R. L. Moore and R. H. Bing

This appears to be the recollection that prompted this note.

Raymond L. Johnson studied mathematics as an undergraduate at the **University of Texas at Austin** and later earned a Ph.D. from **Rice University**. On his MAD page he recalls learning that R. L. Moore, then an enormously influential Texas topologist and teacher, treated Black students explicitly differently. Johnson says an older Black student, Walker Hunt, told him Moore would allow him into a course only on the condition that he begin with a C and could go no higher.

Johnson then gives the image of Moore that stayed with him: Moore attended a topology lecture by a student of **R. H. Bing**—hence a mathematical "grandson" of Moore—and walked out after discovering that the speaker was Black.

Johnson explicitly contrasts this with Bing. He says he had **"a very different image of R. H. Bing"** and reports a story circulating at the time that Bing would not return from Wisconsin to Texas while Moore was there.

Primary MAD sources:

- Raymond L. Johnson profile: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/PEEPS/johnson_raymondl.html
- Scott Williams's dedicated R. L. Moore page, which collects testimony by Walker E. Hunt, Raymond L. Johnson, Vivienne Malone Mayes, and Williams: https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/special/RLMoore-racist-math.html

The Moore page is especially valuable because it preserves named first-person or near-first-person recollections rather than merely asserting in retrospect that Moore was racist. Among the material it collects:

- Walker E. Hunt's account of Moore explicitly assigning him a racial handicap before he could take the course;
- Johnson's account of Moore leaving a Black mathematician's topology talk;
- Vivienne Malone Mayes's statement that Moore did not teach Black students;
- Williams's note that some of Moore's students, including Mary Ellen Rudin, treated him very well professionally. The point is not that Moore's students all reproduced Moore's racism; the site distinguishes Moore from individual members of his mathematical lineage.

## Important chronology problem in the Johnson/Bing anecdote

Johnson's page reports the story that Bing refused to return to Texas while Moore was there and adds that Moore died and Bing returned a year or so later. The **second part of that chronology is inconsistent with independently documented dates**.

Bing had already visited UT in **1971-72** and accepted a permanent position there in **1973**. Moore died on **October 4, 1974**. Thus Bing's permanent return to UT preceded Moore's death, not followed it.

Sources for the dates:

- Michael Starbird, National Academy of Sciences biographical memoir of R. H. Bing, republished by Celebratio Mathematica: https://celebratio.org/Bing_RH/article/52/
- MacTutor biography of Bing: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Bing/
- R. L. Moore papers finding aid, Briscoe Center for American History, UT Austin (death date and archival context): https://txarchives.org/utcah/finding_aids/00304.xml

This does **not** establish that the underlying story about Bing's attitude toward Moore is false. Johnson introduces it as **"Word was"**, i.e. as something reported in the mathematical community, not as something Bing personally told him. But the archive should preserve the recollection **and** flag the chronological mismatch rather than silently converting an oral-history claim into an established fact.

There is independent biographical evidence consistent with Johnson's favorable impression of Bing. Starbird's National Academy memoir describes Bing as treating people kindly and respecting them on their own merits. That evidence supports a contrast in personal reputation, but it is not independent confirmation of the specific claim that Bing stayed away from Texas because of Moore.

There is also an interesting complication: Bing spoke warmly about Moore's mathematics and teaching at the 1973 dedication of Robert Lee Moore Hall. Bing's remarks are preserved by the Legacy of R. L. Moore project:

- https://legacyrlmoore.org/reference/bing.html

So the historical relationship should not be flattened into a simple personal feud. Bing could value Moore as a mathematical teacher while differing sharply from him in conduct or judgment. The MAD recollection is evidence about how Johnson and others perceived that contrast; it is not by itself a complete biography of Bing or Moore.

## Vivienne Malone Mayes as corroborating context

Mayes's MAD profile independently supplies strong evidence of discriminatory conditions at UT. It says she was the only Black person and only woman in her first class, was socially isolated by classmates, was denied a teaching assistantship despite extensive teaching experience, could not join mathematical discussions at a segregated cafe, and could not enroll in Moore's class because he did not teach Black students.

Source:

- https://www.math.buffalo.edu/mad/PEEPS/mayes_vivienne_malone.html

This matters because Johnson's recollection is not an isolated accusation detached from the institutional setting. MAD preserves several overlapping accounts of explicit racial exclusion in the same department and period.

## Why MAD belongs in `people/`

MAD is useful to this repository less as a source for creating hundreds of automatic people dossiers than as a **map of people, testimony, bibliographies, and leads**. It contains material that is easy to lose when later biographies compress careers into degrees, positions and prizes—especially first-person recollections of exclusion, mentoring, hiring barriers, professional networks, and the different reputations of people inside the same mathematical lineage.

For future use, individual dossiers should be created selectively when there is enough material to justify one. MAD should remain cited as a source archive in its own right.
