# Carlo Natali (1948–)

Italian historian of ancient philosophy and honorary professor at Ca' Foscari University of Venice. Natali is especially important to Blackball because his work on Aristotle is not merely doctrinal commentary: it reconstructs Aristotle's life, the institutional character of the Peripatos, the evidence for teaching and research there, and the limits of the surviving biographical tradition.

His 1991 *Bios theoretikos. La vita di Aristotele e l'organizzazione della sua scuola*, substantially revised for the 2013 English edition *Aristotle: His Life and School*, is a first-stop source for questions such as **what Aristotle's "school" actually was, who belonged to it, what happened there, and whether modern categories such as professor, university, student, or enrollment can safely be projected backward.**

## Why this dossier is here

Blackball currently needs a defensible way to talk about the scale and social character of Aristotle's Lyceum without manufacturing a modern student body from fragmentary ancient evidence.

Natali matters because his central interpretation pushes in almost the opposite direction from the easy modern analogy. In the reconstruction summarized by Michael J. Griffin, Natali treats the Peripatetic school not primarily as an institution for the *paideia* of the young, but as a durable setting in which a **small community** could practice the *bios theoretikos* — an intellectual way of life made possible by *scholē*, freedom or leisure, and by philosophizing together.

That interpretation is directly relevant to any attempted claim such as "x% of Athenian citizens attended the Lyceum." Before producing such a percentage, the numerator has to be defined. At minimum Blackball must keep separate:

- the core community of philosophers associated with Aristotle;
- named pupils, collaborators, successors, or other Peripatetics;
- people who heard public lectures;
- people who used the Lyceum gymnasium or surrounding public space;
- later members of the Peripatetic school after Aristotle's death;
- people called "students" only by modern secondary sources.

Those populations are not interchangeable. Natali's work helps define the institutional problem, but the sources inspected so far do **not** provide a synchronized enrollment census from which a clean percentage of Athenian citizens can simply be read off.

## Career chronology

The following chronology comes primarily from Natali's Ca' Foscari curriculum page. The university states that the profile information was supplied by the person to whom the page refers, so it is a strong source for how Natali records his own career but is not independent biographical verification.

- **1948:** born in Perugia on 5 January.
- **1971:** graduated in philosophy from the University of Perugia.
- **1974–1981:** assistant at the University of Padua.
- **1976–1979:** taught history of ancient philosophy at the University of Calabria.
- **1979–1981:** taught history of ancient philosophy at the University of Siena.
- **1981–1999:** associate professor of history of ancient philosophy at the University of Venice.
- **1999–2001:** full professor of history of philosophy at Ca' Foscari University of Venice.
- **2001–2018:** full professor of history of ancient philosophy at Ca' Foscari.
- **2006–2009:** directed the philosophy curriculum at Ca' Foscari.
- **2009–2015:** coordinated doctoral studies in philosophy.
- **2010–2011:** directed Ca' Foscari's Doctoral School of Humanities.
- **2012–2015:** president of the Società Italiana di Storia della Filosofia Antica (SISFA).
- **2018–:** listed by Ca' Foscari as an honorary professor.

His CV also records research stays or fellowships at Clare Hall, Cambridge (1995), the Institute for Advanced Studies in the Humanities in Edinburgh (2005/2006 in the available profiles), and All Souls College, Oxford (2010), plus the Tópicos chair at Universidad Panamericana in Mexico City (2018).

The Centre Léon Robin independently describes him as a former Ca' Foscari professor of ancient philosophy and records the Cambridge, Edinburgh, Oxford, SISFA, editorial, and Aristotle-research connections.

## Research field

Natali's own university profile describes his main subjects as:

- Aristotle's ethics, metaphysics, and rhetoric;
- the history and organization of ancient philosophical schools;
- Xenophon;
- ancient debates over causality and determinism;
- the relationship between Aristotle's theory of science and his ethics.

This specialization matters for weighting his claims. Natali has worked for decades on Aristotle and on ancient philosophical institutions, so his reconstruction of the Lyceum deserves serious attention. It does **not** follow that every claim he makes about every part of Aristotle's corpus is equally secure; Robert Mayhew's review gives a useful counterexample in biology.

## *Aristotle: His Life and School*

### Publication history

The book began as *Bios theoretikos* in 1991. The English edition was not a routine translation. D. S. Hutchinson's editorial work involved retranslating the ancient source passages from newer critical editions, comparing them with Natali's readings, expanding the source index, updating the bibliography, and adding a postscript covering scholarship published after the Italian edition.

Michael J. Griffin therefore describes the 2013 version as effectively an updated and improved second edition. Tiziano Dorandi makes the same point in *Bryn Mawr Classical Review*.

### Structure

The 2013 edition is organized around four problems:

1. Aristotle's biography;
2. institutional aspects of Aristotle's school;
3. the internal organization of the school;
4. the modern historiography of Aristotle's biography.

For Blackball, chapters 2–3 are the most directly relevant to questions about the Lyceum as a social and educational institution.

### Source method

A major strength of Natali's book is that it does not pretend the surviving evidence is homogeneous.

The ancient evidence for Aristotle's life includes hostile and favorable contemporaneous traditions, later biographical material, official or quasi-official material preserved only indirectly, Neoplatonist lives, stories transmitted by much later authors, Aristotle's own works, and evidence about later Peripatetics. Natali follows the source-critical advance associated especially with Ingemar Düring: identify the provenance and agenda of each testimony before combining it into a life.

Hutchinson's work on the English edition strengthened that source layer by independently retranslating the ancient passages from current critical texts. This makes the English edition unusually useful as a map into the primary material. It still does not make later testimonia contemporary records.

## Natali's model of the Peripatos

Natali's distinctive thesis is that Aristotle's school should be understood around the **bios theoretikos**, not by retrofitting a modern university model.

As summarized by Griffin:

- the theoretical life was a personal way of organizing an aristocratic Greek life around *scholē*;
- the Peripatos was not primarily an institution for educating the young;
- it provided a durable setting for *sumphilosophein*, "philosophizing together";
- down through Lyco, it functioned as a foundation supporting a relatively small philosophical community.

Natali also gives substantial weight to Theophrastus' will for reconstructing the material and institutional continuity of the school. Aristotle's own legal position in Athens matters: as a resident alien, he could not simply be treated as the proprietor of a modern private college.

This interpretation is important but should remain an interpretation. "Aristocratic ideal" does **not** by itself establish that every associate was wealthy, that each owned slaves, or that all participants belonged to the Athenian citizen elite. Those are separate empirical questions.

## "Professor", "student", and "school" are hazardous translations

Natali's 1991 article "Aristotele professore?" is itself a warning sign against easy modernization.

A future Blackball answer should therefore avoid an unqualified sentence such as "Aristotle had N students at his university." Each term silently imports institutions that may not fit fourth-century Athens.

The same problem applies to "attended the Lyceum." The Lyceum was also a physical and civic place. Being present there, hearing Aristotle speak, belonging to Aristotle's philosophical circle, collaborating in research, and being a later Peripatetic are different relations.

A useful quantitative project must first decide which relation is being counted.

## Scholarly reception

### Michael J. Griffin, *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews* (2014)

Griffin calls the book a standard-setting philosophical biography and says it has no real competition in English. He especially praises Natali's handling of the fragmentary biographical evidence, his analysis of philosophical life as an institution, and the 2013 edition's improved source apparatus.

For Blackball, Griffin's summary is particularly useful because it identifies the claim that the Peripatos was a **small community** and distinguishes its purpose from the education of the young.

### Tiziano Dorandi, *Bryn Mawr Classical Review* (2013)

Dorandi describes the 1991 Italian book, together with Natali's earlier work on philosophical schools, as indispensable for the history of ancient philosophical schools. He treats the English version as a genuine second edition rather than a mere translation and highlights the chapters on the institutional and internal organization of the Lyceum.

### Robert Mayhew, *Aestimatio* (2015)

Mayhew agrees that the book should serve as a modern standard biography and praises its treatment of the evidentiary limits of Aristotle's life and school. But he also supplies an important domain-specific criticism.

Mayhew regards Natali's treatment of Aristotle's biology as the book's one fundamental flaw. He rejects, among other things, Natali's characterization of Aristotle's zoological work as relying primarily on written sources rather than observation and objects to the comparison that makes Theophrastus look more like an empirical scientist than Aristotle.

This is useful Blackball discipline: **Natali can be a first-rank guide to the biographical and institutional evidence without becoming an all-purpose authority on every Aristotelian subject.**

## What Natali can and cannot establish for a Lyceum headcount

### Supported uses

Natali is strong evidence for:

- the categories and institutional distinctions that a headcount must respect;
- the surviving source types and their biases;
- the centrality of Theophrastus' will and later biographical traditions;
- the importance of collective philosophical life rather than a modern enrollment model;
- the existence of teaching, books, research instruments, data collection, diagrams, lectures, and other organized intellectual practices;
- the historiography of earlier attempts to reconstruct Aristotle's life and school.

### Not yet supported

The material inspected for this dossier does **not** yet justify:

- an exact enrollment count for Aristotle's Lyceum;
- a percentage of Athenian citizens who "attended";
- a claim that the known named Peripatetics exhaust the audience;
- a claim that every participant was an Athenian citizen;
- a claim that every participant was aristocratic, wealthy, or a slave owner;
- a claim that public lecture audiences were identical with the philosophical community.

Those questions require another source-extraction pass, not a conversion of Natali's prestige into a number.

## Class and family background

Natali's professional biography is well documented. His own family wealth, parents' occupations, childhood class position, and the economics of his education were **not established in this pass**.

Do not infer class origin from the fact that he attended the University of Perugia, became a professor, or later held visiting positions at Cambridge and Oxford.

## Retrieval queue

1. Inspect *Aristotle: His Life and School* pp. 72–119 directly and extract every statement bearing on:
   - named members;
   - pupils or associates;
   - public lectures and their audiences;
   - ownership or use of buildings and gardens;
   - the meaning of *Peripatos* and *Lykeion*;
   - teaching versus research;
   - any explicit or implicit numerical evidence.
2. For every candidate headcount item, record the ancient source Natali cites rather than stopping at Natali.
3. Build separate rosters for:
   - Aristotle-era associates;
   - Theophrastus-era associates;
   - later Peripatetics;
   - named outsiders or occasional hearers.
4. Compare Natali with:
   - Ingemar Düring, *Aristotle in the Ancient Biographical Tradition* (1957);
   - John P. Lynch, *Aristotle's School: A Study of a Greek Educational Institution* (1972);
   - later work on the archaeology and civic use of the Lyceum.
5. Only after the numerator is defined, pair it with a defensible denominator for the relevant Athenian population: citizens, adult male citizens, residents, or total inhabitants are different denominators.
6. Treat social-class and slave-ownership claims member by member. Natali's *bios theoretikos* interpretation supplies a hypothesis about leisure and social structure, not a household census.

## Confidence

**High confidence:** birth date and place, education, academic appointments, research fields, major publications, honorary-professor status, and the broad structure and reception of *Aristotle: His Life and School*.

**High confidence about the evidentiary warning:** the modern categories "student", "professor", "university", and "enrollment" cannot simply be assumed when quantifying Aristotle's Lyceum; Natali's institutional reconstruction makes that especially clear.

**Medium confidence / interpretive:** Natali's model of the Peripatos as the institutional expression of an aristocratic *bios theoretikos*. It is a major scholarly interpretation with strong favorable reception, not a primary-source fact.

**Unknown:** a defensible exact Aristotle-era Lyceum attendance count; the social-class distribution of participants; Natali's own family/class background.
