# Carlo Natali (1948–)

Italian historian of ancient philosophy and honorary professor at Ca' Foscari University of Venice. Natali is especially important to Blackball because his work does not consist only of commentary on Aristotle's doctrines. Across five decades he has worked on Aristotle's theology, method, practical wisdom, theory of action, determinism, biography, school, household texts, ethics, and reception, while repeatedly asking what kind of evidence justifies a historical or philosophical reconstruction.

His 1991 *Bios theoretikos. La vita di Aristotele e l'organizzazione della sua scuola*, substantially revised for the 2013 English edition *Aristotle: His Life and School*, remains the most immediately relevant work for Blackball's current questions about Aristotle's Lyceum. But it makes more sense when placed beside Natali's earlier edited volume on Aristotle's school, his work on practical reasoning and action, his translation/commentary projects, and his later argument about scientific method in the *Nicomachean Ethics*.

## Why this dossier is here

Blackball needs a defensible way to talk about the scale, social character, intellectual work, and institutional organization of Aristotle's Lyceum without manufacturing a modern student body from fragmentary ancient evidence.

Natali matters for that problem because his central interpretation pushes against the easy modern analogy. In the reconstruction summarized by Michael J. Griffin, Natali treats the Peripatetic school not primarily as an institution for the *paideia* of the young, but as a durable setting in which a relatively small community could practice the *bios theoretikos*: an intellectual way of life made possible by *scholē*, freedom or leisure, and by philosophizing together.

That interpretation is directly relevant to an attempted claim such as "x% of Athenian citizens attended the Lyceum." Before producing such a percentage, the numerator has to be defined. At minimum Blackball must keep separate:

- the core community of philosophers associated with Aristotle;
- named pupils, collaborators, successors, and other Peripatetics;
- people who heard lectures;
- people who used the Lyceum gymnasium or surrounding public space;
- later members of the Peripatetic school after Aristotle's death;
- people called "students" only by modern secondary sources.

Those populations are not interchangeable.

## Research field

Natali's university profile describes his main subjects as:

- Aristotle's ethics, metaphysics, and rhetoric;
- the history and organization of ancient philosophical schools;
- Xenophon;
- ancient debates over causality and determinism;
- the relationship between Aristotle's theory of science and his ethics.

The books show that list to be narrower than his actual historical range. His early work also includes Marx, Hegelian readings of Aristotle, Galvano Della Volpe, and the history of the principle of non-contradiction. Household management and ancient "economic" literature form another recurrent line.

## Book-by-book argument map

### 1. *Cosmo e divinità. La struttura logica della teologia aristotelica* (1974)

This is Natali's first monograph, published at L'Aquila by Japadre. Its importance is not simply that it is "about Aristotle's theology." The contemporary reviews describe it as a methodological intervention against a dominant way of reading Aristotle.

#### The problem

A large twentieth-century literature, under the influence of developmental or "genetic" interpretations, tried to reconstruct stages in Aristotle's thought: early Platonism, later independence from Plato, and changes in theology or metaphysics across time. Lost works and hypothetical chronology often carried much of the argument.

Natali deliberately changes the question. Instead of asking first how Aristotle's theology developed, he asks what logical and explanatory structure the extant corpus actually gives to talk about divine substance.

#### Texts examined

Reviews report sustained analysis of:

- *Metaphysics*, especially Book Lambda;
- *Physics* VIII;
- *De caelo*;
- *De motu animalium*;
- the ethical works more briefly;
- an appendix on the *Magna Moralia*.

André Wartelle's review describes the *Metaphysics* discussion as occupying roughly pp. 45–102 of the book.

#### Central thesis

The striking thesis, as reported independently by Goldschmidt and Wartelle, is that Aristotle does **not** have an autonomous "theology" in the sense of a separate science whose proper object is God. Aristotle certainly argues for eternal unmoved substance and attributes divinity to it. But the divine appears as a culminating explanatory requirement inside investigations whose subject matter is broader: motion, substance, being, cosmos.

That distinction matters. "Aristotle has a doctrine of divine substance" is not the same claim as "Aristotle founded a discipline of theology analogous to later metaphysics or theology."

#### Historiographical significance

Richard Bodéüs later praised the book for turning away from speculative genetic reconstruction and toward an interpretation of the extant corpus as a structured whole. This does not mean Natali denies every chronological problem. It means chronology cannot do the explanatory work that close analysis of the surviving arguments should do.

This is already a recognizable Natali method: identify the question imposed by later historiography, return to the actual corpus, separate the structure of Aristotle's argument from later disciplinary labels, and resist pretending that a modern institution or category already existed in Aristotle's world.

#### Blackball use

The same habit later becomes crucial for the Lyceum. "Theology," "university," "professor," and "student" can all be retrospective categories that organize evidence helpfully or distort it badly. Natali's first monograph already shows suspicion of that substitution.

### 2. *La scuola dei filosofi. Scienza ed organizzazione istituzionale della scuola di Aristotele* (1981, editor)

This edited volume is an important missing link between Natali's early philosophy work and *Bios theoretikos*. It is not a sole-authored Natali monograph. Its importance lies in the evidence packet Natali assembled around the institutional history of Aristotle's school.

The indexed contents include:

- Natali, introduction, p. 7;
- Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff on philosophical schools, politics, and legal status, p. 29;
- Henry Jackson on Aristotle's classroom and lectures, p. 47;
- Werner Jaeger on publication of Aristotle's treatises, p. 55;
- Fritz Wehrli on the Peripatos down to the first century BCE, p. 69;
- Ingemar Düring, comments and conclusions, p. 97;
- John P. Lynch on the Lyceum in Aristotle's time, p. 107;
- H. B. Gottschalk on the wills of Peripatetic scholarchs, p. 129.

The appendices are almost a research agenda by themselves. The indexed subjects include:

- the physical Lyceum;
- philosophers and theatre;
- the law associated with Sophocles of Sunium concerning philosophical schools;
- data about the school;
- Aristotle and his library;
- honorific inscriptions;
- organization after Aristotle;
- polemics involving the school;
- a dossier of Aristotle passages.

That structure matters more than a generic description such as "a book about Aristotle's school." Natali is putting legal status, property, wills, books, lectures, physical location, public regulation, and internal intellectual work into one institutional question.

#### Relation to *Bios theoretikos*

The 1991 book should therefore not be treated as a sudden isolated biography. Natali had already spent at least a decade assembling the school as a historical institution from several incompatible-looking evidence classes.

For Blackball, *La scuola dei filosofi* is probably one of the most useful source maps for the headcount problem because it makes clear which kinds of records might bear on membership and which only bear on institutional existence.

### 3. Xenophon, *L'amministrazione della casa (Economico)* (1988; second edition 1989; later reissued)

This is Xenophon's *Oeconomicus*. It must not be confused with Natali's later 1995 edition of the pseudo-Aristotelian *Oeconomica*.

A detailed scholarly review describes Natali's edition as containing:

- an introduction, pp. 9–50;
- Greek text facing the Italian translation;
- a Greek text based on E. C. Marchant's Oxford edition, with translation-level departures where Natali judged necessary;
- notes, pp. 211–246.

#### Natali's interpretive caution

The reviewer reports Natali insisting that the ancient *oikonomia* at issue should not simply be called modern "political economy" or treated as if *oikos* already meant a capitalist firm.

That caution is historically sensible. Xenophon's work concerns household, property, agriculture, command, gendered division of labor, slavery, status, and management under a social order very different from modern capitalism.

#### A useful disagreement

Reviewer Leonardo Paganelli pushes in the opposite direction. He thinks Natali's translation and interpretation can understate the managerial and productive character of Ischomachus' household. Paganelli emphasizes:

- Ischomachus as an aristocratic proprietor but also an organizer;
- the household as a productive unit;
- management, supervision, efficiency, and surplus;
- the danger that very general household vocabulary can hide something structurally closer to enterprise management.

This dispute is useful precisely because neither modern word solves it automatically. "Household" can make the operation sound purely domestic; "business" can make it sound capitalist. The real question is what labor, property, command, accounting, production, sale, and dependency actually occur in Xenophon's text.

#### Blackball use

This edition belongs in Blackball's class-and-work lane. It is directly relevant to questions about elite leisure because the leisure of a philosophical household cannot be understood without asking who performs agricultural, domestic, administrative, and enslaved labor.

### 4. *La saggezza di Aristotele* (1989) / *The Wisdom of Aristotle* (2001)

The English edition is described by Ca' Foscari as a complete revision and English translation of the 1989 Italian book, not merely a literal translation.

Its table of contents gives the argument's progression:

1. **Virtue or Science?**
2. **Ends and Means**
3. **The Practical Syllogism**
4. **Aristotle's Conception of Happiness**
5. conclusion and later afterword.

The fourth chapter contains discussions of the Academy's accounts of happiness, happiness as a way of life, the *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7 definition, and the relation of later passages in NE X and the *Eudemian Ethics*.

#### Chapter 1: practical wisdom is not detached theoretical knowledge

Natali's subject is *phronēsis*, practical wisdom. The problem is not simply whether a person "knows the good." The practically wise person has an intellectual capacity that is structurally joined to character and desire.

A page-indexed reading record of the English edition reports Natali's pp. 12–16 argument as follows:

- practical truth cannot be modeled on scientific truth alone;
- good desire is part of successful practical cognition;
- the "right reason" involved in virtue is not best understood as a universal rule mechanically applied to cases;
- it is the reasoning activity of practical wisdom in the particular situation.

This is important because it blocks a simple picture of Aristotle as issuing a moral algorithm.

#### Chapter 2: ends and means

Natali then analyzes deliberation. The relevant structure is not "reason chooses an ultimate end from nowhere and then computes." Rather, character/desire supplies the orientation toward ends while deliberative reason identifies what is to be done here and now.

The same page-indexed notes report a strong formulation around pp. 96–97: deliberation can transmit desirability from an end to a proposed means. Once a course of action is understood as the route to a desired end, the agent can come to desire that action itself.

So deliberation changes how a concrete option appears to the agent. It is not merely arithmetic over fixed preferences.

#### Chapter 3: the practical syllogism

The practical syllogism is therefore not simply a formal syllogism whose conclusion happens to be an action. Natali treats it as one component in a larger psychology of action involving perception of the particular, desire, end, and reasoning about what promotes the end.

A scholarly review of the book praises Natali for moving among *Topics*, *Metaphysics*, *Eudemian Ethics*, and *Nicomachean Ethics* without pretending every occurrence of the same term has exactly the same function.

That is a methodological strength: he reconstructs an Aristotelian theory from dispersed texts while repeatedly asking how much weight each local passage can bear.

#### Chapter 4: happiness and the theoretical life

The book then asks how *phronēsis* fits into Aristotle's final account of happiness, especially the notorious elevation of *theōria* in NE X.

Natali resists a reading in which intellectual contemplation simply cancels moral life. The page-indexed notes report him arguing around pp. 149–151 that the "most perfect virtue" cannot simply be equated with *theōria* alone; the excellences of the rational soul include practical and theoretical wisdom, and neither is valuable merely as an instrument for producing something else.

This is one bridge to *Bios theoretikos*: theoretical life remains privileged, but Natali is not depicting it as a license for amorality or as an intellectual activity detached from the rest of human excellence.

#### Reception

A contemporary review in *Ancient Philosophy* calls the book a model of careful scholarship and emphasizes four components:

- virtue and knowledge;
- means and ends;
- practical syllogism;
- happiness.

The reviewer especially praises Natali's care with context and with the systematic ambiguity of Aristotle's vocabulary.

### 5. *Bios theoretikos. La vita di Aristotele e l'organizzazione della sua scuola* (1991) / *Aristotle: His Life and School* (2013)

This is the work most directly relevant to the current Lyceum research.

The 2013 English edition, edited by D. S. Hutchinson, is not a routine translation. Hutchinson describes retranslating the ancient testimonia from current critical editions, comparing them with Natali's construals, expanding the source index, and updating the secondary literature. Griffin therefore treats the English version as an updated second edition.

#### Chapter 1: biography as graded evidence

The first chapter covers:

- Stagira;
- Aristotle's family;
- the Academy;
- time with Hermias of Atarneus;
- Macedonia and Alexander;
- Callisthenes;
- return to Athens;
- Aristotle's final departure;
- the emergence of a new model of intellectual life.

The method is as important as the chronology. The evidence comes from hostile testimony, apologetic testimony, later biographical traditions, Neoplatonist lives, Aristotle's own texts, and indirect preservation of earlier material. Natali does not give them equal weight.

Griffin highlights this as one of the book's strengths: the reader repeatedly sees the gap between what can be said, what is probable, and what later biographers have merely made vivid.

#### Chapters 2–3: the school as an institution

The second chapter asks what kind of legal or institutional object a philosophical school was. The third asks how the school actually worked.

Themes include:

- whether philosophical schools should be treated as associations or foundations;
- property and Aristotle's status as a metic in Athens;
- Theophrastus' will;
- a place for *sumphilosophein*, philosophizing together;
- collections of books;
- collection and interpretation of data;
- diagrams, lists, and other research/teaching aids;
- lectures and teaching;
- the story of "teaching while walking";
- continuity under later scholarchs.

Natali's answer is not "Aristotle founded a university." His distinctive claim is that the institution supported a particular *way of life*.

#### The *bios theoretikos* thesis

As Griffin summarizes Natali:

- theoretical life is not a profession in the modern Weberian sense;
- the school is not primarily a mechanism for educating a mass of young students;
- it supports a small community able to spend *scholē* on philosophy;
- Aristotle's ethical description of theoretical life has an autobiographical/institutional dimension.

This is where the class question enters. The ideal requires time not absorbed by subsistence labor. That is a social fact worth investigating. It does **not** by itself prove the exact wealth, citizenship, household labor force, or slave ownership of every member.

#### Chapter 4: the biographies of Aristotle are themselves historical objects

Natali closes by reviewing modern Aristotle biography from Zeller through Wilamowitz, Jaeger, Düring, and others.

This matters because "Aristotle" as a historical character has been repeatedly reconstructed to match different intellectual pictures:

- political Aristotle;
- developmental Aristotle;
- scientific Aristotle;
- founder-of-the-modern-university Aristotle;
- philosophical-way-of-life Aristotle.

Natali is not outside that history: his own *bios theoretikos* is another interpretation. The virtue of the book is that it makes much of the evidence and historiographical machinery visible enough to challenge.

#### Important criticism: Aristotle's biology

Robert Mayhew praises the book overall and agrees that it should function as a standard biography. But he calls its treatment of Aristotle's biology the book's fundamental flaw.

Mayhew specifically objects to Natali's suggestion that:

- Aristotle's *History of Animals* relied primarily on written sources rather than observation;
- Theophrastus can be presented as more of an empirical scientist than Aristotle.

This is a useful limit. Natali's expertise in biography, ethics, and philosophical institutions does not turn every biological claim into settled evidence.

### 6. "Aristotele professore?" (1991)

This article in *Phronesis* 36, pp. 61–73, sits beside *Bios theoretikos*. Its title alone should not be used as evidence for a conclusion, but its existence signals that Natali explicitly examined the danger of projecting the modern "professor" category backward.

Until the article itself is directly inspected, Blackball should not paraphrase its argument more strongly than the book and later scholarship warrant.

It belongs in the retrieval queue because the modern-professor analogy is exactly what a casual Lyceum headcount tends to presuppose.

### 7. pseudo-Aristotle, *L'amministrazione della casa (Economico)* (1995)

This is a separate book from the Xenophon translation.

Natali's 1995 Laterza edition contains:

- translation and commentary on the pseudo-Aristotelian *Oeconomica*;
- related Neopythagorean texts;
- what the bibliographic record describes as the first Italian translation of Bryson's *Oeconomicus*.

The pairing is intellectually revealing. Natali is not treating "ancient economics" as one doctrine. He is assembling a tradition of household-management texts in which authorship, genre, philosophical school, domestic hierarchy, property, and normative management differ.

A deeper source dossier should eventually compare the Xenophon and pseudo-Aristotle editions directly, especially on:

- husband/wife hierarchy;
- slave management;
- property;
- agriculture and exchange;
- household surplus;
- relation between *oikonomia* and politics.

### 8. Alexander of Aphrodisias, *Il destino / De fato* (1996; revised edition 2009)

Natali's edition of Alexander's *De fato* includes translation, introduction, commentary, and Greek text in the later edition. It belongs with his work on Aristotle's action theory rather than being an isolated translation job.

#### The philosophical problem

The Hellenistic and imperial debate over fate asks whether a rational causal order makes events necessary and, if so, what becomes of:

- deliberation;
- alternative possibilities;
- praise and blame;
- responsibility;
- agency.

Alexander is one of the major ancient anti-determinist witnesses working in an Aristotelian framework.

#### Natali's later interpretation

In later work on Alexander, Natali argues that Alexander adapts Aristotelian theories of deliberation and action to a philosophical environment transformed by Stoic determinism.

That adaptation itself is historically significant. A later Peripatetic is not merely repeating Aristotle. He is selecting and recoding Aristotelian material because a new problem has become central.

For Blackball, this is another reason not to treat "Aristotelian view" as a timeless block extending through the school.

### 9. Aristotle, *Etica Nicomachea* (1999)

Natali's Laterza edition presents Greek text facing an Italian translation, with introduction and notes. The publisher states that the Greek text is based on Franz Susemihl and Otto Apelt.

The edition is important for Natali's later work because *Il metodo e il trattato* explicitly says its central problem arose while Natali was working on this translation.

That gives a real research sequence:

**translation problem → structural problem → methodological hypothesis → 2017 monograph.**

This is different from writing a general book and then finding examples to support it.

The edition should not be called a new critical edition merely because a repository metadata category uses language associated with critical texts. The safer description is: Greek-facing translation and commentary based on named prior Greek editions, with Natali's own interpretive decisions.

### 10. *L'action efficace. Études sur la philosophie de l'action d'Aristote* (2004)

This French volume gathers essays written mainly between 1985 and 2002. Lloyd Gerson remarks that the essays have enough thematic unity to read like chapters of a single book.

Its major lines are:

- how Aristotle studies human action;
- action versus movement;
- action versus production;
- human action versus divine *energeia*;
- action and pleasure;
- *eupraxia*, successful or good action;
- determinism, autonomy, and responsibility.

#### Method: begin with rich actions, not minimal motions

Natali argues that Aristotle typically works with complex paradigmatic actions rather than deliberately stripped-down modern examples such as raising an arm.

Gerson gives the contrast:

- a minimal analytic example makes boundary conditions easier to isolate;
- Aristotle's richer example—such as defending one's city—shows end, perception, character, means, context, and normative structure at once.

Natali thinks that is deliberate. The object is not the semantic extension of the word "action"; it is the intelligible structure of a human praxis.

#### Action, movement, production

Natali uses *Metaphysics* IX.6 and related texts to distinguish:

- natural movement;
- production;
- action;
- pure activity.

On the account reconstructed by Daniel Devereux, movement and production reach their end at completion. Action, by contrast, is supposed to have its end present in the acting itself, while divine contemplation gives the limiting case of pure activity.

Human action is therefore neither mere bodily movement nor pure divine activity.

#### Devereux's criticism

Devereux presses hard on this point. In the ethical works Aristotle often says actions are performed for further ends, and virtuous activity can succeed or fail at attaining an intended result.

Devereux also thinks Natali understates a sharper Aristotelian criterion: *prohairesis*, choice. Aristotle repeatedly excludes children and nonhuman animals from action in the strict sense because they lack the relevant deliberative choice.

This criticism matters because it changes the ontology:

- Natali emphasizes paradigms and graded structure;
- Devereux argues Aristotle gives a more definite boundary through rational choice and character.

#### Freedom and responsibility

Natali rejects a simple deterministic picture partly because Aristotelian explanation uses heterogeneous causes—formal, final, efficient—not only one homogeneous chain of efficient causes.

Devereux responds that heterogeneous causation alone cannot establish human freedom because animal behavior can also receive multi-causal Aristotelian explanations.

The stronger freedom question therefore turns on whether the action originates in the agent's own deliberative choice and whether alternatives were genuinely open.

This is a substantive dispute, not a decorative "reception" paragraph. It pinpoints where Natali's reconstruction can be challenged from Aristotle's own texts.

### 11. *Aristotle: Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII: Symposium Aristotelicum* (2009, editor)

Natali edited this chapter-by-chapter scholarly commentary on NE VII, with contributors including John Cooper, David Charles, Hendrik Lorenz, Christopher Bobonich, Sarah Broadie, Teun Tieleman, Dorothea Frede, Christof Rapp, and Gwenaëlle Aubry.

Natali also wrote the introduction and one of the substantive chapters.

#### Natali's structural problem

His introduction emphasizes a basic peculiarity: the transmitted Book VII appears to contain two different *logoi*:

- VII.1–10: *akrasia* and related bad states;
- VII.11–14: pleasure.

Each has its own opening and argumentative structure, and the connection between them is not announced the way a modern editor might expect.

That is another example of Natali treating book order and composition as evidence rather than assuming a perfectly designed textbook.

#### Natali's own chapter

His chapter on 1148b15–1150a8 distinguishes:

- beastliness and beastly forms of akrasia;
- akrasia involving *thumos*, anger;
- comparison of the forms by badness.

Natali argues that modern chapter divisions can obscure Aristotle's actual argumentative units.

#### Editorial limit

A *Bryn Mawr Classical Review* reviewer praises the volume but wishes for a larger synthetic discussion of how Book VII fits into the *Nicomachean Ethics*, the *Eudemian Ethics*, and the relation between the akrasia and pleasure sections.

That is a useful warning: a sequence of excellent local commentaries is not automatically a theory of the whole book.

### 12. *Aristotele* (2014)

This is Natali's mature general introduction to Aristotle, about 370 pages. It is much more revealing than a generic "survey."

A long review quotes Natali's own metaphor: the book is intended roughly like a serious guide to a great art city. It should tell a reader where the important structures are, what to look at, where the damaged or disputed parts lie, and how the pieces relate without pretending to replace specialist monographs.

#### Architecture

The book proceeds through:

1. life and works;
2. logic and method;
3. the physical world;
4. first philosophy / metaphysics;
5. practical philosophy;
6. rhetoric and poetics.

The detailed table of contents includes questions often erased in textbook summaries:

- authenticity and relative chronology of the *Organon*;
- science versus dialectical method;
- textual problems of the *Metaphysics*;
- whether first philosophy is a unified science;
- freedom before a developed concept of "will";
- best constitution versus realizable constitutions;
- reforms and revolutions;
- argument, passion, character, and topoi in rhetoric.

#### Mature methodological thesis: *Posterior Analytics* matters everywhere

The most important interpretive feature highlighted by Marco Storni's review is Natali's insistence that the method of the *Posterior Analytics* is not a classroom theory disconnected from Aristotle's actual sciences.

Natali emphasizes the sequence in *Posterior Analytics* II:

1. establish that the object exists;
2. identify characteristic facts or features;
3. reach the object's nature or essence in a definition.

The review traces Natali applying this pattern across:

- *De caelo*;
- *Meteorologica*;
- *De anima*;
- *Metaphysics*, including the substance books;
- practical philosophy;
- the treatment of passions in *Rhetoric*.

Natali does **not** simply abolish dialectic. He argues that dialectical and analytic/scientific procedures have different roles. A highly general inquiry such as the opening of the *Physics* may need endoxa and dialectical testing because its principles are not directly available through ordinary perception. More determinate sciences can rely more heavily on observed phenomena.

This is the mature version of a theme that becomes the explicit subject of the 2017 book.

#### An important change in Natali's own scholarship

This matters biographically as well as philosophically. Natali later says he himself had once accepted a more strongly "dialectical" picture of Aristotle's practical method. His later work changes under the influence of research on Aristotle's biology and scientific method.

So the dossier should not represent "Natali's view" as frozen from 1974 to 2026. His own interpretive framework changes.

### 13. *Il metodo e il trattato. Saggio sull'Etica Nicomachea* (2017)

Natali states the originating problem himself: while translating the *Nicomachean Ethics*, he became dissatisfied with his understanding of the order and structure of the treatise.

The monograph proposes two explicit theses.

#### Thesis 1: audience

Much of the *Nicomachean Ethics* is a *logos* directed to a relatively broad audience that is not expert in philosophy.

Natali's evidence includes Aristotle's effort to guide the audience step by step while often leaving implicit the deeper relations between ethical claims and Aristotle's physics or philosophy of science.

That means accessibility of presentation should not be confused with theoretical isolation.

#### Thesis 2: method

Natali rejects the influential claim that the whole treatise is organized according to the method described at NE VII.1, 1145b2–7—set out appearances, work through puzzles, preserve the reputable opinions.

Instead, he argues that much of the work follows the definition-discovery method of *Posterior Analytics* II.1–8.

#### Chapter 1: can the ethics be a unified treatise?

Natali begins with the historical oddity that there was no obvious pre-Aristotelian model of a systematic ethical treatise comparable to what the *Nicomachean Ethics* becomes.

A collection of moral maxims is not enough. A treatise must explain why its definitions and distinctions hang together.

#### Chapter 2: happiness

According to the detailed review by Manuel Berrón and Matías Kogel, Natali reads NE I as moving roughly from:

- a nominal starting point and reputable opinions about happiness;
- empirical/functional observations;
- the *ergon* argument;
- an essential definition of human happiness.

Natali treats the *ergon* argument not merely as a dialectical victory over rival opinions but as something closer to a "syllogism of fact": the investigation reaches a definition, and once that definition is secured it can serve as an explanatory premise in later inquiry.

#### Chapter 3: virtue

The account of virtue refines the initially abstract definition of happiness.

Natali analyzes:

- division of the soul;
- intellectual and moral virtue;
- acquisition of virtue;
- definition of virtue;
- relation of generic virtue to particular virtues.

The point is architectural: later ethical books depend on definitions reached earlier.

#### Chapter 4: justice shows the treatise is not mechanically uniform

Natali does not force every chapter into one mold.

He sees NE V as methodologically mixed:

- an earlier section that can be reconstructed in the analytic/definitional pattern;
- later material containing aporiai, digressions, distinctions, and looser argumentative organization.

So "the Analytics method matters" does not become "every paragraph is secretly a formal demonstration."

#### Chapter 5: practical wisdom

NE VI completes the account of virtues and explains the intellectual capacity that works with the nonrational/desiring part of the soul.

This chapter also loops back to Natali's 1989/2001 work: *phronēsis* is not an isolated faculty but the intellectual component needed to explain moral virtue and right reason.

#### Chapter 6: weakness and pleasure

Natali treats NE VII and X.1–5 as another structural problem: the transmitted organization places discussions of *akrasia* and pleasure in ways that demand explanation rather than passive acceptance.

#### Friendship and conclusion

The final parts examine friendship and then ask how the treatise's pieces fit.

Natali's conclusion is more nuanced than "dialectic is wrong." The detailed review reports that he recognizes both definitional/scientific and dialectical methods, flexibly combined according to the subject matter.

#### Criticism

The same review offers two useful criticisms:

- Natali's reconstruction can become so technical—rebuilding syllogisms and tracking methodological vocabulary—that the substantive ethical project can disappear behind the machinery;
- he gives surprisingly little attention to the early parts of NE III on voluntariness, deliberation, and choice, especially striking given his long work on action theory.

### 14. *Aristotele, Marx, la contraddizione. Cinque saggi* (publisher: 2024; Ca' Foscari record: 2025)

This book needs to be described accurately. It is **not primarily a new 2024/2025 monograph**. Natali's new preface says the five essays were written more than forty years earlier, in the intense Italian debates of the late 1970s and early 1980s about Marxism, Hegel, dialectic, scientific rationality, and the principle of non-contradiction.

The contents are:

- "Aristotele in Marx (1837–1846)";
- "K. Marx lettore della *Politica* e dell'*Etica Nicomachea* (1857–1867)";
- "L'uso del termine 'contraddizione' nel I libro del *Capitale* di Marx";
- "L'uso del termine 'opposizione' nel I libro del *Capitale* di Marx";
- "Galvano Della Volpe e il principio di non contraddizione."

#### Research setting

Natali explains that Enrico Berti's group at Padua investigated whether:

- Marx's "contradiction" was really Hegel's contradiction;
- Hegelian contradiction really violated Aristotle's principle of non-contradiction;
- a distinct "dialectical logic" was needed in opposition to ordinary scientific reasoning.

Natali was assigned the Marx side of the project.

#### What Natali actually did

The preface describes a strikingly concrete historical method.

For Marx's Aristotle:

- reconstruct Marx's working notebooks;
- collect relevant published and unpublished material;
- identify which editions of Aristotle Marx used;
- identify nineteenth-century scholarship through which Marx encountered Aristotle;
- ask how those mediating works shaped Marx's interpretation.

For "contradiction" in *Capital* I:

- choose a text published during Marx's lifetime rather than an uncontrolled posthumous fragment;
- collate the versions published under Marx's supervision;
- track actual uses of *Widerspruch* and related terms;
- avoid deciding in advance that every occurrence names the same Hegelian concept.

That is Blackball-style provenance work avant la lettre.

#### Aristotle in Marx

Natali concludes that Marx's praise of Aristotle as a "giant" and the greatest thinker of antiquity is not empty ornament.

On Natali's reconstruction, Marx admires Aristotle's capacity to analyze concrete individuals under their determinate concepts: not an abstract Idea detached from particulars, and not merely this one coin, but coinage as a determinate social/economic kind whose causes and function can be investigated.

Natali also argues that Marx's reading of the *Politics* and *Nicomachean Ethics* was mediated by nineteenth-century Hegelian scholarship, which tended to make Aristotle look more systematic and deductive than Natali thinks Aristotle actually was.

#### "Contradiction" in Marx

Natali's 2024 preface says the word is used in several different ways:

1. ordinary contradiction inside a theory or between theory and fact;
2. Hegelian/systematic contradiction used in exposition;
3. "immanent contradictions": opposed but individually intelligible tendencies inside a social process.

On the third type, Natali argues that two opposed necessary tendencies can conflict without one object simply possessing mutually contradictory predicates in the forbidden Aristotelian sense.

His conclusion is therefore anti-dramatic: detailed textual study does not establish a special Marxian "dialectical logic" that abolishes the principle of non-contradiction.

#### Why the reissue matters

The reissue shows that Natali's source-critical habits were present very early:

- identify the exact edition an author read;
- reconstruct notebooks and working materials;
- separate lexical occurrences;
- distinguish an author's text from the later system attributed to it;
- refuse to let political stakes settle a philological question.

That is the same general temperament visible in his later Aristotle biography.

## Editions, translations, and commentaries as research

Natali's bibliography should not be split into "real books" and "mere translations." Several of the translation/commentary projects generated or embodied research questions that later became monographs.

### Xenophon, *Oeconomicus*

The translation forced decisions about what *oikonomia* names and how ancient household command maps—or does not map—onto modern economic categories.

### pseudo-Aristotle, *Oeconomica*

The edition broadens the comparison across Aristotelian and related household-management traditions, including Bryson and Neopythagorean material.

### Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*

The 1999 translation generated the structural question behind *Il metodo e il trattato*.

### Alexander, *De fato*

The commentary belongs to Natali's continuing work on causality, deliberation, alternative possibilities, and responsibility in the Aristotelian tradition.

### Anonymous ancient/Byzantine ethics commentary

Natali's recent publication record includes translation/commentary work on the anonymous commentary on the *Nicomachean Ethics*, as well as a 2026 article on Greek and Byzantine commentators on NE V. The line of research therefore extends from Aristotle's text into the history of its technical interpretation.

## A trajectory across the books

### 1. From "systems" to source structures

A persistent Natali move is to distrust an inherited system when the surviving texts are more complicated.

Examples:

- 1974: do not assume "Aristotelian theology" is an autonomous science merely because later philosophy has such a category;
- 1991/2013: do not assume a modern university behind the word "school";
- 2009: do not assume modern chapter divisions reveal the argument of NE VII;
- 2017: do not assume the *Nicomachean Ethics* has one uniform dialectical method because a famous methodological passage has been generalized to the whole work;
- 2024 reissue: do not assume every Marxian "contradiction" instantiates one Hegelian logical structure.

### 2. Practical philosophy is organized around doing, not merely knowing

The 1989/2001 and 2004 books belong together.

*The Wisdom of Aristotle* asks how true practical judgment, desire, deliberation, and happiness form an integrated capacity.

*L'action efficace* asks what the action itself is: how it differs from movement and production, how success matters, and whether the agent can be a genuine origin of action.

The 1996/2009 Alexander project then shows how the Aristotelian action framework was transformed when determinism became an explicit school controversy.

### 3. Scientific method becomes more central in Natali's later Aristotle

The 2014 and 2017 books make the *Posterior Analytics* far more central than a simple "Aristotle used dialectic/endoxa" account.

Natali's own intellectual development matters here. He reports having once accepted a more strongly dialectical interpretation of practical philosophy. Research on Aristotle's actual sciences—especially biological works and later work on definition/explanation—helped change his view.

That is evidence against treating scholarly authority as static. Natali revises his own methodological picture.

### 4. Institutions matter because philosophy requires material conditions

The institutional books do not reduce philosophy to doctrines.

The Lyceum requires:

- legal status;
- places;
- property or access to property;
- books;
- teaching practices;
- data;
- research tools;
- continuity between generations;
- people with enough *scholē* to live the theoretical life.

The class question therefore belongs inside the intellectual history, not outside it.

### 5. But "aristocratic intellectual life" is not a household census

Natali's *bios theoretikos* interpretation gives a strong reason to investigate resources and labor. It does not prove:

- every Peripatetic was rich;
- every associate was an Athenian citizen;
- every associate owned slaves;
- every lecture hearer belonged to the inner philosophical community;
- every person at the Lyceum had the same class position.

Those need person-level or household-level evidence.

## "Professor", "student", "school", and "economy" are hazardous translations

Natali's bibliography repeatedly encounters words that invite modernization.

- Aristotle as "professor";
- the Peripatos as "university";
- followers as "students";
- *oikonomia* as "economics";
- praxis as modern generic "action";
- ancient fate as modern physical determinism.

The correct response is not to ban modern words. It is to state what relation the word is standing for and test that relation against the source.

A future Blackball answer should therefore avoid an unqualified sentence such as "Aristotle had N students at his university." Being present at the Lyceum, hearing Aristotle speak, belonging to his philosophical circle, collaborating in research, and belonging to the later Peripatetic school are different relations.

## What Natali can and cannot establish for a Lyceum headcount

### Supported uses

Natali is strong evidence for:

- the categories and institutional distinctions a headcount must respect;
- the surviving source types and their biases;
- the importance of legal/property status and Theophrastus' will;
- the distinction between collective philosophical life and a modern enrollment model;
- books, data collection, diagrams, lectures, and other organized intellectual practices;
- the historiography of earlier attempts to reconstruct Aristotle's life and school.

### Not yet supported

The material inspected for this dossier does **not** justify:

- an exact enrollment count for Aristotle's Lyceum;
- a percentage of Athenian citizens who "attended";
- treating the known named Peripatetics as the complete audience;
- assuming every participant was an Athenian citizen;
- assuming every participant was aristocratic, wealthy, or a slave owner;
- treating public lecture audiences as identical with the philosophical community.

The next step is source extraction, not converting Natali's prestige into a number.

## Class and family background

Natali's professional biography is well documented. His own family wealth, parents' occupations, childhood class position, and the economics of his education were **not established in this pass**.

Do not infer class origin from the fact that he attended the University of Perugia, became a professor, or later held visiting positions at Cambridge and Oxford.
