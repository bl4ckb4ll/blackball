# Adam Smith (1723–1790)

Scottish moral philosopher and political economist. Smith is now routinely presented as a founder of economics, but his actual university chair was **Moral Philosophy**, and the institutional world in which he wrote was tiny by modern standards.

## Why this dossier is here

Smith is useful to Blackball for two separate reasons.

First, his own work explicitly analyzes status, reputation, social approval, deference, and the tendency to confuse wealth and rank with merit. Those themes are central to later questions about credentials and institutional prestige.

Second, the title "Professor Adam Smith" is easy to read through a modern research-university frame that did not yet exist. In 1759, when *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* appeared, Scotland had only a few dozen professorial posts altogether. British North America had a handful of tiny colleges. Johns Hopkins University was still 117 years in the future.

## Career chronology

- **1723:** born in Kirkcaldy, Fife.
- **1737:** matriculated at the University of Glasgow. He was about fourteen.
- **1740:** went to Balliol College, Oxford as a Snell Exhibitioner.
- **1746:** returned to Scotland.
- **1748–1751:** delivered public lectures in Edinburgh, including rhetoric and jurisprudence.
- **1751:** unanimously elected Professor of Logic at Glasgow.
- **1752:** transferred to the chair of Moral Philosophy.
- **1752–1764:** Professor of Moral Philosophy at Glasgow. The University describes this as the professorial period from which the ideas of both of his major books grew.
- **1759:** published *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*.
- **1764:** resigned his chair to travel on the Continent as tutor to the young Duke of Buccleuch.
- **1776:** published *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*.
- **1778:** appointed Commissioner of Customs in Edinburgh.
- **1787–1789:** served as Rector of the University of Glasgow.
- **1790:** published the substantially revised sixth edition of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* and died later that year.

Glasgow's own history notes that Smith remembered his professorial years there as the happiest and most honourable period of his life.

## Smith was a moral-philosophy professor, not an economics professor

The distinction is not pedantry. Eighteenth-century disciplinary boundaries were different. Smith's Glasgow teaching encompassed material that would now be distributed across philosophy, jurisprudence, political theory, economics, rhetoric, and history.

Calling him an "economics professor" retroactively makes the modern discipline look older and more institutionally settled than it was. His chair was Moral Philosophy; *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* was published from that setting; *The Wealth of Nations* appeared only in 1776, twelve years after he had left his university chair.

See: [summary of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*](../economics/adam-smith-theory-of-moral-sentiments.md).

## How rare was a professor in Smith's Scotland?

There is no clean 1759 Scottish census of "professors." Titles and institutional arrangements varied: professor, regent, master, principal, civilist, mediciner, and other offices do not map perfectly onto a modern faculty headcount. Some chairs taught large regular classes; some offices had administrative duties; some historical chairs could be close to sinecures.

The useful question is therefore not "what is the exact integer?" but **what scale of professoriate did Smith inhabit?** The surviving institutional evidence supports a reconstruction in the **high fifties — roughly 55–60 chair/professorial teaching offices across Scotland around 1759–1760**. That is an estimate, not a census result.

### Population scale

Alexander Webster's 1755 population account gave Scotland a total population of **1,265,380**, which the National Records of Scotland regards as the most credible pre-1801 estimate. Against a population of roughly 1.27 million, a national professoriate of only a few dozen makes clear how scarce a university chair was.

Smith's own institution was also small. John Rae's biography reports only about **300 students at Glasgow in Smith's day**, with the Moral Philosophy public class never above roughly 80–90 plus about 20 in the private class. Whether every detail of Rae's late-nineteenth-century reconstruction is accepted, this is plainly not the scale of a modern mass university.

## How many professors were there in the world?

**No defensible exact world total has been located, and an exact-looking number would be misleading.** "Professor" was a European institutional title, not a globally standardized occupational category. Counting only people formally called professors would omit learned teachers in institutions elsewhere that served partly analogous functions; expanding the category to include them would require a cross-cultural definition that the sources do not supply.

A more defensible scale check is possible for European-style universities:

- Walter Rüegg's *A History of the University in Europe* reports **143 universities in Europe in 1789**.
- The same work reports about **5,000 professors in European universities in the 1840s**, when systematic university statistics began to become available.
- A modern prosopographical project on European universities and teaching institutions from 1000–1800 has identified tens of thousands of scholars over the entire eight-century period, but those are cumulative career records, not a simultaneous 1759 headcount.

Taken together, these benchmarks suggest that the active professoriate of European-style universities in **1759 was on the order of a few thousand**, not hundreds of thousands. That is deliberately an **order-of-magnitude inference**, not a claimed census. The corresponding "world" total is not meaningfully recoverable without first deciding what non-European institutions and titles are being treated as equivalent.

## British North America in 1759: the scale was even smaller

In 1759 there was no United States. The relevant comparison is the British colonies in North America.

A history of the American academic profession, summarizing William Carrell's 1968 research, reports only **10 people with the title professor in all American higher education in 1750**, most at Harvard or William & Mary. This does **not** mean there were only ten college teachers: presidents and short-term tutors did much of the teaching. It shows how unusual the permanent professorial role itself was.

The same source notes that Harvard in 1750 had two Hollis professors and a permanent tutor supporting President Edward Holyoke. William & Mary's own history says that in 1729 its "full faculty" consisted of a president, six professors, an usher, and a writing master.

By the time *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* appeared in 1759, six colonial colleges that became major American institutions were operating:

| Institution as it existed by 1759 | Founding/college date | Scale/context |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Harvard College | 1636 | First college in the American colonies. |
| College of William & Mary | 1693 | Second-oldest; royal charter. |
| Yale College / Collegiate School | 1701 | Chartered as the Collegiate School; renamed Yale College in 1718. |
| College of New Jersey (Princeton) | 1746 | British North America's fourth college; moved to Princeton and Nassau Hall in 1756. |
| King's College (Columbia) | 1754 | Its first class consisted of **eight students and one faculty member**, Samuel Johnson. |
| College of Philadelphia (Penn) | 1755 college charter | Its predecessor Academy opened earlier; first College class graduated in 1757. |

So if the remembered fact was that **another famous American institution had only just been founded when Smith was writing**, that is basically right — but the candidates are Princeton (1746), King's/Columbia (1754), and the College of Philadelphia/Penn (1755), not Johns Hopkins.

## Johns Hopkins: wrong century, but useful comparison

Johns Hopkins University was founded and formally opened in **1876**, **117 years after** the first edition of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*. Johns Hopkins describes itself as the United States' **first modern research university**, organized around the integration of research and teaching and with strong graduate education modeled partly on German universities.

That is the useful contrast. Smith's Glasgow chair belonged to an eighteenth-century teaching university and to the Scottish moral-philosophy tradition. Hopkins belongs to the later research-university model that reshaped American higher education in the late nineteenth century.

### "First American university" is definition-sensitive

Institutional firsts are messy:

- Harvard calls itself the first college in the American colonies (1636).
- Penn's archives say the College of Philadelphia acquired a medical school in 1765 and thereby became "America's first university" by having more than one faculty/school; the institution did not use "University" in its name until 1779.
- William & Mary says its 1779 law school made it the first college in the country to become a university.
- Johns Hopkins claims a different first: the first **modern research university**, in 1876.

These claims use different definitions. The dossier should preserve the definitions rather than force them into one winner.

## Smith's status in this institutional world

Smith was elected to the Glasgow Logic chair at about twenty-seven and transferred to Moral Philosophy the following year. In a country with only roughly five dozen chair-level university posts, this placed him inside a very small institutional elite.

That fact should not be converted into a claim that every professor was wealthy, politically powerful, or intellectually eminent. Chairs varied in income and function, appointments involved patronage as well as scholarly reputation, and the Scottish universities were internally heterogeneous. But a professorship was plainly a scarce post, and Smith's career unfolded inside a network in which the individual professoriate was small enough for the names and appointments of many members to be traced directly.

This context also sharpens a theme in Smith's own moral theory: **rank and public distinction attract attention and deference, but attention and deference are not the same thing as merit.**

## Confidence and open questions

**High confidence:** Smith's Glasgow appointments and dates; TMS publication date; Edinburgh's 1760 professor count; Glasgow's pre-1760 count; colonial-college chronology; Johns Hopkins 1876 chronology; the 1755 Scottish population estimate.

**Medium confidence / reconstructed:** the Scotland-wide total of roughly 55–60 chair/professorial offices, because institutional titles and counting conventions differ.

**Low precision by design:** the world/European professor count. "A few thousand" is only an order-of-magnitude inference. Do not replace it with a precise integer unless a genuine synchronized census or defensible reconstruction is found.
