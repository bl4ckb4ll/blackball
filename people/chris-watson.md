# Chris Watson

English sound recordist, composer and former member of Cabaret Voltaire whose career grew out of a practice he had already been doing for its own sake since childhood: carrying microphones into places, listening closely, recording what other people normally do not hear, and manipulating or arranging those recordings.

> Research status: evidence-led dossier, first pass 2026-10-01. The career sequence is well documented in interviews and professional biographies. The economics are much less visible: public sources show many distinct kinds of paid work, but not what proportion of Watson's income came from each one at different stages.

## Why he belongs in the Blackball people file

Watson is useful beside [Brennan Lee Mulligan](brennan-lee-mulligan.md) for a specific question:

> What does it look like when something a person does because they are absorbed by it gradually becomes the center of a working life, without the person first choosing a conventional corporate profession with a stable ladder?

His case is especially useful because it is **not** a clean story of rejecting institutions.

Watson did not simply stay outside employment until the world discovered him. He spent important years inside Tyne Tees Television, where he says he received excellent technical training across location sound, film, documentary, music programming and post-production. He later took a dedicated sound-recordist post at the Royal Society for the Protection of Birds. Those jobs supplied training, equipment, colleagues, reputation and access.

What remained unusually continuous was the underlying activity. The boy recording birds on a garden table, the Cabaret Voltaire member using tape and found sound, the television sound engineer, the wildlife specialist, the BBC documentary recordist, the Touch recording artist and the gallery installation composer are not separate occupational identities pasted together after the fact. Watson repeatedly describes them as applications of the same basic practice.

That makes the case more interesting than generic advice to “follow your passion.” The question is how a private practice acquired **skills, equipment, institutional footholds, collaborators, clients, archives, audiences and several adjacent markets** until it could support a durable career.

## The practice came first

Watson has repeatedly traced the beginning to a portable reel-to-reel tape recorder his parents gave him at about age eleven or twelve.

In a *Sound on Sound* interview he recalled first recording sounds inside the house and then realizing the battery-powered recorder could be taken outside. Seeing birds at the garden bird table through the kitchen window but not hearing them led him to put the microphone outside near the table and record them.

The important detail is not merely that he showed early talent. The object his parents gave him created a cheap private laboratory. Recording became something he could repeat, inspect, alter and improve without needing a school assignment, employer or audience.

He later encountered musique concrète, especially Pierre Schaeffer, and recognized a larger artistic tradition in the activity he had already discovered.

## Cabaret Voltaire: the private practice becomes collective production

In the early 1970s Watson co-founded Cabaret Voltaire with Stephen Mallinder and Richard H. Kirk in Sheffield. Tape recording, tape loops, electronics, found sound and manipulation were central to the group's work.

This stage matters because it shows one route by which an idiosyncratic private activity becomes socially legible. Watson did not begin by applying for a job called “field recordist.” The same fascination with recorded sound first became part of a collaborative experimental-music scene.

Watson later said that he gradually became more interested in what he was hearing **outside** than in studio work. He was also increasingly interested in the relationship between sound and film, and dissatisfied with the sound in wildlife films. In a Bandcamp interview he summarized the thought bluntly: he believed he could do better.

So the transition out of the band was not a rejection of the original interest. It was a narrowing and deepening of it.

## Tyne Tees Television: institutional apprenticeship, not a detour

Watson joined the sound department at Tyne Tees Television in 1981.

In a later interview he described Tyne Tees as a large and unusually fertile creative environment. He worked across location recording, films, documentaries, music programmes and post-production. He explicitly says he needed professional skills and knowledge if he was going to do something with his interest in outdoor wildlife recording, and saw television sound as the place to acquire them.

This is one of the most useful parts of the career for Blackball.

A conventional summary can make the television job look like the “real career” that replaced youthful experimentation. Watson's own description suggests almost the reverse: the institution functioned as an **apprenticeship and tool-building period for a pre-existing interest**.

The job supplied things that private enthusiasm alone could not:

- systematic technical training;
- repeated location work;
- film and documentary production experience;
- post-production knowledge;
- professional equipment;
- working relationships across broadcasting;
- a reputation for being able to deliver usable sound under production constraints.

This is a material correction to any bootstrap story. The childhood recorder mattered, but so did a company employing hundreds of people and maintaining a professional production environment.

## The RSPB move: specialization with reduced downside

By the mid-1980s Watson says he was getting into a rut at Tyne Tees and saw an advertisement for what he describes as the Royal Society for the Protection of Birds' first dedicated sound-recordist post.

A particularly important material detail appears in his Lindisfarne interview: **Tyne Tees gave him a two-year sabbatical** to take the RSPB job.

That makes the move look quite different from the standard romantic narrative of abandoning security to follow a calling. He was able to deepen a risky specialization while retaining a route back to his previous employer.

At the RSPB he worked specifically on wildlife sound and helped establish or expand a sound library. The role brought his technical broadcast background into direct contact with ornithology and natural-history field craft.

## Freelance work and the BBC: relationships compound

After the RSPB period Watson moved into freelance sound recording.

The route into the David Attenborough natural-history work is unusually concrete. Watson has described former colleagues moving from the RSPB orbit to the BBC Natural History Unit. Peter Bassett later called him about a new David Attenborough series, *The Life of Birds*, because it would require substantial wildlife wild-track recording and Bassett knew that was Watson's specialty.

Watson accepted. *The Life of Birds* won a BAFTA for factual sound, and Watson's relationship with BBC natural-history production continued across many later projects.

This is not simply “networking” in the empty sense. The relationship had information in it:

- colleagues knew what Watson was unusually good at;
- a production eventually existed that needed exactly that specialization;
- someone who had seen his work could identify the fit;
- the high-profile project then produced further reputation and subsequent work.

The career therefore accumulated through **specific remembered competence**, not merely through generalized social access.

## One skill, several markets

Watson's career is not organized around one employer or one product.

In interviews he explicitly rejects a sharp boundary between his forms of work. He identifies himself as a **sound recordist** and says sound lets him move among media.

Documented forms include:

- wildlife and natural-history television;
- documentary and feature-film location sound;
- BBC radio productions;
- solo recordings released by Touch;
- collaborations with other musicians and sound artists;
- gallery, museum and festival installations;
- live multichannel performances;
- sound design and material for computer games;
- commissioned site-specific pieces;
- lectures, demonstrations and field-recording workshops.

His own site still documents that mixed model in 2026. In January 2026 Touch released *Planet Ocean*, a one-hour collection assembled from ocean recordings around the world. In May 2026 Watson led a dawn-chorus recording workshop at Holystone North Wood, combining an overnight field session with a practical indoor workshop.

The point is not that every one of these activities was equally lucrative. Public sources do not give that information. The point is that no single employer had to contain the whole practice.

## Touch: an independent outlet beside commissioned work

Watson's solo field-recording work found a durable home at the independent label Touch.

*Stepping into the Dark* appeared on Touch in 1996. The label subsequently released *Outside the Circle of Fire*, *Weather Report*, *El Tren Fantasma* and later projects including *Planet Ocean*.

This matters because the broadcast client and the artistic outlet are not the same institution.

The television work pays for highly specialized sound recording in service of a larger production. The Touch work lets Watson edit and arrange recordings into pieces whose primary subject is the recorded sound itself.

That gives the same field practice at least two distinct economic and artistic lives:

```text
location recording
    ├── commissioned production material
    └── source material for Watson's own composed / edited work
```

The distinction should not be exaggerated into complete independence. Touch is still a label, commissions still come from institutions, and distribution still requires organizations. But Watson is not dependent on one corporation defining what counts as useful output from his skill.

## The archive itself becomes an asset

*El Tren Fantasma* is an unusually revealing example.

Watson recorded a Mexican railway journey while working on a BBC television production. He later recalled that he had offered to help with post-production if he could retain access to the original recordings. The tapes then sat for years before he returned to them and built a standalone sound work around the railway journey.

The underlying work therefore outlived the job that originally financed the recording.

This suggests another mechanism by which a self-directed practice can become durable: accumulated work product can become a **personal archive of future possibilities**, provided rights and access allow reuse.

That qualification matters. Blackball should not generalize from Watson into the assumption that workers normally own or may freely repurpose material created for employers or clients. In this case Watson describes having specifically arranged access to the recordings.

## Not “outside corporations”; portable identity across institutions

Watson's case is easy to misstate in either direction.

It would be wrong to describe him as a conventional corporate employee who simply climbed a broadcasting ladder. He repeatedly moved among music, television, wildlife recording, radio, records, galleries, performances and teaching, and his own account centers the craft rather than a job title.

It would also be wrong to describe him as someone who proved institutions were unnecessary.

His career depended at different points on:

- parents who put a portable recorder in his hands;
- collaborators in an experimental music scene;
- Tyne Tees training and production infrastructure;
- a protected two-year RSPB specialization period;
- colleagues who later connected him to BBC work;
- production budgets that sent him to difficult locations;
- access to professional and sometimes expensive recording equipment;
- an independent label willing to publish unusual field-recording albums;
- galleries, broadcasters, festivals and universities commissioning work;
- audiences willing to buy records, attend installations or pay for workshops.

The more interesting proposition is narrower:

> Watson built a professional identity around a capability that remained portable between institutions.

The institutions changed. The thing he knew how to do kept becoming more valuable.

## Comparison with Brennan Lee Mulligan

[Brennan Lee Mulligan](brennan-lee-mulligan.md) is not doing the same work, but the comparison is useful.

### Similarity: the practice predates the career

Mulligan was immersed in LARP, storytelling, performance and game-making long before *Dimension 20*. Watson recorded and manipulated sound long before professional wildlife recording.

Neither case begins with a labor-market calculation that identifies a vacant prestigious job and then reverse-engineers the credentials.

### Similarity: communities convert private skill into public skill

For Mulligan, Wayfinder, teenage game-making, improv and later UCB provided repeated social practice.

For Watson, Cabaret Voltaire, broadcast production crews, RSPB wildlife work and later field-recording communities did something similar.

A private obsession can become technically sophisticated alone, but a career requires other people to experience the work, trust it, criticize it, remember it and invite the person back.

### Similarity: adjacent markets matter

Mulligan's abilities cross writing, improv, game mastering, actual play, podcasts, live performance and television-style subscription media.

Watson's abilities cross television, radio, records, installations, films, games, workshops and live sound.

In both cases the durable unit is not one job description. It is a cluster of capabilities that different organizations and audiences can purchase in different forms.

### Important difference: specialist service versus personality-centered media

Watson's professional value was for a long time largely **behind the microphone**: a production could hire him because he could obtain difficult sounds that other people could not.

Mulligan's current work is much more audience-facing. His personality, improvisational presence and relationship with recurring viewers are themselves part of the product.

That difference changes the economics. Watson's model can generate work through specialist reputation among producers and institutions even when the general public does not know his name. Mulligan's current model is much more closely tied to recognizable authorship and audience attachment.

## A more useful career sequence than “follow your passion”

The evidence supports something like this:

```text
private repeated practice
    ↓
small collaborative scene
    ↓
formal technical apprenticeship
    ↓
narrower specialization
    ↓
professional reputation among peers
    ↓
freelance / commissioned work
    ↓
independent artistic output beside client work
    ↓
archive + reputation + teaching + commissions + new media
```

This is not a prescription. It is a description of one career.

Several steps depended on circumstances that may not generalize: an unusually apt childhood gift, a flourishing regional television company, the availability of a two-year sabbatical, a dedicated RSPB post, colleagues moving into the BBC Natural History Unit, and a label willing to release field recordings as standalone art.

The case is useful precisely because those supports can be named instead of hiding them behind “he loved sound enough.”

## Blackball relevance

Watson belongs in the same broad Blackball inquiry as Mulligan, Gauss and Mordell, although the mechanisms are very different.

The recurring question is not whether someone “deserved” to spend a life on the thing they cared about.

It is:

> **What material and social mechanism lets an activity somebody would do anyway become an activity they can keep doing while paying for a life?**

For Watson the answer appears to be a sequence rather than a single institution:

- family-provided equipment;
- peer collaboration;
- institutional training;
- protected specialization;
- freelance reputation;
- broadcast clients;
- reusable recordings and accumulated expertise;
- an independent label;
- commissions;
- teaching and workshops.

That is more concrete than saying he “made a career from his passion,” and less misleading than saying he escaped employment.

## Current status, 2026

Watson remains professionally active.

His official site lists continuing installation, recording and teaching work. Touch released *Planet Ocean* in January 2026, assembled from ocean recordings collected internationally. He also continued leading field-recording workshops in 2026.

This longevity matters. The case is not a short interval in which an unusual hobby briefly produced income. The same core practice has survived changes in music scenes, broadcast technology, recording hardware, institutions and distribution formats for more than five decades.

## Research leads

The current dossier is much stronger on **how the work developed** than on its economics. Useful next questions:

1. Reconstruct Watson's employment dates, salary conditions and training at Tyne Tees more precisely.
2. Determine exactly how the two-year RSPB sabbatical was structured and whether Tyne Tees guaranteed a return position.
3. Investigate Hoi Polloi Film & Video Ltd., which older professional biographies identify with Watson from the mid-1990s, and distinguish company ownership from ordinary freelance contracting.
4. Find contract-level evidence about ownership and reuse of location recordings. *El Tren Fantasma* is suggestive but should not stand in for every project.
5. Estimate the relative economic importance, by period, of broadcast work, Touch releases, installations, commissions, workshops and other work. Do not infer this from artistic prominence.
6. Reconstruct major equipment costs over time. Field recording is unusually dependent on microphones, recorders, travel and access, so the capital requirements matter.
7. Investigate the role of Mike Harding / Touch as an intermediary and long-term collaborator rather than treating the label as an impersonal distribution service.
8. Compare Watson directly with other people who turned a private technical practice into a multi-client working life without becoming primarily an employee-manager: Brennan Lee Mulligan is one comparison, but sound, photography, specialist scientific instrumentation and open-source software may produce closer structural analogues.
