# Raymond L. Johnson

American mathematician in partial differential equations and harmonic analysis, longtime University of Maryland professor, and a central first-person source for the history of Black mathematicians at the University of Texas and Rice University. His autobiographical account is preserved both on his University of Maryland page and by **Mathematicians of the African Diaspora (MAD)**.

## Education and mathematical career

- Grew up in Alice, Texas.
- B.S. in mathematics, University of Texas at Austin, 1963.
- Entered Rice in 1963 in the middle of the legal fight over desegregating the university. He initially worked as a research associate while litigation over Rice's charter delayed formal enrollment.
- Earned a Ph.D. in mathematics from Rice under Jim Douglas Jr. His dissertation was *A Priori Estimates and Unique Continuation Theorems for Second Order Parabolic Equations*.
- Rice's graduate-alumni record gives the doctorate as **1969**; historical Rice News coverage also describes him as receiving the doctorate in 1969. Some later Rice faculty pages list **1970**, and the MAD profile itself contains both years. The repository should preserve the discrepancy rather than silently normalize it.
- Joined the University of Maryland, College Park mathematics department after graduate school and eventually became a full professor and department chair.
- Spent 1976–1978 at Howard University while its mathematics Ph.D. program was being developed, then returned to Maryland.
- Research moved from ill-posed/parabolic partial differential equations toward Besov spaces and harmonic analysis.
- Returned to Rice in 2009 as a visiting mathematics professor and is listed by Rice as an adjunct professor.

## Rice desegregation

Johnson's career is inseparable from Rice's desegregation. Rice's founding charter restricted the institution to white students. By the time Johnson applied for doctoral study, Rice's trustees were trying to remove that restriction, but alumni litigation delayed the change.

Johnson recounts that he arrived in 1963 and spent a year as a research associate before formally enrolling in 1964 after Rice prevailed in court. Rice now describes him as its first Black student and its first Black graduate. Two Black undergraduates enrolled the following year.

Johnson also records that he nearly left Rice after discovering that his support was lower than that of other students in his cohort; he obtained an NSF graduate fellowship and stayed.

## R. L. Moore and R. H. Bing

Johnson's autobiographical page is the source of the recollection that prompted these dossiers.

As a University of Texas undergraduate, Johnson considered taking a course from the influential topologist R. L. Moore. He says an older Black student, Walker Hunt, told him that Moore would let Hunt enroll only on the condition that Hunt begin with a C and could go no higher.

Johnson then records the image of Moore that stayed with him: Moore attended a topology lecture given by a student of R. H. Bing and walked out after discovering that the speaker was Black.

Johnson immediately contrasts this with Bing. He says he had “a very different image” of Bing and reports hearing that Bing would not return from Wisconsin to Texas while Moore remained there.

### Chronology warning on the Bing story

The reported sequence in Johnson's page says Moore died and Bing returned to Texas a year or so later. Independently documented dates do not support that sequence: Bing accepted a permanent UT position in **1973**, and Moore died in **1974**.

That mismatch does not erase Johnson's recollection or prove that the underlying report about Bing's attitude was false. It means the dossier should distinguish:

- Johnson's favorable personal image of Bing;
- a community report Johnson remembered hearing about Bing's reluctance to return;
- the independently documented chronology, which contradicts the final part of the anecdote as written.

See [R. H. Bing](r-h-bing.md).

## Maryland and mentoring

Johnson writes that he was the first African American appointed to the University of Maryland mathematics department and later the first African American there to rise from assistant professor to full professor. He served as associate chair for graduate studies and later chaired the department.

He also describes building a recruitment and support process that contributed to Maryland graduating a substantial number of African American mathematics Ph.D.s. Rice's later account of his career says he developed a national reputation for mentoring graduate students and received the **Presidential Award for Excellence in Science, Mathematics and Engineering Mentoring**.

## Research

Johnson's dissertation and early work concerned unstable or ill-posed parabolic problems, a priori estimates, and unique continuation. His 1971 *Transactions of the American Mathematical Society* paper, *A Priori Estimates and Unique Continuation Theorems for Second Order Parabolic Equations*, states that it contains essentially the results of his Rice thesis.

He later describes his interests as moving toward representations of solutions, Besov spaces, and harmonic analysis.

## Why this dossier is here

Johnson is not only a subject of the historical record; he is a source. His own account connects school segregation in South Texas, integration at the University of Texas, Rice's desegregation lawsuit, the professional culture around R. L. Moore and R. H. Bing, and later efforts to recruit and mentor Black mathematics Ph.D. students. The MAD archive is valuable partly because it preserves this kind of first-person professional history rather than only lists of degrees and awards.
