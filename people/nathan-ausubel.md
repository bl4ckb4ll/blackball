# Nathan Ausubel — Jewish folklore, material support, hunger, and learning

## Why Blackball is recording him

Nathan Ausubel's *A Treasury of Jewish Folklore* contains several distinct items about rabbis, teachers, yeshiva students, hunger, fasting, and communal support. They should not be collapsed into one generic story.

The Blackball use is narrower: trace exactly which texts support a comparison between intellectual or religious work and precarious material support, and distinguish Ausubel's own editorial framing from stories he credits to earlier writers.

## Source discipline: credited and uncredited items

Ausubel's contents page states that items not otherwise credited are the editor's work, consisting of stories from oral tradition and adaptations from foreign-language sources.

That matters here. "The Rabbi's Nourishment" and "The Modest Saint" do not carry numbered source references in the text, and neither appears among the item-level credits for Part One in the notes. The safe attribution is therefore **Ausubel's anthology, uncredited item**, under his stated editorial/oral-tradition/adaptation policy. Do not invent a more specific original source without finding one.

By contrast, "The Cabalists" carries source note 38, and Ausubel explicitly identifies its earlier source.

## "The Rabbi's Nourishment" — p. 11

This is the closest Ausubel item to the remembered starving-rabbi joke.

A village Jew is asked how the rabbi can survive on the very small salary the community pays him. The answer says he would otherwise have died of hunger, but his piety leads him to fast on Mondays and Thursdays, and the joke calls those fasts what "sustains" him.

What it supports:

- the rabbi is materially underpaid;
- hunger is explicit;
- religious fasting and inadequate pay are put into comic tension;
- a pious practice becomes the joke's absurd explanation for survival on an inadequate salary.

What it does **not** by itself support:

- that the community praises the rabbi *because* he is hungry;
- that hunger is literally mistaken for fasting;
- that the community deliberately withholds support in order to make him holy;
- a general historical claim about all rabbis or Jewish communities.

An independent *Encyclopaedia Judaica* article on Jewish humor preserves a very close popular saying: a rabbi is so poor that without his Monday-and-Thursday fasts he would starve. That is useful evidence that the joke circulated as a broader folk motif, but it does not establish the exact transmission history of Ausubel's version.

- Encyclopedia.com mirror of the *Encyclopaedia Judaica* article: https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/hummash

## "The Modest Saint" — p. 14

A disciple boasts that his rabbi fasts every day except Sabbaths and holidays. A cynic says he has personally seen the rabbi eating on weekdays. The disciple answers by treating the observed eating itself as evidence of the rabbi's saintly modesty: he supposedly eats only to conceal his fasting.

This supports a different comic mechanism: piety language can absorb contrary evidence about eating and fasting.

It does **not** mention salary, poverty, inadequate communal support, or hunger. It should not be fused with "The Rabbi's Nourishment" to manufacture a story that Ausubel does not actually tell.

## I. L. Peretz, "The Cabalists" — pp. 112–116 in the Bantam abridgment

This is the strongest structural source for the Blackball motif.

The Lashchev yeshiva has declined until only its principal, Reb Yekel, and one student remain. Both are ragged and extremely poor. Ausubel's text says the **impoverished townspeople** have been sending less food to the yeshiva and offering fewer eating days. Other students leave. Reb Yekel and his pupil remain and repeatedly go hungry.

The narration then explicitly turns material deprivation into ascetic practice: because they are already forced to go hungry and lie awake, they use the involuntary "long fasts" and bodily mortification as an opportunity for Kabbalistic study.

The ending intensifies the connection but also makes the causal sequence more complicated. When food finally arrives, the pupil refuses his portion and chooses an additional penitential fast because he had coveted his master's food. After a nocturnal mystical experience he dies. The town admires the manner of his death, while Reb Yekel says that with a few more fasts he might have died by the "Divine Kiss." The story has therefore moved from involuntary hunger caused by poverty into voluntary religious fasting before the death that is admired; those stages should not be collapsed.

This supports:

- a communal support system for a school and its students;
- that system shrinking when the town itself becomes poor;
- real hunger caused by inadequate food support;
- the conversion of involuntary deprivation into religious ascetic practice;
- a later religious valuation of a death following additional voluntary fasting.

It does **not** support blaming affluent townspeople who refuse to pay. The text specifically says the townspeople are impoverished, and the pupil's final fast is voluntary.

## Ausubel's own note on yeshiva students — pp. 487–488

Immediately after source note 38, Ausubel adds a signed editorial note about the yeshiva-bocher.

This is useful because it states the distinction the stories alone can blur. Ausubel says that supporting poor scholarly youths was regarded as a religiously meritorious act, while also saying that widespread poverty made that support inadequate. He describes *essen teg* ("eating days"), in which students circulated among households for meals; sometimes they received only leftovers and remained hungry.

He also distinguishes possible social outcomes: piety and learning might lead to a respected rabbinate, while becoming a melamed could mean a materially poor calling even though it involved teaching children the word of God.

This supports a careful formulation: **learning could be socially and religiously valued while the material arrangements supporting learners and teachers remained precarious or inadequate**. It does not justify saying that communities universally honored scholars *by* starving them.

## Adjacent items that should stay distinct

### "Strictly Kosher"

A teacher of Scripture in a small Polish town is described as sick of drudgery, cold, and hunger before becoming a robber. This is evidence for poor material conditions of a religious teacher, not for the fasting/piety joke.

### "An Author's Life After Forty"

A young Talmudic scholar who wants to write a learned book is told that the life of a learned author involves peddling his own work and enduring hunger until he becomes accustomed to it. This is an intellectual-labor/hunger joke, but not evidence about communal salary or rabbinic support.

### "For Honor"

An unpaid synagogue warden explains that he does the job for honor. This gives Ausubel an explicit honor/unpaid-labor joke, but it is **not** about a starving rabbi or teacher. Do not splice it into "The Rabbi's Nourishment" and present the composite as one source.

## Connection to *Danger Man*, "The Leak"

The *Danger Man* scene and the Ausubel/Peretz material meet at a narrow, defensible point: **teaching and learning can be highly valued even when their material support is poor or precarious**.

The comparison then divides:

- In "The Rabbi's Nourishment," tiny salary, hunger, and pious fasting form the joke.
- In Peretz's "The Cabalists," an impoverished town's shrinking food support produces actual hunger; the story then converts that deprivation into ascetic practice and finally into an additional voluntary fast associated with a religiously admired death.
- In Ausubel's historical/editorial note, supporting scholars is itself meritorious, yet widespread poverty makes the support inadequate.
- In *Danger Man*, the boy does not deny or spiritualize the teacher's hunger. He tries to feed him materially because the teacher, as the boy says, "feeds my mind."

So **"honored because underfed" is a Blackball interpretive label, not a phrase that should be attributed to Ausubel**. The stronger source-supported motif is: *valued learning, precarious subsistence, and the ways religious language can coexist with or reinterpret material hunger*.
