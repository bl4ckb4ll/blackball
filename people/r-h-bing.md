# R. H. Bing (1914–1986)

American topologist whose career connects directly to Robert Lee Moore, the University of Texas, and Raymond L. Johnson's recollection preserved by **Mathematicians of the African Diaspora (MAD)**.

See also:

- [Mathematicians of the African Diaspora](mathematicians-of-the-african-diaspora.md)
- [Robert Lee Moore](robert-lee-moore.md)
- [Raymond L. Johnson](raymond-l-johnson.md)

## Career

- Born October 20, 1914, in Oakwood, Texas; died April 28, 1986, in Austin.
- His legal name really was **R. H. Bing**; the initials were not abbreviations for given names.
- Graduated from Southwest Texas State Teachers College in 1935.
- Worked as a high-school teacher and coach in Palestine, Texas, while taking summer courses at the University of Texas.
- Earned an M.Ed. from UT in 1938 and a Ph.D. in mathematics there in 1945 under Robert Lee Moore. His dissertation was *Concerning Simple Plane Webs*.
- Joined the University of Wisconsin in 1947 and remained there until 1973, apart from visiting appointments and leaves.
- Returned permanently to the University of Texas at Austin in 1973.
- Chaired the UT mathematics department from 1975 to 1977.
- President of the Mathematical Association of America, 1963–1964; president of the American Mathematical Society, 1977–1978.
- Elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1965.

## Mathematics

Bing worked principally in geometric topology and continuum theory, especially the topology of three-dimensional spaces. His name is attached to a large group of ideas and results, including Bing's metrization theorem, Bing shrinking, the Bing double, and the Bing–Borsuk conjecture. His work on decomposition spaces and 3-manifolds became characteristic enough that later mathematicians sometimes referred informally to “Bing-type topology.”

Selected publication/reference points:

- *The Geometric Topology of 3-Manifolds*, AMS Colloquium Publications.
- *Collected Papers*, two volumes, American Mathematical Society.
- AMS memorial notice after his death: https://community.ams.org/journals/notices/198608/198608FullIssue.pdf

## Moore's student, but not reducible to Moore

Bing was one of R. L. Moore's most distinguished students. He spoke warmly about Moore's mathematics and teaching at the 1973 dedication of Robert Lee Moore Hall, describing Moore as both a creative researcher and a master teacher.

Source:

- “Remarks by Professor R. H. Bing about R. L. Moore,” October 5, 1973: https://legacyrlmoore.org/reference/bing.html

That record matters because the relationship should not be simplified into a personal feud. Bing could value Moore's mathematical teaching while differing from him in conduct, judgment, or treatment of other people.

## Raymond L. Johnson's recollection

Raymond L. Johnson's autobiographical account, reproduced by MAD, explicitly contrasts Bing with Moore. Johnson reports that Texas wanted Bing back from Wisconsin and that “word was” that Bing would not return while Moore remained at Texas. Johnson then says he had “a very different image” of Bing from the image he had of Moore.

### Chronology correction

The final part of Johnson's remembered story cannot be literally correct as written. Bing accepted a permanent position at UT in **1973**. Moore died on **October 4, 1974**. Bing therefore did not wait until after Moore's death to return permanently.

This chronology does not prove that the underlying report about Bing's attitude toward Moore was false. Johnson introduces it as something circulating by word of mouth, not as a statement Bing personally made to him. The safest record is therefore:

- Johnson remembered a strong favorable contrast between Bing and Moore;
- he reported hearing that Bing did not want to return while Moore was present;
- independently documented dates contradict the specific “Moore died, then Bing returned” sequence.

## Independent evidence about Bing's reputation

The National Academy biographical memoir describes Bing as a man of strong character and independent judgment who respected people on their own merits and treated people kindly. That is consistent with Johnson's favorable impression, but it is not independent proof of the specific story about why Bing stayed at Wisconsin.

Source:

- Michael Starbird, NAS memoir: https://celebratio.org/Bing_RH/article/52/

## Why this dossier is here

Bing is mathematically important in his own right, but he is especially useful in this repository as a point of comparison inside the same mathematical lineage. Johnson's account does not describe “Texas mathematics” as uniformly racist; it distinguishes sharply between Moore and Bing. Preserving that distinction is one reason to keep the MAD material at the level of named people and attributed recollections rather than flatten it into institutional summary.
