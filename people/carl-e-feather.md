# Carl E. Feather

## Why Blackball is recording him

Carl E. Feather is the author J. D. Vance cites in *Hillbilly Elegy* for a community-level history of Appalachian migration into northeast Ohio. The name matters: the cited author is **Carl E. Feather**, not “Glen Feather.”

The cited book is:

*Mountain People in a Flat Land: A Popular History of Appalachian Migration to Northeast Ohio, 1940–1965* (Ohio University Press, 1998).

This is a particularly good source for Blackball because it is not a distant national survey. Feather built the book around migrants to **Ashtabula County, Ohio**, using family stories and oral history to reconstruct why people left Appalachia, how they found work, what they encountered in Ohio, and what they retained or lost after migration.

It is also a useful source-audit case. *Hillbilly Elegy* cites **page 4** of Feather's 255-page book. That citation proves Vance used at least a specific passage from the book. It does **not** prove that he read the entire book, and it does not prove that he did not. Blackball should retain that distinction rather than guessing about Vance's private reading process.

## Career and standpoint

Feather is a journalist, photographer, local historian, and oral historian rather than a conventional university historian. A 1999 H-Appalachia review explains that *Mountain People in a Flat Land* grew out of a newspaper series and that Feather's interest in migration was personal: he had himself been a migrant as a child.

Later biographical material describes him as a retired journalist and professional photographer whose work has appeared in *Goldenseal* magazine since 1987. His more recent books return explicitly to his own family's migration between West Virginia and Ohio.

That standpoint matters. Feather's book is not simply “research about hillbillies.” It comes out of journalism, interviews, family migration experience, and sustained attention to a specific Ohio community.

## *Mountain People in a Flat Land*

Publication details:

- Carl E. Feather, *Mountain People in a Flat Land: A Popular History of Appalachian Migration to Northeast Ohio, 1940–1965*.
- Athens: Ohio University Press, 1998.
- xxvi + 255 pages.

The publisher's description frames the migration around a striking labor-market contrast. In the early 1940s, transportation from Appalachia to northeast Ohio could cost more than a week's wages in a lumber camp, while a physically able worker arriving in industrial northeast Ohio might find work extremely quickly. The move therefore involved both a large expected economic gain and a real cost in money, kinship, place, and culture.

Feather narrows the story to Ashtabula County rather than treating “urban Appalachians” as a homogeneous national population. The book follows migrants' accounts of coal towns, lumber camps, family life, subsistence practices, migration routes, jobs, prejudice, cultural adjustment, and continuing ties to the mountains.

## What Vance actually cites

In *Hillbilly Elegy*, note 9 gives:

> Carl E. Feather, *Mountain People in a Flat Land: A Popular History of Appalachian Migration to Northeast Ohio, 1940–1965* (Athens: Ohio University Press, 1998), 4.

The surrounding chapter uses historical sources to place Vance's grandparents within the large twentieth-century migration from Appalachia to industrial Midwestern communities.

### Reading-completeness audit

The evidentiary situation differs sharply from Jack Temple Kirby's.

Kirby's cited source is a sixteen-page article and is cited repeatedly. Feather's source is a 255-page book, while Vance's note identifies one early page.

Therefore record the following separately:

- **Source present in notes:** yes.
- **Specific page cited:** p. 4.
- **Evidence Vance consulted at least that material:** yes.
- **Evidence in the citation itself that he worked through the entire 255-page book:** no.
- **Evidence that he definitely did not read it:** no.

This is a useful general Blackball rule. A bibliography entry or page citation should never silently become “the author read and absorbed this whole source.” The claim should be no stronger than the documentary trail.

## What the book contains beyond Vance's page citation

The H-Appalachia review is especially useful because it describes the architecture and emphasis of the whole book. Feather's project is an oral popular history organized largely around particular migrant families. Its subjects include:

- poverty and limited work that pushed families out of Appalachian communities;
- industrial opportunity pulling migrants north;
- family and acquaintance networks that helped subsequent migrants follow earlier ones;
- housing and material hardship after arrival;
- prejudice against supposedly stupid, lazy, or backward “hillbillies”;
- attempts to preserve foodways, reunions, gardening, hunting, and other connections to rural life;
- economic improvement that did not necessarily erase attachment to the homeplace;
- the transformation of a migrant identity across generations.

The review also notes that Feather deliberately aimed to counter degrading stereotypes of Appalachian migrants. Although the book foregrounds oral history and accessible storytelling, its chapters direct readers toward academic and historical sources as well.

- Catherine Kaikowska, review on H-Appalachia, March 1999: https://rmtrain.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=2929

## Why Feather is unusually important for reading *Hillbilly Elegy*

Feather provides a close comparison case for Vance because both projects concern Appalachian families moving into Ohio. But the evidentiary forms are very different.

Vance's central evidence is his own family memory, interpreted through memoir and selected social-science/history citations. Feather intentionally assembles many migrant voices from one destination community. That means Feather can help test whether a feature in *Hillbilly Elegy* is:

- peculiar to the Vance family;
- common in a specific Kentucky-to-Ohio migration stream;
- found more broadly among Appalachian migrants;
- a later stereotype imposed on heterogeneous migrants;
- or a phenomenon whose prevalence cannot be established from either source alone.

The important Blackball move is not to choose one storyteller as “the authentic Appalachian.” It is to widen the denominator.

## Labor-market questions to extract

Feather's material should eventually be coded into concrete migration and labor variables rather than left as atmosphere:

1. origin county and destination county;
2. occupation before migration;
3. cash wage or approximate earnings before migration;
4. destination employer and occupation;
5. destination wage where recoverable;
6. time between arrival and first job;
7. transportation cost;
8. who supplied information about the job;
9. whether the employer recruited directly;
10. relatives or acquaintances already at destination;
11. housing arrangement on arrival;
12. job stability and layoffs;
13. union membership where relevant;
14. return migration or repeated travel home;
15. discrimination or “hillbilly” stereotyping;
16. home ownership and material advancement over time;
17. children's educational and occupational outcomes.

This would turn “people moved north for jobs” into a measurable set of migration decisions, costs, labor-market gains, and risks.

## Chain migration and social infrastructure

A particularly useful theme in Feather is that migration is not adequately modeled as an isolated worker comparing two wage offers. Family networks and earlier migrants reduce uncertainty and transaction costs. They can supply information about openings, transportation, a temporary place to sleep, food, introductions, and a route back home.

For Blackball, this is a reminder that the worker's effective outside options depend partly on social infrastructure. Two people offered the same nominal factory wage may face radically different costs of reaching and surviving at the destination.

The later comparison with Middletown should therefore ask not just “what did Armco pay?” but who already had a relative inside the plant, who could get an interview, who could afford the trip, who had somewhere to stay, and who could survive a failed attempt.

## Stereotype versus population

Feather's explicit anti-stereotype purpose is important when pairing him with *Hillbilly Elegy*. A highly visible memoir can make a handful of dramatic family traits appear representative of an entire regional population. Feather's many-family approach does not automatically solve that sampling problem, but it gives Blackball more variation to work with.

The reading should specifically mark counterexamples:

- stable versus unstable households;
- poor versus relatively prosperous migrants;
- industrial versus nonindustrial jobs;
- urban versus semi-rural settlement in Ohio;
- permanent migration versus return migration;
- strong versus weak preservation of Appalachian identity;
- different responses to hostility from native Ohioans.

The existence of variation is itself evidence against any story in which “Appalachian culture” operates as a single fixed treatment applied to everyone from the region.

## Later work

Feather continued to write about West Virginia, northeast Ohio, local history, photography, and his own family migration.

Recent works include:

- *My Fathers' Land* (2023), a family and migration history following his ancestors and his own movement from West Virginia to Ohio and back;
- *A Gathering of Feathers* (2024), a genealogical and community history centered on the Feather family in Preston County, West Virginia.

The 2024 author biography identifies him as a seventh-generation descendant of Jacob Vatter and Mary Conolly, a retired journalist and professional photographer, and a contributor to *Goldenseal* since 1987.

- https://books.google.com/books/about/A_Gathering_of_Feathers.html?id=ABHK0AEACAAJ

These later books should be treated separately from the 1998 migration study unless a specific claim requires them.

## Comparison with Jack Temple Kirby

Feather and Jack Temple Kirby give Blackball two different resolutions on the same historical transformation.

- **Kirby:** large-scale scholarly history of the southern exodus and the restructuring of rural southern life.
- **Feather:** oral/popular history of particular Appalachian migrants in one northeast Ohio county.
- **Vance:** family memoir that uses both forms of scholarship to locate one family's Kentucky-to-Ohio story inside the wider migration.

That triangle is useful because it permits a direct representativeness test: move from Vance's family, to Feather's community sample, to Kirby's regional history, and record where the story changes at each scale.

- [Jack Temple Kirby](jack-temple-kirby.md)

## Research queue

Blackball should eventually go beyond the secondary descriptions and work through *Mountain People in a Flat Land* itself. Priority questions:

- recover the original newspaper series from which the book developed;
- identify the interview subjects and family cases chapter by chapter;
- separate recollection from contemporaneous documentary evidence;
- identify employers and plants named in the migration stories;
- compare interview claims with census, wage, union, company, and local newspaper records;
- map origin-to-Ashtabula migration chains;
- compare Ashtabula County with Middletown and other Ohio Appalachian destinations;
- inspect whether the parts of Feather's book not cited by Vance complicate, support, or contradict the way *Hillbilly Elegy* frames migration and assimilation.

## Source discipline

Feather's oral-history method is a strength for questions about remembered experience, family networks, stigma, and the texture of migration. It is not by itself a population survey.

Preserve the evidence type for each claim:

- interview recollection;
- newspaper reporting;
- publisher summary;
- scholarly review;
- demographic/statistical evidence;
- Blackball inference.

Do not convert vivid testimony into an unmeasured population rate, and do not discard testimony merely because it is not a census table. The point is to keep the layers visible and compare them.
