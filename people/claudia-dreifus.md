# Claudia Dreifus

## Why Blackball is recording her

Claudia Dreifus is a journalist, interviewer, author, and teacher whose reporting practice is central to understanding *Higher Education?* (2010), coauthored with Andrew Hacker.

The book should not be treated as Hacker's academic argument with Dreifus's name attached. Hacker himself described the collaboration as depending on Dreifus's journalism to keep the project focused on real people. Contemporary accounts of the book emphasize extensive campus visits, conversations, and classroom observation alongside published scholarship and statistics.

That makes Dreifus especially important for reconstructing **how the evidence was gathered**.

## Biographical / professional anchor

- Journalist and interviewer based in New York.
- B.S., New York University.
- Longtime contributor to *The New York Times*.
- For roughly two decades produced the “Conversation with…” interview feature in the *New York Times* Science section.
- Has written for outlets including *Scientific American*, *Ms.*, *Smithsonian*, *The Nation*, *The New York Review of Books*, and others.
- Lecturer at Columbia University's School of Professional Studies / sustainability programs.
- Her Columbia teaching includes science journalism, international reporting, op-ed writing, and personal essays.
- Columbia describes her as the author of six books and a recipient of journalism and teaching honors.

Her reporting career has included work from Central America, Northern Ireland, Burma/Myanmar, South Africa, Chile, and Puerto Rico. Columbia's own descriptions emphasize practical field reporting and interviewing rather than academic prose.

## Role in *Higher Education?*

The book emerged from a collaboration between an academic insider and an experienced reporter. Dreifus's role is visible directly in chapter 3: the chapter opens from her experience as an adjunct instructor denied appropriate office space for student meetings.

The authors later said they spent three years researching the book. Publisher copy describes a road trip across American higher education, and their prose repeatedly refers to their travels, campus observations, and conversations.

A particularly concrete example comes from Arizona State University. In a public discussion around the book, Dreifus described ASU as unusually experimental and singled out Barrett Honors College and the Polytechnic campus; she referred to laboratory work the authors had seen during their visit. This is the kind of evidence Blackball should preserve at its actual scale:

**reported observation at ASU during a particular visit**, not “ASU is objectively better than elite universities.”

## Interviewing as method

Dreifus's larger career makes interviewing itself an important source-method question. Her work is built around asking specialists to explain what they do in language outsiders can understand. At Columbia she has explicitly taught scientists and sustainability students to move away from academic or bureaucratic prose and toward journalism for a general audience.

For *Higher Education?*, that creates both a strength and a limitation.

### Strength

The authors can notice details that aggregate higher-education datasets miss:

- how professors talk about students when speaking informally;
- whether an adjunct has an office;
- who is actually standing in front of a class;
- what a campus chooses to display and fund;
- whether students can get sustained attention from senior faculty;
- what administrators say a program is for.

### Limitation

A reporter's selected cases are not a probability sample. The most vivid anecdote can be true and still atypical.

Blackball should therefore store Dreifus's observations as **field reports with provenance**, then ask what broader data would establish frequency.

## Questions

1. Are Dreifus's reporting notebooks or interview recordings for *Higher Education?* archived?
2. Which campuses did she personally visit?
3. Which interviews did she conduct versus Hacker?
4. How many classroom observations were made, and how were classes selected?
5. Were conversations recorded, transcribed, contemporaneously noted, or reconstructed later?
6. Which interview subjects were anonymized and why?
7. Did institutions know they were being evaluated for a book when the authors visited?
8. How much of the final “Top Ten” came from Dreifus's firsthand reporting?
9. Does *Interview* describe practices that can help evaluate the reliability/provenance of quotations in *Higher Education?*?
10. What changes did Dreifus's reporting introduce relative to Hacker's 2005 NYRB precursor?

## Source discipline

Do not infer that Dreifus supplied all qualitative material and Hacker all statistics. That division is plausible as a rough description of complementary backgrounds but is not yet documented chapter by chapter.

Do not reduce her to “Hacker's wife.” She had a substantial journalism and teaching career independently of their collaboration.

Do not turn journalism into either privileged truth or “mere anecdote.” Preserve the specific observation, source, date, location, and reporting conditions, then seek prevalence or causal evidence separately.
