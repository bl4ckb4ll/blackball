## Why this belongs in Blackball

Kirp's subject is upstream of the syllabus itself: **what institutional machinery determines what can be taught, who gets to teach it, which departments are rewarded, which kinds of knowledge get subsidized, and which are forced to justify themselves as revenue producers?**

The book is therefore useful for a book-first syllabus project because it asks what happens to curricula when universities increasingly organize themselves through markets, rankings, brands, revenue targets, star hiring, donor and corporate relationships, intellectual-property claims, online sales, and competition for tuition-paying students.

Kirp is not making the simple claim that money suddenly invaded a previously moneyless university. Universities have always needed patrons, tuition, land, appropriations, donors, and budgets. His narrower and more interesting claim is that **market language and market incentives increasingly became internal rules for deciding what a university should do**. He repeatedly asks what is gained by those mechanisms and what ceases to be visible when every unit is asked to produce a measurable return.

That distinction matters. A university can buy paper, pay salaries, and balance a budget without treating Shakespeare, physics, philosophy, engineering, admissions, or a research laboratory as competing profit centers.

## The argument in one pass

Kirp moves through concrete institutions rather than building the book around one abstract model. Among the mechanisms he follows are:

- admissions changing from gatekeeping and counseling toward **enrollment management and marketing**;
- universities trying to manufacture or defend prestige through branding and selective recruitment;
- the **superstar academic market**, in which a small number of celebrated faculty command very high salaries and reduced teaching while large amounts of undergraduate teaching move to adjuncts and graduate students;
- **revenue-center management**, where schools and departments are made responsible for generating the income attached to their own enrollments;
- professional schools becoming increasingly self-financing and selling custom instruction to corporate clients;
- university research entangled with patents, sponsors, confidentiality agreements, and proprietary claims;
- the attempt to sell university teaching online as a commercial product;
- the contrasting possibility that giving teaching material away can itself strengthen a university's reputation and public mission;
- for-profit higher education organized around standardized curricula, career credentials, placement, and managerial control.

The title's joke is literal as well as figurative. In the discussion of revenue-center management, the humanities can find themselves pushed to package general-education teaching for maximum enrollment — the kind of institutional setting in which even Shakespeare can become a product line.

Kirp's conclusion is not “abolish markets.” It is closer to: **markets have uses, but a university has purposes that cannot be reduced to market transactions, so markets have to be kept in their place.** The difficult question is where that place is when institutions face real financial pressure.

## Chapter map

### Introduction — “The New U”

The book opens with the claim that the vocabulary of business — customers, products, markets, competition, efficiency, return — has moved from metaphor into university governance. Kirp sets this against older accounts of the university as a community organized around knowledge, scholarship, teaching, and public purposes.

### Part I — The Higher Education Bazaar

**1. “This Little Student Went to Market” — David L. Kirp and Jeffrey T. Holman**  
Admissions becomes an organized market. Colleges do not merely decide whom to admit; they segment applicants, manage yield, manipulate aid, build brands, and compete for the students who improve both tuition revenue and perceived prestige.

**2. “Nietzsche's Niche: The University of Chicago”**  
Chicago becomes a case about institutional distinctiveness, financial constraint, and the attempt under Hugo Sonnenschein to expand undergraduate enrollment and change the university's economic model without destroying the peculiar intellectual culture that made Chicago valuable in the first place.

**3. “Benjamin Rush's ‘Brat’: Dickinson College”**  
A small liberal-arts college illustrates the pressure to identify and market a niche. The case asks what it means for a college to articulate an educational identity when survival itself depends on attracting enough students.

**4. “Star Wars: New York University” — Jonathan VanAntwerpen and David L. Kirp**  
NYU's pursuit of academic stars shows prestige operating as a market. Expensive senior appointments, rankings, departmental reputation, reduced teaching loads, graduate labor, and adjunct labor are not separate stories; they are parts of one institutional allocation system.

### Part II — Management 101

**5. “The Dead Hand of Precedent: New York Law School”**  
A professional school is used to examine managerial reform, institutional inertia, and the problem of changing organizations whose inherited rules and academic customs do not look like those of ordinary firms.

**6. “Kafka Was an Optimist: The University of Southern California and the University of Michigan”**  
This is one of the most useful chapters for Blackball. Kirp examines decentralized budgeting / revenue-center management: academic units are made to experience enrollments, tuition, and costs much more directly. The incentive is supposed to make units responsive and financially responsible. The danger is that departments begin competing with one another for enrollments and teaching revenue, offering courses because they capture money rather than because an institution has made a coherent educational judgment about who should teach what.

**7. “Mr. Jefferson's ‘Private’ College: Darden Graduate School of Business Administration, University of Virginia” — David L. Kirp and Patrick S. Roberts**  
Darden's movement toward self-financing and corporate executive education raises a sharper version of the same question. A school can gain autonomy from public appropriations by finding paying clients, but those clients can then have preferences about curriculum, ownership, confidentiality, and what counts as useful knowledge.

### Part III — Virtual Worlds

**8. “Rebel Alliance: Classics Departments in the Associated Colleges of the South” — Jonathan VanAntwerpen and David L. Kirp**  
Rather than simply letting small classics departments disappear under local enrollment pressure, institutions cooperate. It is an important counterexample: coordination and shared academic infrastructure can preserve subjects that a narrow department-by-department market test might eliminate.

**9. “The Market in Ideas: Columbia University and MIT”**  
The contrast is unusually relevant to an open-knowledge project. Columbia invested heavily in Fathom, an attempt to commercialize online educational material. MIT took a radically different route with OpenCourseWare: publish course materials openly and treat distribution as a public and reputational good. Kirp uses the comparison to ask whether knowledge is most valuable when fenced and sold or circulated.

**10. “The British Are Coming—and Going: Open University”**  
Distance education is treated not as a new invention of the web era but as an institutional form with its own history, economics, and organizational demands.

### Part IV — The Smart Money

**11. “A Good Deal of Collaboration: UC Berkeley” — David L. Kirp and Elizabeth Popp Berman**  
University-industry research collaboration can bring laboratories money and access to applied problems while also creating disputes over patents, disclosure, publication, and the boundary between public scholarship and private advantage.

**12. “The Information Technology Gold Rush: IT Certification Courses in Silicon Valley” — David L. Kirp, Robert Ness, and Debra Solomon**  
Short-cycle credential markets show higher education responding directly to employer demand and rapidly changing technical fashions. The case is useful precisely because some education really is vocational and time-sensitive; the question is not whether such markets exist but what follows when their logic becomes a general model for higher education.

**13. “They're All Business: DeVry University”**  
The proprietary university takes market organization furthest: centralized curriculum, career orientation, placement claims, managerial control, and students treated explicitly as customers buying a credential and occupational path. Kirp asks what conventional universities may learn from that responsiveness while also asking what academic freedom and public purposes look like in that model.

### Conclusion — “The Corporation of Learning”

Kirp returns to the university as a **commons of knowledge**. The central danger is not commerce by itself. It is allowing the price signal to become the only signal: if a teaching or research activity has benefits that are diffuse, delayed, shared, hard to measure, or unavailable for private capture, then a market test systematically understates its value.

## Especially useful Blackball connections

### 1. A syllabus is downstream of a budget

A reading list does not emerge from disembodied faculty taste. Who is employed, which departments survive, how many students a course must attract, whether a professor has time to teach, and whether a subject is regarded as a cost center all affect which books ever reach a classroom.

### 2. “Choice” can be institutionally manufactured

Enrollment figures look like student preferences, but Kirp's admissions and curriculum cases make the causal chain messier. Institutions advertise programs, package courses, set requirements, hire stars, discount tuition, schedule classes, close departments, and distribute advising. What is later reported as demand partly reflects those upstream choices.

### 3. Open access can be an institutional strategy rather than charity

The Columbia Fathom / MIT OpenCourseWare contrast is worth keeping near the center of Blackball. It is a concrete case where attempting to enclose educational material as a product was not obviously the successful market strategy, while giving material away created public value and reputational value at once.

### 4. Internal markets are not automatically real markets

A department inside a university cannot be treated as though it were an independent shop without changing the institution. Courses share students; departments provide prerequisites and general education for one another; scholarship creates benefits that cross unit boundaries; and some subjects are valuable precisely because a university protects them from immediate demand fluctuations. Internal transfer prices can illuminate costs, but they can also manufacture pseudo-competition among pieces of an organization that are supposed to cooperate.

## How reviewers read it

### Ronald G. Ehrenberg — *Journal of Economic Literature* (2004)

Ehrenberg's review is strongly favorable. He emphasizes that Kirp sees economic forces across an unusually broad range of institutions — public and private, undergraduate and graduate, nonprofit and proprietary — and praises the book's ability to explain those forces without turning the argument into a technical economics exercise.

Citation: Ronald G. Ehrenberg, review of *Shakespeare, Einstein, and the Bottom Line*, *Journal of Economic Literature* 42 (2004), 1140–1142.  
Cornell record / copy: https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstream/handle/1813/76056/Ehrenberg235_Review_of_Shakespeare_Einstein_and_the_Bottom_Line.pdf?sequence=1

### John Rothfork — *Education Review* (2004)

Rothfork stresses the mechanisms connecting prestige and money: enrollment management, superstar hiring and adjunctification, revenue-center budgeting, corporate sponsorship, and commercialization of online education. His review is particularly useful for reconstructing the cases and reports that Kirp's project grew out of Kirp's own administrative experience at Berkeley and research supported by the Ford Foundation.

Review: https://educ-review.blogspot.com/2025/02/kirp-david-l-2003-shakespeare-einstein.html

(The page is a later repost; the review itself is dated September 9, 2004.)

### Malcolm Getz — *ILR Review* (2005)

Getz supplies an important skeptical reading. He likes the case studies but argues that Kirp sometimes moves too quickly from vivid cases to a broad thesis about markets eroding higher education's commons. Market responsiveness and entrepreneurship, Getz argues, can coexist with public goods; he also regards some claims about the decline of liberal education as debatable. He finds the proprietary restrictions surrounding academic work especially troubling, while criticizing the book for offering relatively few concrete policy remedies.

Citation: Malcolm Getz, review, *ILR Review* 58, no. 2 (2005): 312–314.  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/001979390505800212

This criticism is worth retaining because it identifies the book's methodological limit: **thirteen revealing cases are not by themselves a causal estimate of the effect of “the market” on higher education as a whole.** The book is strongest as institutional anatomy and comparative case study.

## Credit, thanks, and source graph

The cover says David L. Kirp, but the intellectual object is larger than one name. **Thank you to Kirp for doing the fieldwork and for writing institutional economics as recognizable human institutions rather than as an abstract market diagram.** The book is unusually valuable because it goes inside admissions offices, departments, professional schools, research arrangements, online ventures, and proprietary universities and asks what people actually did when money and educational purpose collided.

Just as importantly, credit the collaborators who are explicitly part of the book:

- **Jeffrey T. Holman** — coauthor on the student-market chapter.
- **Jonathan VanAntwerpen** — coauthor on the NYU and Associated Colleges of the South chapters.
- **Patrick S. Roberts** — coauthor on the Darden chapter.
- **Elizabeth Popp Berman** — coauthor on the Berkeley university-industry chapter.
- **Robert Ness** and **Debra Solomon** — coauthors on the Silicon Valley IT-certification chapter.

And thank the many faculty members, administrators, students, institutional staff, and other interviewees whose organizations supplied the evidence. Rothfork reports that the research was supported by the **Ford Foundation**; that support belongs in the provenance trail too.

The book contains roughly **49 pages of notes**. Blackball should not flatten that source network into “Kirp says.” An exhaustive citation graph requires a page-by-page pass through those notes. This first pass records some of the intellectual ancestors and interlocutors visible in the book and its cases:

- **John Henry Newman**, on the idea and purposes of a university;
- **Karl Jaspers**, likewise on *The Idea of the University*;
- **Thorstein Veblen**, especially *The Higher Learning in America* and the conflict between business management and academic life;
- **Robert Maynard Hutchins**, in the history of Chicago's conception of undergraduate education;
- **Alfred North Whitehead** and **Frederick Rudolph**, in the broader history and philosophy of higher education;
- **Robert H. Frank**, whose work on winner-take-all markets helps make sense of academic superstar competition;
- **Richard Krachenberg**, an early higher-education marketing voice whom Kirp places in the longer development of explicit market thinking;
- **Jane Smiley**, whose *Moo* provides a fictional but recognizable satire of the entrepreneurial university;
- and the many scholars whose movement through NYU's philosophy market makes the “Star Wars” chapter possible, including **Paul Boghossian, Peter Unger, Thomas Nagel, Ned Block, Hartry Field, Stephen Schiffer, Christopher Peacocke, Richard Rorty, Kit Fine, and Andrew Ross**.

Those names should be expanded rather than erased as Blackball gets a full notes-level pass. Scholarship is a graph, not a single-author property label.
