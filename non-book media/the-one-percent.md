# Jamie Johnson — *The One Percent* (2006)

## Why this belongs next to *Born Rich*

Jamie Johnson's second documentary continues the problem posed in *Born Rich* but changes scale. *Born Rich* asks what inherited wealth does to the children who receive it; *The One Percent* asks what concentrated wealth does to a society and what wealthy people think they owe to everyone else.

For Blackball, the most useful way to read the two films together is not as a single portrait of "rich people." They contain sharply different responses to inherited advantage.

Two people should be foregrounded:

- **Josiah Hornblower** in *Born Rich*: a Whitney/Vanderbilt descendant who spent roughly two years working as a machinist in the Texas oil fields.
- **Chuck Collins** in *The One Percent*: a great-grandson of Oscar Mayer who gave away the inheritance that would otherwise have made paid work unnecessary.

These are not representative cases. They are useful contrasting cases.

# Chuck Collins — Oscar Mayer descendant; gave away his inheritance

Collins is the clearest counterexample in Johnson's two documentaries to the assumption that an heir can respond to inherited wealth only by finding personally meaningful work while keeping the financial floor intact.

In the film, Collins says that he is a great-grandson of meatpacker Oscar Mayer and that when he was sixteen his father told him he would inherit enough money that he would never have to work. He then says that he decided to give up that wealth.

The sequence is unusually useful because Collins does not describe the decision as effortless. He recounts writing to his father, whose response was to come to Boston and press him on worst-case scenarios: what if Collins later had a child with a severe disability, for example? Collins says his answer kept returning to the fact that he would then be in the same position as almost everyone else rather than protected by inherited capital.

A machine transcript of the film places this sequence around 42:06–44:17. It should be spot-checked against an inspected recording before exact quotations or frame descriptions are treated as primary evidence.

Independent accounts establish the broad facts more securely:

- Collins says that in 1986, at about age twenty-five, he gave away an inheritance worth almost half a million dollars to the Funding Exchange and other community funds working on social-justice projects.
- A 2004 *New Yorker* profile of philanthropist Zell Kravinsky identifies Collins as a nonfictional example of someone who had already given away a roughly half-million-dollar inheritance during his lifetime.
- Later Institute for Policy Studies material consistently describes him as an Oscar Mayer descendant who relinquished his trust-fund wealth and then spent his career studying inequality, estate taxation, philanthropy, and concentrated wealth.

# Pairing Collins with Josiah Hornblower

Hornblower and Collins are useful together because they represent two materially different attempts to answer the same legitimacy problem.

| | Josiah Hornblower | Chuck Collins |
|---|---|---|
| Film | *Born Rich* | *The One Percent* |
| Inherited position | Whitney/Vanderbilt descendant | Oscar Mayer descendant |
| Deliberate response | Leaves college and works about two years as a machinist in Texas oil fields | Gives away the inheritance that would otherwise make paid work unnecessary |
| What is surrendered | Comfort, status consistency, and time | The personal capital cushion itself |
| What remains | Family history, education, networks, and a financial safety net | Family history, education, networks, and accumulated social advantages even after the money is transferred |
| Blackball use | Work as an attempt to construct independence from inheritance | Renunciation of inheritance as an attempt to place oneself under more ordinary economic constraints |

The important distinction is not "good heir" versus "bad heir." Hornblower's oil-field work can be sincere even though the safety net remains. Collins's giveaway can be sincere even though giving away money cannot erase upbringing, education, family relationships, or social knowledge already acquired.

This comparison is precisely why the Johnson films should not be reduced to a single thesis about how heirs behave. They preserve several incompatible answers to the question of what, if anything, a person should do about advantages they did not earn.
