# The *Up* series — *Seven Up!* to *70 Up*

Checked and updated through 2026-08-31.

## Why it belongs in Blackball

The *Up* series is one of the rare cultural artifacts in which class is not reconstructed retrospectively from adult success. The camera starts when the subjects are children and repeatedly returns to the same people. That makes it possible to compare:

- childhood speech and expectation;
- school pathways;
- occupation;
- marriage and family;
- geography;
- health;
- money;
- political attitudes;
- social mobility;
- and the participants' own changing interpretation of the project.

The original 1964 programme was deliberately built around British class differences. Over time the films became less like a demonstration of a single class thesis and more like fourteen biographies that complicate the thesis.

The series is not a scientific panel survey. Fourteen people cannot represent Britain, participant attrition is nonrandom, the original sample contains only four girls and one nonwhite child, the interviewer/editor has enormous power, and the fact of being filmed may itself affect the subjects. It is still an extraordinary longitudinal primary source.

Official collection:
https://www.itv.com/watch/the-7-up-collection/3a0317

## Origin and method

*Seven Up!* was made for Granada Television's *World in Action* and broadcast in 1964. Paul Almond directed the first programme; Michael Apted, then a researcher, helped find the children and subsequently directed the recurring films through *63 Up*. The initial premise drew on the maxim usually rendered as "give me the child until he is seven and I will show you the man" and used children from sharply different class locations to ask how much future life was already visible in childhood.

The first programme brought twenty children together but the continuing series followed fourteen:
Bruce Balden, Jackie Bassett, Symon Basterfield, Andrew Brackfield, John Brisby, Peter Davies, Susan Davis (later Sullivan), Charles Furneaux, Nicholas Hitchon, Neil Hughes, Lynn Johnson, Paul Kligerman, Suzanne Lusk (later Dewey), and Tony Walker.

The project was not originally designed as a seventy-year longitudinal study. Later participation was voluntary and several subjects skipped installments or permanently withdrew.

# Film-by-film record

## 1. *Seven Up!* — 1964 — age 7

- Director: Paul Almond
- Broadcaster: ITV / Granada, *World in Action*
- Original air date: 5 May 1964
- Approximate length: 40 minutes

The film begins as a class polemic. Children from very different social settings are asked about school, money, work, marriage, race, religion, leisure, and their future. The privileged Kensington boys can already name educational institutions far into the future; working-class children speak from a different horizon of expectation. The construction is intentionally stark.

The strongest evidence is not that seven-year-olds can literally predict their biographies. It is that institutions and expectations are already unequal at seven. Some children know the route through preparatory school, public school, and Oxbridge as ordinary family knowledge. Others encounter work and adult responsibility as a much nearer prospect.

The London Zoo/playground material also stages class contact physically: children who ordinarily inhabit separate institutions are brought together for the camera.

Important methodological limit: the programme's creators selected children to illustrate social extremes. The series should never be cited as a random sample proving a numerical claim about British mobility.

## 2. *7 Plus Seven* / *14 Up* — 1970 — age 14

- Director: Michael Apted
- Original air date: 15 December 1970
- Approximate length: 53 minutes

At fourteen the children can compare their seven-year-old answers with adolescence. School sorting is more visible: some are already moving along the educational paths predicted at seven, while others are closer to leaving school and work.

The film introduces a pattern that becomes central to the project: the archive argues with the present. Apted can replay a confident childhood prediction and ask the teenager what happened to it. This creates extraordinary longitudinal evidence but also gives the filmmaker rhetorical power—the editor chooses which old promise the adult must confront.

The subjects also begin to understand the camera as something that can embarrass them. From this point onward, the series is not merely observing development; it is recording people who know that an earlier self can be played back to them.

## 3. *21 Up* — 1977 — age 21

- Director: Michael Apted
- Original air date: 9 May 1977
- Approximate length: 100 minutes
- All fourteen continuing subjects appear.

At twenty-one the series reaches the point where childhood class forecasts can be compared with university, training, jobs, relationships, and emerging adult identity. Some privileged predictions look almost mechanically accurate. Other lives are already diverging from what the seven-year-old imagined.

The importance of *21 Up* is that class stops looking like a single destination and begins to look like a set of probabilities and resources. Education, confidence, family help, and networks matter, but so do temperament, relationships, health, and decisions.

This is Charles Furneaux's last active installment for decades. His later refusal becomes an important reminder that longitudinal filming depends on continuing consent.

## 4. *28 Up* — 1984 — age 28

- Director: Michael Apted
- Original air dates: 20–21 November 1984
- Approximate total length: 136 minutes
- Charles declines active participation; John appears through archive rather than a new interview.

By twenty-eight, marriage, children, career, unemployment, geographic movement, and mental health have made the biographies too complex to fit the original binary class scheme cleanly. The series becomes recognizably about lives rather than predictions.

Neil's situation is one of the most consequential developments in the entire project. The cheerful child who wanted to become an astronaut is now living precariously and at points homeless. The film forces viewers to confront the limits of a simple "class determines everything" account: childhood location matters, but adult life can also be destabilized by events and conditions that a school-placement chart cannot describe.

Peter's criticism of Thatcher-era Britain later generated hostile tabloid attention and contributed to his long withdrawal from the project. This shows that participation could have direct reputational costs outside the edit itself.

## 5. *35 Up* — 1991 — age 35

- Director: Michael Apted
- Original air date: 22 May 1991
- Approximate length: 116 minutes
- Charles remains out; Symon and Peter do not take part in new filming.

At thirty-five the series is increasingly about family formation, divorce, career consolidation, parenthood, and the participants' relationship to their own childhoods. Viewers can now see not only what happened to the original children but what kind of parents they became.

Attrition matters more here. A participant's absence is data: it may reflect dislike of the project, publicity costs, competing obligations, or a wish not to let Apted define another seven-year chapter.

The film also sharpens a recurring gender problem in the project. The original design gave much more space to boys, and women's lives can be forced into repetitive questions about marriage, children, and domestic happiness. Jackie later challenges Apted directly about this pattern.

## 6. *42 Up* — 1998 — age 42

- Director: Michael Apted
- Original air dates: 21–22 July 1998
- Broadcaster: BBC One
- Approximate length: 133 minutes
- John again declines a new interview; Charles remains absent.

At forty-two, the films contain enough historical footage to show how misleading any single installment can be. A person who looked permanently lost at twenty-eight may be more stable; a stable marriage may have ended; a career may have changed.

Neil reappears in a markedly different situation from *28 Up*: he has completed Open University study, is involved in local politics, and at one point stays with Bruce. The friendship between participants is itself evidence that the project did not simply observe fourteen isolated lives; it also created a weak social network among people who would otherwise probably never have met.

The original class question persists, but the film increasingly emphasizes care, friendship, work satisfaction, and family over occupational rank alone.

## 7. *49 Up* — 2005 — age 49

- Director: Michael Apted
- Original air dates: 15 and 22 September 2005
- Approximate length: 134 minutes
- John returns; Peter and Charles remain absent.

At forty-nine, participants are openly capable of analyzing the series that analyzes them. They criticize Apted's questions, editing, and tendency to fit them back into the original class thesis. This reflexivity is one of the most valuable parts of the later films.

Career and class are still present, but the film gives increasing weight to marriage, children, prejudice, disappointment, and the meaning of ordinary stability. The series has by now accumulated enough footage that a participant can challenge the story the archive seems to tell.

## 8. *56 Up* — 2012 — age 56

- Director: Michael Apted
- Original air dates: 14, 21, 28 May 2012
- Approximate length: 144 minutes
- Peter returns after a long absence.

The age of fifty-six brings retirement planning, grandchildren, illness, austerity, redundancies, and reflection on whether the original class premise was fair. Lynn's experience with cuts affecting her long library career and Jackie's confrontation over disability and benefit policy put institutions back at the center of the series.

Peter's return is also a lesson in participant agency. He had stayed away after hostile publicity and returned decades later, in part around his musical work. The series cannot simply summon its subjects on schedule; they negotiate the value and cost of being seen.

## 9. *63 Up* — 2019 — age 63

- Director: Michael Apted
- Original air dates: 4, 5, 6 June 2019
- Approximate length: 150 minutes
- Lynn had died in 2013 and is represented through a tribute/family material.
- Suzy does not give a new interview.

# The fourteen participants

## Andrew Brackfield

Andrew is one of the three boys chosen from a wealthy Kensington pre-preparatory school. At seven the trio can already name a sequence of elite schools and universities, making them the original programme's clearest illustration of inherited institutional knowledge.

Andrew's actual trajectory broadly follows that expectation: Cambridge (Trinity College), then a legal career as a solicitor, marriage, and children. He is the only one of the three Kensington boys who participates actively in every installment through *63 Up*.

That apparent confirmation of the original thesis needs careful interpretation. The remarkable fact is not that a seven-year-old possessed supernatural foresight. It is that the family/school system had already made an elite educational route so predictable that a child could recite it. The prediction works because institutions are doing work.

In later installments Andrew is useful precisely because he is not a cartoon plutocrat. He talks about family satisfaction and ordinary domestic outcomes. This makes him a good case for separating structural advantage from claims about personal vice or virtue.

At *56 Up*, PBS paired Andrew and John in a clip reflecting on how the series portrayed them:
https://www.pbs.org/pov/films/56up/

## Charles Furneaux

Charles is another of the three Kensington boys. He attends elite schools and goes to Durham University, later undertaking postgraduate study at Oxford. Professionally he becomes a television journalist, producer, and documentary filmmaker; his credits include major factual productions.

Charles's most important role in the *Up* project after age twenty-one is absence. He stops giving new interviews after *21 Up* and becomes critical of the continued use of archival material. The relationship with Apted deteriorates.

That withdrawal matters methodologically. The series is often praised for following "the same people" for decades, but the archive can keep a person inside the story after the person stops cooperating. Charles therefore raises the strongest internal question about ownership of a longitudinal persona: once childhood footage exists, how much control does the adult subject retain over repeated reuse?

Current pre-broadcast material for *70 Up* has reported/anticipated a return, but this dossier does not mark that as completed participation until the programme airs.

Retrieval tags: `elite schooling`, `media`, `documentary ethics`, `withdrawal`, `archive reuse`.

## John Brisby

John is the third Kensington boy. His childhood confidence and knowledge of elite institutions make him one of the original class thesis's strongest cases. He later studies at Oxford and becomes a barrister.

John periodically refuses participation—most notably at twenty-eight and forty-two—then returns. He is also one of the participants most willing to challenge the simplification of his childhood as effortless privilege. Later accounts note that his father died when he was young and that scholarship/support details complicate the visual shorthand of "posh school equals uncomplicated life."

This is an important Blackball warning. Structural privilege is real, but documentary class categories can obscure losses or constraints that do not fit the initial frame. John's objections are evidence against turning the original production design into a total explanation of the person.

His later charitable connection to Bulgaria/family heritage also becomes part of his self-presentation, showing that elite transnational/family ties may matter in ways that a British occupational category does not capture.

## Suzanne “Suzy” Lusk / Dewey

Suzy comes from a wealthy background and is filmed from childhood as one of the four girls in the original fourteen. Her parents' divorce and her own skepticism toward the series become recurring parts of her story.

At twenty-one she is doubtful about marriage; by twenty-eight she is married with children. Over subsequent films she is one of the clearest reminders that statements made at seven, fourteen, or twenty-one are not destiny. The archive can make ordinary changes of mind look like dramatic contradictions.

She repeatedly expresses discomfort with participation and with being turned into a seven-yearly public object. She appears through *56 Up* but declines a new interview for *63 Up*, appearing only in archival material.

For class analysis, Suzy is valuable because her life demonstrates that economic security does not eliminate family disruption, ambivalence, or resistance to publicity. For documentary analysis, she is a long-running case of conditional consent.

## Jackie Bassett

Jackie is introduced with Lynn and Sue as an East End girl. Across the series her life includes marriage, divorce, three sons, multiple jobs, moves, illness, and disputes over disability-related support.

One of her most important contributions to the project is not a biographical event but a methodological confrontation. She challenges Apted for repeatedly asking the women narrow questions about marriage and domestic life while giving men more room to discuss work, politics, and ideas. That critique exposes the gender assumptions built into both the 1964 sample and later interviewing.

By *56 Up* and *63 Up*, Jackie's health, benefits, family losses, and resilience bring the welfare state directly into the longitudinal story. Class is not just childhood accent or school type; it shapes how illness interacts with employment, income, housing, and public policy.

The later films also make clear that being a subject does not make her passive. She argues about the frame, corrects the interviewer, and asserts which parts of her life matter.

## Lynn Johnson

Lynn is introduced with Jackie and Sue in the East End. Her adult life becomes strongly associated with children's education and libraries. She works for decades as a children's/school librarian and is one of the series' clearest examples of public-service work as a source of identity.

Later films show institutional austerity entering an individual biography. Cuts and changes to library services affect her work directly. This is important because it prevents "mobility" from being measured only by salary: Lynn's story concerns commitment to a public institution whose value is not well summarized by income.

She marries Russ, has children and grandchildren, and remains closely connected to the other East End women.

Lynn develops a serious brain condition and dies in May 2013, after *56 Up* and before *63 Up*. She is the first of the original tracked fourteen to die. *63 Up* includes a tribute and family material rather than a new interview.

## Susan “Sue” Davis / Sullivan

Sue is the third of the East End schoolfriends Jackie, Lynn, and Sue. She later works in university administration. She marries young, has children, divorces by the period covered around *35 Up*, and later forms a long-term relationship.

Her biography is useful against two simplifications. First, a working-class childhood does not imply a single occupational outcome: she develops a stable administrative career in higher education. Second, social mobility is not equivalent to becoming culturally unrecognizable from childhood; she remains legible as the same person while institutions and responsibilities change around her.

In a 2019 Radio Times interview Sue says she is proud of the record the series has made while also describing the fear of saying something irreversible because subjects do not control the final edit. That is a concise statement of the exchange at the heart of the project: an extraordinary family archive purchased at the price of public vulnerability.

## Tony Walker

At seven Tony is the energetic East End boy who wants to be a jockey. He does race professionally at a small scale, including at Kempton Park, but does not establish a long-term racing career. He becomes a London taxi driver.

Tony is one of the series' best examples of childhood desire surviving without producing the predicted occupation. Horses remain important, but the adult livelihood becomes taxi work. The result is neither "dream achieved" nor "dream failed" in a simple way.

His taxi career also makes urban knowledge a form of skilled labor. Later films include property, family life, acting-extra work, Spain, grandchildren, marital difficulty, and changing East End demographics.

At *63 Up*, Tony reflects on being publicly criticized after comments in *56 Up* about demographic change. His insistence that he was expressing an East End traditionalist perspective does not settle the interpretation; it supplies evidence about how he understood the controversy. The episode demonstrates how a participant's political/social remarks can become more consequential than the original class experiment.

## Paul Kligerman

Paul is first seen in a children's home with Symon. Soon after the first film his family emigrates to Australia, so the series follows a life that leaves Britain geographically while remaining tied to the original cohort.

His adult work includes manual trades such as bricklaying and later other practical/maintenance work. He marries, has children, and lives a comparatively private life in Australia.

Paul often appears less verbally self-analytical than some participants, but that makes the accumulated footage useful in a different way: housing, work, family stability, aging, and migration are visible without an elaborate theory of self.

His relationship with Symon becomes especially important in *63 Up*. ITV's third episode centers an emotional reunion in Australia between the two men who had been filmed together in care at seven. The scene turns the series back toward childhood institutions: two lives can diverge geographically for decades and still share a memory of care placement that few outsiders possess.

## Symon Basterfield

Symon is the only mixed-race child among the continuing fourteen and begins in the same children's home as Paul. Public accounts describe a difficult family background, including his mother's depression and lack of a relationship with his father.

He marries young, has five children, divorces, later remarries, and becomes involved in fostering. His work includes freight/industrial employment. He skips *35 Up* but returns thereafter.

Symon's story exposes a major weakness in the original design: one nonwhite participant cannot bear the evidentiary burden of representing race in Britain. His experience can be studied as his experience, not converted into a population claim.

His later foster parenting gives the care-home origin a powerful longitudinal echo. A child filmed inside institutional care becomes an adult providing care to other children. That is a meaningful biographical continuity without needing to claim that age seven "predicted" it.

The reunion with Paul in *63 Up* is one of the series' strongest direct comparisons between shared childhood institution and divergent adult geography.

## Nicholas “Nick” Hitchon

Nick grows up in a Yorkshire Dales farming family and is the cohort's rural child. At seven he wants to learn about the Moon; as a teenager his interest in science is already much more concrete.

He studies physics at Oxford, earns a doctorate, moves to the United States in 1982, and spends roughly four decades at the University of Wisconsin–Madison working on plasma/nuclear-fusion-related engineering. He becomes a professor and department chair and publishes extensively.

Nick is therefore a fascinating class case because his path combines rural origin, selective education, science, international migration, and professional-academic status. The original class extremes do not map neatly onto a simple rich/poor ladder.

He also repeatedly says the interviews are uncomfortable and that early films could represent him poorly. Yet he continues participating. This makes him useful for studying the difference between disliking representation and deciding that the historical project is still worth contributing to.

By *63 Up* he is seriously ill with cancer and speaks explicitly about mortality. He retires from Wisconsin in 2022 and dies on 23 July 2023 at age 65. He therefore will not appear in new filming for *70 Up*; any appearance would be archival.

## Peter Davies

Peter is introduced in a Liverpool suburban school alongside Neil. As a child he imagines an ambitious future; by adulthood his route includes university, teaching, later legal work, music, marriage, and children.

His most important relation to the project is his long withdrawal. In *28 Up* he criticizes the Thatcher government. Tabloid coverage attacks him, and he declines *35 Up*, *42 Up*, and *49 Up*. He returns in *56 Up* after roughly twenty-eight years away.

This is one of Blackball's best examples of selection effects. If a participant disappears after a politically costly appearance, later installments are not a neutral panel. The people willing to remain may systematically differ from those who leave.

Peter's return also demonstrates that participation can serve participants' own purposes. Contemporary coverage notes his country/Americana band, The Good Intentions, around the time of his return. That does not invalidate his interview; it means the documentary relationship is negotiated, not one-way extraction.

## Neil Hughes

Neil begins as one of the most cheerful and imaginative seven-year-olds, saying he wants to become an astronaut. The contrast with his later life is the series' most famous warning against treating childhood personality as destiny.

He attends university at Aberdeen but leaves, works various jobs, and by *28 Up* is living precariously and at times homeless in Scotland. Later films document continued mental-health struggles, writing/performance interests, movement around Britain, and eventually a more stable civic life.

By *42 Up* he has completed an Open University degree and serves as a Liberal Democrat councillor in Hackney; Bruce at one point gives him a place to stay. Later he is involved in local politics in Cumbria and lay religious work.

Neil's story can easily be exploited as melodrama, so Blackball should keep several claims distinct:
- the series documents severe instability and homelessness;
- it also documents later political/civic participation and housing stability;
- no single installment is the definitive Neil;
- the archive's emotional power comes partly from repeatedly cutting from adult distress back to the exuberant child.

## Bruce Balden

Bruce begins at boarding/preparatory school and is remembered for missing his father, who was serving abroad in Rhodesia. As a child he imagines a life of service, including missionary aspirations.

He studies mathematics at Oxford, teaches, works for a period in Bangladesh, then teaches in a London comprehensive and later at an independent school in St Albans. He marries and has sons.

Bruce complicates a crude class reading in two directions. His education demonstrates substantial inherited institutional advantage, but his adult choices repeatedly orient toward teaching and service rather than high-paying finance or law. Structural privilege does not dictate occupational values.

His friendship with Neil is also important. By helping Neil during a difficult period, Bruce becomes part of another participant's causal story. The project has created relationships across the class divisions it originally set out to observe.

At *63 Up*, retirement is approaching and he reflects on teaching and aging. Radio Times records him saying the series began as a polemic about class but became much more human over time.

# What the series says about class

## 1. Childhood institutions matter enormously

The early films' strongest prediction is institutional, not psychological. Children already occupy different schools, neighborhoods, family cultures, and expectation systems. Knowing the names of elite institutions at seven is evidence that pathways are socially transmitted long before a university application exists.

## 2. Class predicts constraints better than complete biographies

The films do not show fourteen people mechanically executing a seven-year-old script. They show unequal sets of options. Wealth and elite schooling can lower some risks and open some doors, but they do not determine marriage, illness, happiness, political belief, or every career move.

## 3. Work is not one variable

The series includes professional law, academic science, teaching, taxi driving, library work, administration, freight/manual work, building trades, and periods of unemployment or unstable work. Occupational title alone cannot summarize security, autonomy, social value, or satisfaction.

## 4. The welfare state and public institutions enter the biographies

Libraries, schools, universities, housing, benefits, health care, and local councils are not background scenery. They mediate class outcomes directly. Later films are especially useful because austerity, disability, retirement, and public-service cuts become visible in individual lives.

## 5. Family formation can reproduce or disrupt class

Marriage, divorce, migration, children, foster care, and grandparents change the trajectories. The original films often treated class as a parent-to-child transmission; the later films let viewers see those children become the transmitting generation.

## 6. The documentary itself becomes an institution in their lives

Publicity, embarrassment, friendships with other participants, repeated contact with the production team, and the knowledge that one's childhood can be replayed all become causal features. A serious analysis must consider the observer effect rather than pretending the camera was absent.

# Retrieval cautions

1. Do not turn the original class hypothesis into a law of individual destiny.
2. Do not infer population statistics from fourteen selected lives.
3. Do not treat absence from an installment as missing-at-random.
4. Do not quote a childhood statement as if it were an adult's current belief.
5. Preserve the film date whenever retrieving a view: `Tony@28` and `Tony@63` are different observations.
6. Separate a participant's self-description from Apted's narration and from a critic's interpretation.
7. Record edit/selection effects when a claim depends heavily on juxtaposition with archival footage.
8. Treat *70 Up* as pre-broadcast until it actually airs.
