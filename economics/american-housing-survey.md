# American Housing Survey

## What this is

The **American Housing Survey (AHS)** is a long-running survey sponsored by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development and conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau. It was launched in **1973** as the Annual Housing Survey and has continued, under the current name, as a major source on the U.S. housing stock.

Unlike the Survey of Construction / Characteristics of New Housing, the AHS is not limited to newly constructed homes. Its purpose is to describe the condition, characteristics, costs, and use of the broader housing stock.

Official data page:

- https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/ahs/data.html

History of the survey:

- https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/ahs/about/ahs-introduction-history.html

## 1973 starting point

The first national AHS was conducted in **1973**. Census describes the initial national sample as roughly **60,000 housing units**.

The 1973 national results include reports on:

- general housing characteristics;
- housing and neighborhood quality;
- financial characteristics of the housing inventory;
- characteristics of recent movers;
- financial characteristics cross-tabulated by housing and neighborhood quality.

1973 national summary report:

- https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/ahs/data/1973/ahs-national-report.html

## Public-use microdata

The Census Bureau publishes a **1973 National Public Use File (PUF)** and later public-use microdata. In AHS microdata the basic observational unit is an individual housing unit; public-use records contain most survey information while excluding information that could directly identify a particular housing unit or person.

The 1973 PUF is available directly from Census in CSV and SAS forms, with version-control documentation:

- https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/ahs/data/1973/ahs-national-puf-microdata.html

Technical documentation, including codebooks and questionnaires:

- https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/ahs/tech-documentation.html
- https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/ahs/tech-documentation/def-errors-changes.1973.html

## What it is useful for

AHS can be used to study material living conditions directly rather than using income or GDP as proxies. Depending on survey year and variable availability, it supports questions about matters such as:

- air-conditioning and other equipment;
- dwelling characteristics and size;
- age and condition of the housing stock;
- housing costs and financing;
- rents and owner costs;
- neighborhood and housing-quality indicators;
- mobility and recent movers;
- changes to housing units over time.

Because public-use variables and coding change across survey years, any long-run series should be built from the year-specific codebooks rather than assuming that a current variable name has always meant exactly the same thing.

## Relationship to Characteristics of New Housing

These two sources answer different questions and belong together:

- **Characteristics of New Housing / Survey of Construction**: what was being built in a given year?
- **American Housing Survey**: what did the housing stock and the households occupying it look like?

The first is especially good for technological diffusion in new construction and changing design standards. The second is better for measuring how quickly those changes penetrated the stock actually occupied by households.

## Why keep it in this corpus

Housing is a large component of household consumption and wealth, but its qualitative changes can disappear inside aggregate monetary measures. The AHS makes changes in material conditions observable in their own terms: what people lived in, what equipment the dwelling had, what it cost, and how the stock changed.

Placed next to long-run GDP series such as Maddison's, this provides a useful check on what rising measured output corresponded to in ordinary physical living conditions.

## Reuse / preservation note

The files linked above are explicitly **public-use** Census microdata and statistical products. Census policy encourages citation, reuse, replication, and verification. Census Bureau employee-created works generally are not subject to U.S. copyright protection, although source and provenance metadata should be preserved.

Citation policy:

- https://www.census.gov/about/policies/citation.html

A local mirror of selected PUF releases is therefore a reasonable preservation project. If mirrored, retain the original Census filename, survey year, version, source URL, retrieval date, and accompanying codebook/version-control file so the bytes remain interpretable later.
