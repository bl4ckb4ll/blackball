# Automotive technician pay — career-claim audit

This dossier tests a narrow but reusable Blackball question:

> When somebody advertises a skilled occupation using a high annual earnings figure, what does a new entrant actually have to do, buy, risk, and survive before that figure becomes attainable — and what fraction of workers ever reach it?

Automotive service is a strong case because the occupation has all of the following at once:

- a visible shortage narrative;
- employer and celebrity advocacy for vocational education;
- specific six-figure claims;
- a compensation system whose quoted "hourly rate" may not correspond to clock hours at work;
- worker-owned capital in the form of tools;
- formal and informal training costs;
- substantial physical demands and injury risk;
- a large enough occupation for government wage distributions to exist;
- direct practitioner testimony describing the same mechanisms from inside the trade.

The conclusion should not be "trades bad" or "college good." The point is to audit the transaction with the same standards Blackball applies to college.

## The headline claim

In November 2025 Ford CEO Jim Farley said Ford dealerships had about 5,000 mechanic openings and described the work as a "$120,000 a year job," while also saying it takes about five years to learn the work. Contemporary coverage traces the remarks to *Office Hours: Business Edition*.

This is an unusually testable career claim because the relevant population, training path, compensation mechanics, and wage distribution can all be investigated.

Source trail:

Do not silently rewrite Farley's statement as "every Ford technician is offered a guaranteed $120,000 salary." The later reporting interprets the figure as an attainable high-end outcome after years of skill acquisition, not ordinary starting pay.

## Denominator first: what technicians actually earn

BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, May 2025:

- national median, automotive service technicians and mechanics: **$50,620**;
- bottom 10 percent: below **$34,660**;
- top 10 percent: above **$81,790**.

BLS motor-vehicle-and-parts-dealer industry data, 2025:

- median automotive service technician/mechanic pay: **$58,140**;
- mean: **$59,290**.

Thus $120,000 is not a description of the middle of the occupation or even the middle of dealership technicians. It is more than twice the dealer median and substantially above the national 90th-percentile threshold.

Sources:

- https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/automotive-service-technicians-and-mechanics.htm
- https://www.bls.gov/iag/tgs/iag441.htm

Blackball rule: never answer "what can this occupation pay?" with a maximum, anecdote, or exceptional worker when a distribution exists. Record the denominator and quantiles first.

## A quoted hourly rate is not necessarily a clock-hour wage

The U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division describes the dealership "flat rate" system explicitly. A flat-rate hour is **not an actual clock hour**. Jobs are assigned a standard number of billable/credited hours; the technician may receive more or fewer credited hours than the number of hours physically spent at work depending on completed jobs.

That distinction changes the meaning of statements such as "$42 an hour" or "$45 an hour." They may describe compensation per credited flat-rate hour rather than guaranteed pay for every hour present at the shop.

Primary source:

- DOL Wage and Hour Division, Field Operations Handbook, chapter 21, section 21h04(d): https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/field-operations-handbook/Chapter-21

### Risk allocation

Under flat rate, part of ordinary business/workflow risk can land on the worker. Relevant variables include:

- whether enough cars arrive;
- how work is assigned among technicians;
- whether the technician has repeatedly done a particular repair and can beat book time;
- diagnosis time;
- difficult fasteners or previous bad repairs;
- waiting for parts, approvals, or service-advisor decisions;
- warranty/recall labor allowances;
- changes in vehicle design that reset accumulated speed advantages.

The mechanism is not that every flat-rate technician loses money. Experienced specialists can benefit enormously from beating standard times. The Blackball question is **who bears each source of variance and at what career stage**.

## Best-case worker versus ordinary worker

The Wall Street Journal's 2026 reporting provides a particularly useful matched contrast.

A Ford Senior Master Technician, Ted Hummel, was reported at about **$160,000** in 2025. The accompanying podcast explains that:

- he had nearly two decades of experience;
- he had reached Ford's highest training designation described in the report;
- his flat-rate skill let him complete some jobs much faster than the standard credited time;
- about one third of his wages came from being paid to teach apprentices;
- the reporter characterized earnings at that level as roughly top-5-percent territory rather than the ordinary mechanic experience.

The same report follows Russell Wickham, who described a very different path:

- an early 40-clock-hour week producing a 20-flat-rate-hour paycheck;
- an example in which a nominal $26 flat rate worked out to about $16.36 per clock hour present;
- annual mechanic earnings ranging from about $26,000 to $89,000 over his career;
- leaving the occupation after concluding that the compensation no longer justified the demands, before later returning for reasons including enjoyment of the craft.

These are individual cases, not a representative sample. Their value is that they make the flat-rate mechanism concrete and expose why the same occupation can generate both spectacular earnings stories and severe dissatisfaction.

## Entry and training are not zero-cost

Ford itself operates several technician-entry routes rather than treating competence as instantaneous.

### ASSET

Ford's ASSET FAQ says:

- typical duration: **24 months**;
- alternation between classroom education and work at a sponsoring Ford/Lincoln dealership;
- typically an Associate of Applied Science in Automotive Technology;
- a basic set of automotive hand tools is typically required.

Ford describes the program as a paid co-op / earn-while-you-learn route and says an ASSET graduate has up to a year of dealer experience by graduation.

Sources:

- https://www-newfordtech.app.ford.com/Home/FAQ/ASSET
- https://www-newfordtech.app.ford.com/Programs/ASSET/Overview

### Maintenance & Light Repair

Ford describes MLR as **9–12 months** of specialized training aimed at entry-level dealership or Quick Lane work.

Source:

- https://www-newfordtech.app.ford.com/Programs/MLR

### FACT

Ford describes FACT as a **15-week Ford-specific program** layered on top of UTI/NASCAR Tech automotive training; the FAQ lists prior/core automotive training as a prerequisite.

Sources:

- https://www-newfordtech.app.ford.com/Programs/Fact/Overview
- https://www-newfordtech.app.ford.com/Home/FAQ

These are different routes and should not be collapsed into one "five-year training program." Farley's five-year statement concerns the time to learn high-skill work at the level he was discussing; Ford's formal entry programs have their own durations and credentials.

## Worker-owned tools are capital

BLS says employers usually own computerized diagnostic and larger power/shop equipment, while service technicians **typically own their common hand tools themselves**.

Ford's ASSET FAQ likewise says a basic automotive hand-tool set is typically required for students.

The WSJ reporting adds examples of much larger accumulated tool/toolbox expenditures. Treat those individual numbers as reporting examples, not a universal required startup bill.

Blackball should record separately:

1. minimum tools required to begin a specific program/job;
2. tools supplied by the school/dealer;
3. worker-owned tools accumulated over time;
4. replacement, calibration, financing, and theft/loss costs;
5. whether a quoted wage comparison subtracts those costs.

Sources:

- https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/automotive-service-technicians-and-mechanics.htm
- https://www-newfordtech.app.ford.com/Home/FAQ/ASSET

## Physical cost belongs in the transaction

BLS states that automotive service technicians commonly work with greasy parts and tools, sometimes in uncomfortable positions; may lift and maneuver heavy objects; stand for most of the day; and may be exposed to fumes, chemicals, and noise. BLS also says the occupation has **one of the highest rates of injuries and illnesses of all occupations**.

This does not mean the occupation is uniquely intolerable, nor that injury is inevitable. It means a career comparison that counts only tuition and gross wages is incomplete.

Source:

- https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/automotive-service-technicians-and-mechanics.htm

Ford's current New Ford Tech recruitment material says its programs can provide a **"stable and lucrative career path"** and separately describes technician work as **"A JOB THAT COMMANDS RESPECT."**

Those are marketing claims, not wage statistics. They belong in the dossier because Blackball should compare seller/recruiter language with measurable outcomes rather than throwing the marketing away after enrollment.

## Southeast Michigan reality check

A small current-ad snapshot shows why local job advertisements should be retained rather than replacing them with national rhetoric:

- Varsity Ford, Ann Arbor: light-repair technician listing indexed at **$18–$22/hour**;
- Champion Automotive Group, Ypsilanti: mechanic/technician listing indexed at **$75,000–$100,000/year**, asking for 2–5+ years of experience;
- Ed Koehn Automotive Group, Greenville: Master Certified Ford Technician indexed at **$30–$42/hour**;
- Briarwood Ford, Saline: third-party indexed Senior Master Technician listings have appeared around **$65,000–$110,000**, with Ford certification and roughly five years' experience requested.

These search indexes are volatile secondary records, not a frozen population census. Preserve dated screenshots or first-party postings before using them as durable evidence.

## Reusable Blackball career-claim test

For college, trade school, apprenticeship, certification, boot camp, or employer-sponsored training, record:

1. **Exact claim** — "can earn," "average," "median," "starting," "up to," guaranteed salary, commission, flat rate, etc.
2. **Population/denominator** — all workers, employed completers, dealership workers, top specialists, survivors after five years, etc.
3. **Distribution** — count, median, quantiles, top share; never only a high-water mark.
4. **Clock time versus paid units** — especially piece rate, commission, flat rate, billable hours, gig pay.
5. **Training time** — formal education plus time to competence and time to advertised earnings.
6. **Cash cost/debt** — tuition, fees, licensing, tools, transportation, relocation.
7. **Worker capital** — tools, vehicle, workspace, computer, uniforms, insurance.
8. **Risk allocation** — who absorbs slow demand, rework, warranty time, cancellations, bad leads, downtime.
9. **Physical/schedule cost** — injury, wear, nights/weekends, travel, weather, schedule volatility.
10. **Attrition** — what fraction leave before reaching the advertised state and where they go.
11. **Local continuity** — do relevant jobs exist near the worker, and did comparable postings persist over the full training horizon?
12. **Enforceability** — what does the student/worker owe regardless of outcome, and what does the seller/employer owe if the promised outcome never appears?
13. **Seller-controlled reporting** — who defines employment, success, placement, earnings, and exclusions?
14. **Counterfactual** — what could the same person earn/build/learn during the training interval without buying the intervention?

The same audit should be applied symmetrically. A trade advocate does not get to advertise the exceptional master technician as typical; a university does not get to advertise its exceptional graduate as typical either.
