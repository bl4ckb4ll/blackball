# Robert Frost — “Two Tramps in Mud Time” (1934)

Robert Frost's **“Two Tramps in Mud Time”** belongs in Blackball as a compact literary treatment of the difference between **work chosen for its own sake** and **work needed for income**.

The speaker is splitting wood in his own yard because he enjoys the activity. Two men recently associated with lumber camps appear and want the job for pay. The poem therefore puts the same manual task into two different economic positions: the person who can do it voluntarily and the people who need somebody to pay them for doing it.

See also: [Robert Frost — “The Death of the Hired Man” (1914)](robert-frost-death-of-the-hired-man-1914.md).

## Publication

- First published in *The Saturday Review of Literature*, October 6, 1934.
- Dartmouth's Robert Frost collection and Amherst College's Frost periodicals inventory both record that appearance.
- Collected in *A Further Range* (1936).

## Blackball reading

The poem is useful because it does not reduce the issue to **worker versus nonworker**. The speaker presents himself as skilled with an axe, while the two strangers are associated with lumber work. The difference is instead control and necessity.

Questions to preserve:

- Who owns the wood, tools, and place where the work occurs?
- Who can afford to perform the task without pay?
- Who controls the pace, duration, and stopping point?
- Does enjoyment of manual work survive when the same work is done for wages under somebody else's terms?
- When one person's recreation is another person's livelihood, what moral weight should economic need have?
- Is the ability to turn necessary labor into a hobby itself partly a class advantage?

The ending's aspiration to unite vocation and avocation is worth taking seriously without assuming that labor markets ordinarily make love and need coincide.

## Evidence boundary

This is a poem, not labor-market data. It does not establish unemployment rates, prevailing wages, the representativeness of the two men, or a general rule that material need gives somebody a superior claim to every available task. Its value is to state a labor and class problem unusually clearly.
