# An ordinary lunch: food, need, and available money — first-person field note

**Recorded:** 2026-10-16  
**Source class:** Contemporaneous first-person account provided by a Blackball participant in conversation. Not independently corroborated; no interview, receipt, photograph, or independently verified recipient history is available.  
**Location:** Deliberately not specified; do not identify or speculate about the recipient.

## Account

The narrator saw a man he understood to be homeless and hungry while out on an errand. They made eye contact. The narrator did not report that the man demanded food, presented credentials, or supplied a story to establish whether he deserved help.

The narrator bought a sandwich for himself and the same kind of lunch for the man. He also bought grape leaves, offered some to the man, and reports that the man said he had never tried them. The purchase involved real, limited money: the narrator had enough for two lunches, but not an unlimited budget for every possible recipient of assistance.

The narrator was struck by how ordinary the decision was: someone appeared to need food; another person could afford a meal; the second person bought the first a meal. No inquiry into the recipient's work history, moral character, or future productivity was necessary to make that particular decision.

The narrator also expressed anger at the apparent indifference of people passing by, and at a world capable of producing and selling abundant food in which someone can nevertheless go hungry. He did not record the total number of passersby, what each person saw, whether they could help, or whether anyone had assisted the man earlier. Those are unknown, not observations.

The same day, the narrator reported that a forthcoming good-paying job offer was reducing the immediate pain and anger associated with his own earlier economic insecurity. He maintained that relief from personal desperation had not invalidated his observations of other workers' and unhoused people's difficulties.

He contrasted two positions:

- **After access to paid employment:** once a position actually starts and payroll operates, working scheduled hours can produce a comparatively predictable stream of income.
- **Before access:** applying, finding a willing employer, maintaining an address, transport, communications, and sufficient stability to keep searching can be materially difficult, especially without housing. The narrator has worked beside economically desperate people and reports seeing some treated as unusually vulnerable to employer mistreatment.

The narrator's first-person employment experiences merit preservation as testimony. They do not establish how often particular employers mistreat unhoused workers, or the universal claim that an unhoused person cannot get hired. The distinction between observed cases, plausible mechanisms, and measured prevalence is part of the finding, not grounds for throwing the account away.

## What this observation establishes — and does not

**Direct first-person report:** the narrator describes his purchase and exchange; his own limited budget; his response to eye contact; his anticipation of better wages; and workplace conduct he says he has personally witnessed.

**Interpretation:** the absence of a need to prove moral merit before giving a meal; the tension between readily purchasable food and unmet immediate needs; and the possible loss of bargaining power when workers have very few alternatives.

**Not established:** the recipient's full circumstances or previous meals; what other passersby knew or did; food insecurity rates; the availability of local public and private assistance; or the causal effect of homelessness on hiring and workplace treatment.
