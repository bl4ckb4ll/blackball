## What the mismatch means

The main divergence is produced by **composition**.

A national manufacturing index combines semiconductors, transportation equipment, machinery, food, fabricated metals, defense/space, chemicals, and many other industries. In July 2026, expanding categories and strong new orders were sufficient to produce a high PMI and positive industrial production even while the same ISM report documented high input prices, steel shortages, freight problems, and negative respondent comments.

Minnesota Implement effectively has a much narrower private price-and-demand index:

> steel inputs + freight + farm/compact-equipment demand + snowfall + small-shop purchasing power

That bundle can deteriorate while U.S. manufacturing in aggregate expands.

## What would count as a genuine contradiction?

A stronger test would compare the shop against data with nearly the same exposure set. For example:

- Minnesota fabricated-metal manufacturers reporting falling input prices while Koeckeritz reports rapidly rising ones;
- Minnesota compact/farm equipment retail sales rising strongly while his relevant orders collapse;
- LTL quotes for comparable West Concord → East Coast bulky freight falling while his shipping quotes rise;
- normal/high local snowfall while he attributes weak replacement demand to a low-snow winter.

The sources collected here mostly **do not** produce those contradictions. Instead, the narrow public measures generally corroborate the direction of his experience, while broad national measures look considerably stronger.

## Research implication

For future Blackball economic field reports, store both:

1. the **micro account** — prices actually quoted, units sold, customer behavior, lead times, staffing, margins, cancellations, and owner expectations; and
2. the **matched public vector** — geography × industry × input × customer sector × freight mode × weather/product shock.

Then compare the micro account first to its matched vector and only secondarily to GDP, unemployment, national PMI, or broad inflation. This makes it possible to study the distribution of economic experience rather than asking an under-specified question about whether “the economy” is good or bad.
