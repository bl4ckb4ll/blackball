## Same owner, contemporaneous detailed account — March 2026

The March ABC 6 / KAAL interview is useful because it gives concrete figures from the same proprietor only months before the August video.

Koeckeritz reported:

- At the Owatonna Farm and Power Show, the firm sold **seven units outright**, versus his description of a more typical one or two units at the show followed by a trickle of sales. This is a reminder that the shop's evidence is not uniformly negative.
- Steel prices for the shop had risen **30–60% on most things**.
- He gave a plate-of-steel example that had moved from about **$600 to $1,800–$2,200**, and tubing from about **$150 to $600**. These are individual procurement examples, not price indexes.
- Roughly **70% of the business** was described as almost entirely snowplows.
- Mild Minnesota winters reduced replacement demand because customers' existing plows had seen less use.
- Shipping an average eight-foot plow to the East Coast had risen from roughly **$600 to $1,400–$1,800**; he said only two East Coast plows sold that year because customers resisted paying freight of roughly $1,400 on a $3,000 plow.
- Some less-common metal inputs were unavailable because mills were concentrating on higher-margin products; the shop sometimes had to source materials through retail channels such as Amazon or eBay.
- He compared the business environment with the 2008 crash, when the company eventually closed for two years before rebuilding, and with the COVID disruption after its move to West Concord.
- His own model of the downturn is cyclical: survive until activity finds a bottom and then participate in the recovery.

## Interpretation guardrails

This is high-value evidence about **one firm's incidence of economic conditions**. It is not, by itself, evidence that national output, employment, or GDP are falling.

The firm has an unusually concentrated exposure profile:

1. steel-intensive production;
2. bulky products with expensive outbound freight;
3. farm and small-equipment customers sensitive to capital budgets;
4. snow-removal products sensitive to local weather;
5. small-volume purchasing with less bargaining power and fewer substitute specifications than a very large manufacturer.

Those exposures should be tested individually against public data rather than collapsed into a single question such as “Is the economy good or bad?”
