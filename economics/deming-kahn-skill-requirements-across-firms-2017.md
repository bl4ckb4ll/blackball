## Why this matters for Blackball

This paper is useful evidence against treating an occupation as though it has one fixed employer-independent bundle of requirements.

The authors analyze skill language in job postings and group a wide range of advertised keywords into ten broad skill categories. They find **substantial variation in advertised skill requirements even within narrowly defined occupations**.

For a worker, that means a statement such as:

> “This occupation requires A, B, and C.”

can hide substantial firm-level variation. One corporation may emphasize A, another B, another a combination of A and social or organizational skills, even when all three are nominally hiring for the same occupation.

## Main result relevant here

The paper finds large differences in skill demand across firms and local labor markets, including within narrowly defined occupations.

The authors focus especially on cognitive and social skills and report positive relationships between those advertised skills and measures of pay and firm performance. They also find evidence that cognitive and social skill demands complement one another.

For Blackball's purposes, the most important result is the **heterogeneity itself**: the wording and skill bundle attached to a job title are partly firm-specific rather than simply properties of the occupation.

## Worker-side interpretation

This undermines simplistic career advice based on occupational averages.

A worker who is weak on requirement C may be a bad match for one firm's version of a job and a plausible match for another firm's version of what appears to be the same job. Therefore useful job-search advice should compare the worker not only with an occupational title but with the **distribution of requirements across actual firms and vacancies**.

That creates a practical search problem:

- Which employers emphasize the skills this worker already has?
- Which requirements vary enough across employers that searching more broadly is cheaper than acquiring another credential?
- Which combinations recur together?
- Which employers ask for unusually broad bundles relative to their pay?
- How much does local labor-market tightness change advertised requirements?

## Important limitation

This is still a **job-posting study**. It measures what firms advertise, not the complete realized hiring rule.

It therefore cannot by itself tell us:

- whether a listed skill was actually required of the person hired;
- whether strong A compensated for missing B;
- how many applicants had all listed qualifications and were rejected;
- which requirements recruiters or managers waived;
- how referrals, interviews, salary expectations, or vacancy duration affected selection.

So this paper should not be used to claim that posting language equals hiring behavior. Its value is different: it shows that even **stated demand varies substantially between firms inside the same occupation**.

## Blackball research use

Combine this finding with studies of realized hiring.

A useful worker-facing system should distinguish at least three layers:

1. **occupation-level convention** — what is commonly associated with the job title;
2. **firm/posting-level stated demand** — what this employer says it wants now;
3. **revealed hiring behavior** — what characteristics its actual hires possess and which stated requirements appear to be waived.

The third layer is the one workers ultimately care about, but Deming and Kahn show why collapsing the first two layers is already a serious mistake.

## Possible worker-facing metrics

- **within-occupation requirement dispersion** — how much postings for the same occupation differ across firms;
- **skill-bundle frequency** — frequency of A, B, C, A+B, A+C, etc.;
- **firm-specific requirement premium** — requirements unusually common at a particular firm relative to the occupation;
- **pay-to-requirement ratio** — compensation relative to breadth or rarity of advertised demands;
- **search-versus-train comparison** — whether finding another employer with a different skill bundle appears cheaper than acquiring the missing credential.

The purpose is to help a worker search the actual landscape rather than treating a generic occupational description as destiny.
