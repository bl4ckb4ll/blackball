# Paul Lafargue — *The Right to Be Lazy* (1880/1883)

Paul Lafargue's *The Right to Be Lazy* (*Le Droit à la paresse*) is one of the sharpest nineteenth-century attacks on treating work as a moral good in itself. It belongs in Blackball alongside Keynes's later “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren,” but Lafargue is much less cautious: he attacks the working class's internalized “love of work,” argues that machinery should reduce necessary labor rather than intensify production, and repeatedly proposes a maximum working day of roughly **three hours**.

The remembered “anarchist essay” label is understandable because the text circulates widely in anarchist and anti-work collections, including The Anarchist Library. But Lafargue was not an anarchist at this stage. He was a leading French socialist/Marxist, helped found the French workers' party, married Laura Marx, and was Karl Marx's son-in-law.

## Publication and provenance

- Author: **Paul Lafargue** (1842–1911).
- Original French title: ***Le Droit à la paresse***.
- Lafargue says in the 1883 preface that the piece first appeared as a refutation of the “Right to Work” in the weekly ***L'Égalité*** in **1880**, second series.
- The expanded/reissued text is commonly dated **1883**; the Charles H. Kerr English translation is the standard English text reproduced by Project Gutenberg and the Marxists Internet Archive.
- Lafargue wrote the 1883 preface from **Sainte-Pélagie Prison**.

This note therefore uses **1880/1883** rather than pretending there is only one clean publication date.

## The central claim

Lafargue's target is not simply bad working conditions. He attacks the moralization of work itself: the idea that industriousness, ever-longer work, and ever-greater production are inherently virtuous.

His argument is roughly:

1. industrial capitalism trains workers to treat work as a duty and even a passion;
2. long hours exhaust workers and their families;
3. machinery and productivity could instead reduce necessary labor;
4. under capitalism, increased productive capacity instead produces periodic overproduction, unemployment, colonial market-seeking, and pressure to consume more goods;
5. workers should therefore demand a radical reduction in working time rather than merely demand more work.

The famous concrete proposal is not vague. Lafargue says workers must accustom themselves to **working only three hours a day**, leaving the rest for “leisure and feasting,” and later imagines a law forbidding anyone to work more than three hours daily.

## “Enough to get by” versus accumulation

For the Blackball theme, the useful point is that Lafargue rejects the assumption that every gain in productive capacity ought to be converted into **more production, more work, more sales, and more accumulation**.

That is close to the recurring “enough to get by and no more” question, but it is not exactly the same claim as a subsistence farmer refusing a cash crop. Lafargue is writing about an industrial working class and argues for using productivity to create leisure while still producing and consuming enough goods to satisfy social needs.

So the connection should be kept at the level of the underlying choice:

> When people can produce enough for a decent life with less labor, must the surplus capacity be turned back into more work and more accumulation, or can people simply stop working sooner?

That makes the essay a useful counter-source to economic narratives that treat maximum labor-force participation, output growth, consumption, career advancement, and accumulation as self-evident human goals.

## Machinery is supposed to buy time

One of the strongest Blackball-relevant pieces of the essay is Lafargue's treatment of machinery. He does not oppose machinery as such. His complaint is that labor-saving machinery under capitalist organization fails to save workers labor.

He argues that reducing hours and making labor more expensive can push employers toward better machinery, while social ownership of productive technology could turn mechanical productivity into **leisure and liberty** rather than alternating overwork with unemployment.

That distinguishes two questions that are often collapsed:

- **technical productivity:** how much can be produced per hour of human labor?
- **social use of productivity:** are the gains taken as more output and accumulation, or as less compulsory labor?

## Overproduction and forced expansion

Lafargue also links excessive work to overproduction. In his account, goods pile up, capital searches for outlets, governments and firms search abroad for markets, and colonial expansion becomes one way of finding consumers and investment opportunities.

His account is polemical and should not be treated as a complete theory of nineteenth-century imperialism. But as a source it is valuable because it explicitly connects the work ethic to a political economy in which society must continually find new reasons and new markets for producing more.

## Comparison with Keynes

Blackball already has John Maynard Keynes's 1930 “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren.” The pairing is useful.

**Lafargue, 1880/1883:**
- attacks the moral cult of work;
- wants a maximum working day around three hours;
- treats machinery as a possible route to liberation from toil;
- emphasizes overproduction and the absurdity of producing beyond useful need.

**Keynes, 1930:**
- predicts that productivity could eventually solve the basic “economic problem”;
- imagines three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week;
- distinguishes absolute wants from relative/status wants;
- treats the future problem as learning how to use freedom from economic necessity.

They reach a strikingly similar question from different political traditions: **what happens if technology makes a decent material life possible with much less labor, but society keeps organizing status and morality around work anyway?**

See: [John Maynard Keynes — “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren”](economic-possibilities-for-our-grandchildren-1930.md).

## Classification caution

Do not flatten this into “anarchists oppose work.” Lafargue's political position was Marxist/socialist, and his argument is not that all purposeful activity should disappear. He distinguishes compulsory industrial toil from leisure, feasting, art, science, physical activity, and freely chosen pursuits. The polemical word **lazy** is deliberately aimed at the moral prestige of wage labor.

The Anarchist Library is therefore useful as evidence of the essay's later circulation in anarchist/anti-work traditions, not as evidence that Lafargue himself should be classified as an anarchist in 1883.

**Directly inspectable:**
- the English text itself;

- his treatment of machinery, overproduction, leisure, and consumption;

**Contextual but separately sourced:**
- Lafargue's role in the French socialist/Marxist movement;
- his marriage to Laura Marx and relationship to Karl Marx.

**Interpretive Blackball connection:**
- the comparison with “enough to get by” choices and with Keynes;
- the use of the essay as a counterexample to treating ever-higher production, employment, consumption, and accumulation as automatically desirable.

The last category is an analytical use of the source, not a claim that Lafargue was writing about Reconstruction-era freedpeople, subsistence farming, or any other specific case outside his own argument.
