# Robert Frost — “The Death of the Hired Man” (1914)

Robert Frost's **“The Death of the Hired Man”** belongs in Blackball as a literary source on aging, seasonal wage labor, dependence, dignity, practical skill, education, and the boundary between an employer's economic obligation and a broader human obligation to a worker.

The poem appears in *North of Boston* (1914). Its central event is a conversation between Warren and Mary about Silas, an aging hired man who has returned to their farm exhausted and ill. Warren remembers him primarily as an unreliable worker who left during haying time when labor was scarce. Mary sees a sick old man trying to preserve some self-respect. Before Warren can settle the question, Silas dies.

See also: [Robert Frost — “Two Tramps in Mud Time” (1934)](robert-frost-two-tramps-in-mud-time-1934.md).

## Why Blackball should keep it

### Wage dependence and dignity

Silas wants enough pay to avoid begging or being dependent on somebody else's generosity. The poem makes clear that paid work is not only an income source for him; it is one of the remaining ways he can claim adult independence and self-respect.

That makes the poem useful for separating two things that are often collapsed:

- whether a worker is economically productive enough for an employer to want him;
- whether a person still needs income, standing, and a way to avoid humiliating dependence.

Age can make those two diverge sharply.

### Seasonal labor and bargaining power

Warren's complaint is that Silas leaves during haying season, exactly when farm help is scarce and another employer can tempt him away with more money, then returns when Warren has less use for him.

That is a small labor-market model inside the poem:

- labor demand changes by season;
- the worker moves when outside offers improve;
- the employer interprets that move as disloyalty;
- the worker later needs the old relationship again;
- neither side has a stable long-term contract that fully resolves the conflict.

Blackball should preserve the symmetry. An employer may regard a worker as replaceable when demand is low; a worker may regard the employer as replaceable when demand is high. Moral language about loyalty can conceal changing bargaining power on either side.

### Practical knowledge versus formal education

Silas remembers Harold Wilson, a younger farm worker who went through college and later taught there. Their old arguments mix manual skill, folk knowledge, Latin, schooling, and confidence. Silas wants another chance to show Harold how to build a load of hay properly.

This is directly relevant to Blackball's credential questions. The poem does **not** prove that schooling is useless or that practical workers are always right. In fact, one of Silas's examples of allegedly superior practical knowledge is dowsing with a hazel branch, which is not a sound empirical technique. That is exactly why the passage is useful: practical experience can contain real skill and bad beliefs at the same time, while formal education can confer real knowledge and status without erasing the value of occupational competence.

The correct Blackball question is not “books or experience?” It is: **which claimed competence is actually demonstrated, what is the credential doing, and who gets authority from each kind of knowledge?**

### Employer, family, and social obligation

Silas has a wealthy brother but does not go to him. Instead he returns to the farm. Warren and Mary therefore argue over what “home” means and who has to take someone in when ordinary economic reciprocity has failed.

The poem is useful for questions that wage accounting alone cannot answer:

- When a long-term worker can no longer produce enough to justify his wage, does the relationship end completely?
- Does past service create any residual obligation?
- Why might a person prefer dependence on an employer-like household to dependence on family?
- How much social protection historically came through personal relationships because formal retirement, unemployment, disability, and welfare institutions were weak or absent?

## Evidence boundary

This is a narrative poem, not representative evidence about early twentieth-century farm labor. It should generate questions, not prevalence estimates. The poem does not tell us how typical Silas was, what his exact wages were, or how often farmhands moved between employers this way.

## Publication and public text

*North of Boston* was first published in London by David Nutt in 1914. “The Death of the Hired Man” is included in the collection. A 1915 Henry Holt U.S. edition is fully digitized, and Project Gutenberg distributes *North of Boston* as public domain in the United States.
