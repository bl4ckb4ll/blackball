# John Maynard Keynes — “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren” (1930)

John Maynard Keynes's 1930 essay **“Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren”** is the source behind the remembered claim that rising productivity would eventually leave people with very little necessary work and enormous amounts of leisure.

The remembered version — “everyone will sit around reading books” — captures part of the mood but is **not Keynes's actual claim**. He does not prescribe book-reading as the future use of leisure. His argument is broader: if technical progress and capital accumulation solve the problem of material subsistence, the central human problem changes from producing enough to deciding how to use freedom from economic necessity.

## Publication and provenance

- Author: John Maynard Keynes
- Title: **“Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren”**
- First published in two installments in *The Nation and Athenæum*, **11 and 18 October 1930**.
- Reprinted in Keynes, *Essays in Persuasion* (1931).
- Cambridge University Press's edition of Keynes's collected writings notes that the essay grew out of talks Keynes had begun giving in 1928 and later developed into a 1930 Madrid lecture.

## What Keynes actually predicts

Keynes starts from rapid increases in technical efficiency. He argues that technological unemployment can occur when labor-saving improvements arrive faster than society finds new uses for labor, but he treats this as a transitional problem rather than the permanent end state.

His long-run forecast is strikingly concrete:

- within roughly **one hundred years** — approximately 2030 from his standpoint — the standard of living in “progressive countries” could be **four to eight times** the 1930 level;
- humanity could be within sight of solving what he calls the **economic problem**, meaning the struggle to satisfy basic material needs;
- necessary work could be spread widely enough that **“three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week”** might suffice for a long period;
- the resulting problem would be how to use leisure and freedom from pressing economic cares so as to **“live wisely and agreeably and well.”**

The forecast is conditional. Keynes explicitly asks the reader to assume no major wars and no major increase in population.

## The part that is easy to misremember

Keynes is not simply forecasting that machines make everyone idle. He thinks human beings have been trained by scarcity to organize life around striving, work, saving, accumulation, and deferred gratification. If necessity recedes, people may initially be bad at leisure.

That is why the essay spends so much time on the cultural and psychological problem of abundance. Keynes treats wealthy people with independent incomes as an imperfect advance sample of the problem: possessing free time does not automatically teach someone how to use it well.

So “we will all sit around reading books” is a useful shorthand only if it is understood as:

> Productivity might make subsistence work occupy a small part of life, leaving culture, enjoyment, relationships, self-directed activity, and other non-economic purposes to fill the rest.

Book-reading is compatible with that picture, but it is not the specific activity Keynes predicts.

## Absolute and relative wants

A major qualification in the essay is Keynes's distinction between two kinds of wants:

1. **absolute wants** — needs people feel regardless of what other people possess;
2. **relative wants** — wants whose satisfaction depends on being above or ahead of others.

He thinks absolute wants can plausibly become satiated. Relative wants may not be. This matters because productivity growth by itself does not guarantee that people will choose additional leisure over additional consumption, status competition, or accumulation.

## Work, distribution, and social norms

Keynes's fifteen-hour week is not presented as an automatic engineering consequence of productivity. He imagines deliberately spreading the remaining work widely. He also anticipates changes in the social value placed on accumulation and the money motive once continued accumulation is no longer needed to escape material scarcity.

This makes the essay more interesting than the simple failed-prediction version. The question is not merely whether output per worker rose enough. It is also what institutions and social norms do with the gains: more consumption, more positional competition, more accumulated wealth, shorter hours, or some mixture.

## Why this belongs in Blackball

The essay is useful for separating several claims that are often collapsed:

- **technical capacity:** how much human labor is physically required to produce a given standard of living;
- **distribution:** who receives the gains from higher productivity;
- **labor time:** whether productivity gains are taken as higher output/income or shorter working hours;
- **status consumption:** whether rising relative wants keep material competition open-ended;
- **culture of work:** whether paid employment remains a central source of status, structure, and legitimacy even when less work is technically necessary;
- **leisure:** whether people have institutions, education, communities, arts, hobbies, and habits that make abundant free time desirable rather than frightening.

Keynes's essay is therefore not just a prediction about automation. It is a compact argument that technological abundance can move the hard problem from **production** to **distribution and the art of living**.
