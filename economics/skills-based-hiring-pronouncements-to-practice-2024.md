## Why this matters for Blackball

A job posting is not the employer's hiring rule.

This study is unusually useful because it compares **changes in what employers publicly said they required** with **changes in who those employers subsequently hired**. That gives evidence about the gap between stated requirements and revealed hiring behavior.

The important question is not merely:

> What qualifications appear in job advertisements?

It is closer to:

> When a corporation says a qualification is or is not required, does that statement actually change who gets hired?

The answer in this study is often **no**.

## Data and method

The researchers tracked more than **11,000 roles** in which an employer dropped a college-degree requirement in 2014 or later and for which enough hiring was observable both before and after the change.

They then matched those roles to observed hires using the Burning Glass Institute database of online career histories covering more than **65 million U.S. workers**.

This allowed them to compare the share of workers without bachelor's degrees hired before and after the advertised degree requirement disappeared.

## Main findings

Across the studied roles, dropping the BA requirement was associated with only about a **3.5 percentage-point increase** in the share of hires without a BA. In rough terms, removing degree requirements from 100 jobs produced fewer than four additional non-degree hires.

The report divides firms that removed degree requirements into three broad groups:

### Skills-Based Hiring Leaders

About **37%** of the firms studied produced most of the actual change. In these firms, the share of workers hired without BAs into the affected roles increased by nearly **20 percentage points**.

### In Name Only

About **45%** of firms removed degree requirements from postings but showed **no meaningful change in actual hiring behavior**.

This is the clearest result for the distinction Blackball needs to preserve:

**advertised requirement != revealed hiring requirement**

Changing the text of a posting did not necessarily change the type of person selected.

### Backsliders

Roughly **one-fifth** of firms initially hired a larger share of workers without BAs after dropping degree requirements, but the change did not persist. Over the longer run they ended up hiring a smaller share of non-degree workers.

## Other reported outcomes

Among the firms that genuinely changed their hiring behavior, workers hired without degrees into roles that previously asked for degrees had higher reported retention, and the report says those workers received substantial average pay increases relative to their prior employment.

The report estimates that, despite widespread public announcements about skills-based hiring, the resulting expansion of opportunity amounted to **less than one in 700 hires** in the year examined.

## What this study establishes

It provides direct evidence that:

1. Job-posting requirements can change without realized hiring changing.
2. Employers differ substantially in whether a public hiring-policy change affects actual selections.
3. A public statement such as "degree not required" is therefore not enough to infer the employer's effective hiring rule.
4. Job-posting databases measure an important public signal, but not the full selection process.

## What it does *not* reveal

This is still not the dataset needed to identify the complete corporate hiring rule.

The study observes postings and realized hires. It generally does **not** reveal the full applicant pool, which applicants were automatically screened out, recruiter rankings, interview scores, referrals, competing offers, salary expectations, hiring-manager preferences, or why one qualified candidate beat another.

So it cannot answer questions such as:

- How often does exceptional strength in A compensate for missing B or C?
- How many applicants possessed A+B+C and were nevertheless rejected?
- Which advertised requirements are hard gates and which are negotiable?
- How does the employer's willingness to compromise change with vacancy duration or offered compensation?
- How much do referrals, internal status, interview performance, or salary expectations substitute for formal credentials?

Those require applicant-level denominator data that corporations largely keep private.

## Blackball research use

Treat job postings as **noisy observations of stated demand**, not ground truth about hiring.

Where possible, pair postings with evidence about revealed behavior:

- actual employee backgrounds;
- hires who violate advertised requirements;
- requirement changes over time;
- reposted or long-open positions;
- internal promotions;
- salary changes;
- employer disclosures about screening systems;
- applicant or recruiter testimony;
- datasets connecting requisitions to actual hires.

The long-term target should be a **revealed corporate hiring dataset**: evidence capable of estimating what actually changes the probability that a corporation hires a particular candidate, rather than merely cataloguing what corporations say they want.
