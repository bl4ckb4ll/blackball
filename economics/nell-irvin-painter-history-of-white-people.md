# Nell Irvin Painter — *The History of White People*

Nell Irvin Painter, *The History of White People* (W. W. Norton, 2010).

## Short summary

Painter's book is better understood as a history of the **idea and social category of "white people"** than as a history of every population now classified as white. Her central argument is that a single, stable "white race" is not an ancient biological fact. The boundaries and hierarchy of whiteness were repeatedly constructed, argued over, and changed in response to politics, science, migration, class, labor, and ideals of beauty.

The book begins well before modern racial classifications, then follows European and American writers who tried to divide humanity into ranked races. Painter spends substantial time on eighteenth- and nineteenth-century racial science: the invention and prestige of labels such as "Caucasian," skull measurement, theories of Anglo-Saxon or Teutonic superiority, and later eugenics and intelligence testing. These systems presented historically contingent social rankings as though they were objective natural science.

For the United States, one important thread is the **expansion of who counted as white**. Groups such as Irish, Italian, Jewish, Slavic, Greek, and other European immigrants were at different times described by influential Americans as inferior races or as not fully belonging to the preferred Anglo-Saxon population. Over time, many of these distinctions weakened and the American category of whiteness widened.

## Why this belongs in economics

Painter is not writing an economic history, but the book is useful for economic history because racial classification repeatedly intersects with **labor and class**. Who was considered fit for enslavement, undesirable immigration, low-status manual work, full citizenship, or respectable middle-class membership was not independent of racial ideology. Painter therefore gives a history of one of the systems through which labor populations were ranked and social status was distributed.

The economic point should not be overstated: Painter does **not** argue that class or labor relations alone explain race. Her account combines intellectual history, migration, politics, science, aesthetics, and economic hierarchy. The value here is that it makes "white" itself into a historical variable rather than treating it as a timeless background category when reading wage, immigration, education, housing, or labor history.

## Scope note

This is a sweeping synthesis covering more than two millennia. Its treatment of modern U.S. racial thought and the changing boundaries of whiteness is the core reason to keep it here. Some reviewers have considered parts of the ancient and pre-modern synthesis too broad or generalized, so claims from those sections should be checked against specialist scholarship before being used as stand-alone evidence.
