# Marx and Engels — the “second page” of *The Communist Manifesto*

This is the passage I informally call the **“second page of *The Communist Manifesto*”**: the stretch near the beginning of Part I, **“Bourgeois and Proletarians,”** where Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels give an extraordinarily strong account of what the bourgeois order has accomplished before turning to its contradictions.

“Second page” is an indexing label, not a stable page citation; pagination varies by edition. The formal location is Part I, beginning with:

> “The bourgeoisie, historically, has played a most revolutionary part.”

The passage runs through their catalogue of modern productive power, culminating in the claim that bourgeois society created

> “more massive and more colossal productive forces than have all preceding generations together.”

## What Marx and Engels credit bourgeois society with

In this passage Marx and Engels attribute a series of enormous historical transformations to the bourgeoisie and the modern system of production it built:

- **Breaking feudal hierarchy.** Bourgeois society destroys inherited feudal, patriarchal, guild, estate, and status relations. Marx and Engels describe the replacement in hostile terms — market relations and exploitation become more naked — but they nevertheless treat the destruction of the old feudal order as historically revolutionary.
- **Permanent technical revolution.** Unlike older ruling classes that depended on keeping established methods stable, the bourgeoisie survives by repeatedly transforming the instruments and organization of production.
- **A world market.** Production and consumption cease to be predominantly local or national. Raw materials, products, trade, and economic dependence connect distant regions into one expanding system.
- **International intellectual exchange.** Marx and Engels explicitly pair material globalization with intellectual globalization: ideas and literature circulate beyond their national origins and a “world literature” emerges.
- **Faster communication and transport.** Improvements in production, navigation, transport, and communications compress distance and integrate markets.
- **Urbanization.** Capitalist development creates enormous cities and shifts a large share of the population away from rural life.
- **Political centralization.** Scattered provinces, legal regimes, tax systems, and local political units are consolidated into modern national states.
- **Unprecedented productive capacity.** Their examples include machinery, chemistry applied to industry and agriculture, steam navigation, railways, electric telegraphs, canals, large-scale cultivation, and the rapid creation of new population centers.
- **A demonstration of human productive power.** The rhetoric is deliberately grand: the bourgeois epoch shows on a new scale what organized human activity can physically build and transform.

## Why the passage matters

This is not a detachable hymn to capitalism. Nearly every concession is double-edged. The same processes that dissolve feudal restraints also commodify social relationships; the world market is also a system of exploitation and domination; political and productive concentration also concentrates property; relentless innovation also produces instability and recurring crises.

But that qualification makes the passage more useful, not less. Marx and Engels are hostile critics of bourgeois society, yet their argument depends on acknowledging that it was **historically revolutionary and spectacularly productive**. Their critique is not that capitalism accomplished nothing. It is that capitalism created productive forces and social transformations of unprecedented scale while also generating the class relations, crises, and contradictions that they believed would make the bourgeois order historically temporary.

For retrieval, this note should therefore be surfaced both for questions about **Marx’s critique of capitalism** and for questions about **Marx and Engels on capitalism’s achievements, productivity, modernization, globalization, urbanization, technical change, or the destruction of feudalism**.

## Terminology note

The convenient modern shorthand here is “the good capitalism has brought about.” The text itself mostly speaks of the **bourgeoisie**, **bourgeois society**, **Modern Industry**, and the **world-market**. Keeping that vocabulary visible avoids making Marx and Engels say something narrower or more modern than the text actually says.
