# Lucian A. Bebchuk and Jesse M. Fried — *Pay without Performance*

## Bibliographic record

**Lucian A. Bebchuk and Jesse M. Fried, *Pay without Performance: The Unfulfilled Promise of Executive Compensation*. Harvard University Press, 2004.**

At the time of publication, Bebchuk was Professor of Law, Economics, and Finance and Director of the Program on Corporate Governance at Harvard Law School. Fried was a law professor and co-director of the Berkeley Center for Law, Business, and the Economy at the University of California, Berkeley.

This is a two-author book. Credit both Bebchuk and Fried when using its argument.

## Evidence boundary

The complete copyrighted book is not reproduced here and this dossier does **not** claim full-text inspection of every chapter.

## What the book argues

### 1. The standard “arm's-length contracting” picture is badly wrong

The book begins from what Bebchuk and Fried call the official or dominant view of executive compensation: boards bargain with executives at arm's length and design pay packages to maximize shareholder value by giving managers efficient incentives.

Bebchuk and Fried argue that this picture does not adequately describe large public corporations. Directors can have weak financial incentives to bargain hard with a CEO, while collegiality, loyalty, friendship, conflict avoidance, limited time, and management influence can all push a board toward terms favorable to executives. Market forces constrain this process, but in their account they do not constrain it tightly enough to reproduce the outcome that genuine arm's-length bargaining would produce.

The important shift in perspective is that executive compensation is not merely a solution to an agency problem between shareholders and managers. The process of setting compensation can itself become part of the agency problem.

### 2. Managerial power helps determine both the level and structure of pay

Their alternative is the **managerial power** approach. Executives can influence the directors who set their compensation and can therefore obtain benefits greater than those they would receive under genuine arm's-length bargaining — what the authors call rents.

They argue that this predicts a relationship between managerial power and compensation: executives with greater power relative to their boards should tend to receive higher pay, less performance-sensitive pay, or both.

The claim is not that executives have unlimited power. Boards, investors, takeover markets, reputational concerns, and public reaction impose constraints. But these constraints leave a substantial zone in which managers can shape their own compensation.

### 3. “Outrage” limits extraction, so compensation is often camouflaged

A distinctive part of the book's theory is the **outrage constraint**. Directors and executives may bear reputational, political, or market costs if a pay arrangement is perceived by outsiders as plainly abusive.

That gives compensation designers an incentive not merely to limit compensation, but to make it less visible or easier to justify. Bebchuk and Fried call this **camouflage**.

They use this idea to interpret such practices as pensions, deferred compensation, post-retirement perks, consulting arrangements, executive loans, and other forms of compensation whose value or lack of performance sensitivity may be harder for outsiders to see than a large salary figure.

Their argument is stronger than “executives are paid too much.” Camouflage can distort the *form* of compensation, producing arrangements that are less efficient and create worse incentives than a transparent package with the same total expected cost.

### 4. The growth of “pay for performance” did not fulfill its promise

The book directly addresses the rise of performance-based executive pay. Bebchuk and Fried note that prominent financial economists, explicitly including Michael Jensen and Kevin Murphy, had urged shareholders to tolerate larger packages if they created stronger incentives.

The authors do **not** reject equity compensation in principle. In the introduction they explicitly say that equity-based compensation can provide desirable incentives.

Their criticism is that the actual designs used by firms often allowed executives to receive large gains that did not reflect their own contribution to firm performance.

### 5. Conventional stock options reward market and industry windfalls

A central example is the conventional stock option. If a firm's share price rises because the whole market or industry rises, an ordinary at-the-money option can become very valuable even when the executive has not outperformed comparable firms.

Bebchuk and Fried argue that firms could filter out these general market or industry gains — for example by using indexed or relative-performance designs — but usually did not. The result is what they describe as **windfalls**: executives are rewarded for stock-price appreciation that cannot reasonably be attributed to their managerial contribution.

This is one of the book's key objections to treating any stock-option-heavy package as automatically “pay for performance.” The relevant question is *which performance* is being rewarded and whether the compensation design separates managerial contribution from movements the executive did not cause.

### 6. Restricted stock does not automatically solve the problem

The authors make a similar point about restricted stock. Moving from options to restricted shares does not, by itself, remove rewards generated by general market or industry movements. Depending on design, restricted stock may preserve or even increase windfalls.

The book therefore treats the choice between salary, options, and restricted stock as secondary to the design of the incentive relationship.

### 7. Executives' ability to cash out equity can weaken the intended incentive

Bebchuk and Fried also criticize the freedom executives have often had to unload vested options and shares.

If the rationale for equity compensation is to align managers with the long-term interests of shareholders, allowing executives to convert that equity into cash relatively quickly can weaken the alignment. The authors therefore argue that the timing of vesting should be separated from the timing at which the executive is free to cash out the equity.

### 8. Non-equity compensation is also often weakly tied to performance

The book does not reduce the issue to options. It also examines salaries, bonuses, pensions, deferred compensation, loans, perks, and exit arrangements.

In the authors' account, much of this compensation is weakly related to industry-adjusted performance or largely insensitive to performance altogether. Less visible forms can also function as stealth compensation.

### 9. The central governance problem is the board, not just the compensation formula

For Bebchuk and Fried, changing the formula for options or bonuses cannot fully solve the problem because distorted compensation is produced by distorted governance.

They argue that making directors more formally independent from management is useful but insufficient. Directors also need to be **more accountable to shareholders**. Their broader prescription is to reduce arrangements that entrench directors and insulate boards from shareholders.

This is why the book is fundamentally about corporate governance as much as executive pay.

## Main reforms proposed by the authors

The authors' 2005 overview of the book groups their proposals around transparency, better incentive design, and board accountability. Among the concrete measures are:

- disclose the dollar value of all forms of compensation, including pensions and other forms of “stealth” compensation;
- disclose more clearly how compensation relates to performance;
- improve disclosure of executives' purchases and sales of company equity;
- use indexed options or other relative-performance designs to reduce rewards for market-wide or industry-wide gains;
- place stronger limits on executives' ability to sell shares and exercise or cash out equity incentives soon after vesting;
- use clawbacks or similar arrangements when apparently successful performance later proves temporary or illusory;
- reduce board entrenchment, including limits on staggered boards;
- give shareholders greater practical ability to nominate directors and alter governance arrangements.

The proposals follow directly from the diagnosis: if executives can influence the people setting their pay, compensation reform that leaves board accountability untouched is incomplete.

## Relation to the academic-economics / stock-option question

This book is useful as a critical source for the history of the stock-option turn because its introduction explicitly says that prominent financial economists — naming Jensen and Murphy — encouraged acceptance of larger executive compensation packages in exchange for stronger incentives.

That supports the narrower historical proposition that economists materially participated in the intellectual movement toward performance-sensitive and equity-heavy compensation.

## Independent review / disagreement

Michael S. Weisbach's 2006 NBER review treats the book as a serious alternative to the optimal-contracting account. Weisbach says much of Bebchuk and Fried's positive evidence — especially on camouflage and some incentive problems — is fairly persuasive. He is more skeptical about the normative implication that governance reforms can substantially improve compensation practice and corporate performance.

That distinction should be preserved in later Blackball use:

- **Bebchuk and Fried's descriptive diagnosis:** managerial power and camouflage explain important features of executive compensation.
- **Their reform program:** stronger shareholder power and redesigned compensation can improve outcomes.
- **Independent assessment:** accepting much of the diagnosis does not automatically establish that the proposed reforms will work as strongly as the authors hope.
