# Adam Smith — *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759)

Adam Smith's first major book is a theory of how human beings come to make moral judgments while living among other people. It is not a preliminary version of *The Wealth of Nations*, and it is not mainly a defense of altruism. Its central problem is more basic: **how can a partial creature, who experiences the world from inside one body and one set of interests, learn to judge itself and other people from a standpoint that is not merely its own?**

The book first appeared in 1759 while Smith was Professor of Moral Philosophy at the University of Glasgow. Smith revised it repeatedly; the sixth edition of 1790, published shortly before his death, is substantially enlarged and is the standard mature text. This note summarizes that mature work while keeping the 1759 publication date explicit.

## One-paragraph summary

People do not directly feel another person's passions, but they can imaginatively place themselves in the other's situation. Smith calls the fellow-feeling produced by that imaginative movement **sympathy**. Social life becomes a continuing attempt to bring the passions of the person directly affected and the responses of spectators into some degree of correspondence. From repeated acts of judging and being judged we learn to imagine a better-informed, less interested spectator — the **impartial spectator** — and eventually to scrutinize our own conduct from that standpoint. Moral judgment therefore grows out of ordinary social interaction but is not reducible to whatever an actual crowd happens to approve. Justice, resentment, conscience, general rules, prudence, beneficence, and self-command all emerge inside this account. Smith also identifies a systematic failure: people readily confuse wealth, rank, visibility, and power with genuine merit, so admiration of the rich and great can corrupt the very moral sentiments on which social judgment depends.

## Argument map

### 1. Sympathy is imaginative fellow-feeling, not simple kindness

For Smith, "sympathy" is broader than modern sympathy or compassion. We cannot literally enter another person's consciousness. We instead imagine the person's circumstances and ask what we would feel if situated there. The result can be fellow-feeling with grief, joy, anger, resentment, gratitude, ambition, fear, or other passions.

This is why Smith's moral psychology is not based on an assumption that people are naturally self-sacrificing. A self-interested person still depends on other people's reactions and still possesses the capacity to imagine their situations.

### 2. Propriety arises from whether a spectator can "go along with" a passion

The person directly affected normally feels an emotion more intensely than an outside spectator can reproduce it. Social concord therefore requires movement on both sides: the spectator makes an effort to enter the person's circumstances, while the person principally concerned moderates the expression of passion toward a level with which spectators can sympathize.

Smith calls conduct or feeling **proper** when an appropriately situated spectator can go along with it. This creates two paired families of virtue: humanity and compassion on the spectator's side, and restraint or self-command on the side of the person whose passions are strongest.

### 3. The impartial spectator is an internalized critical standpoint

Actual spectators are ignorant, partisan, fashionable, intimidated by rank, or personally interested. Smith therefore does not simply equate morality with popularity. Through experience we learn to ask how a **well-informed and impartial spectator** would judge the case.

This imagined standpoint becomes the internal judge sometimes described by Smith as the person within the breast. Conscience is therefore social in its development without being identical to immediate social applause.

### 4. Merit, demerit, gratitude, and resentment connect morality to justice

Smith distinguishes judging whether a passion or action is fitting from judging whether an agent deserves reward or punishment. Gratitude toward beneficent action and resentment toward injury are central here.

Justice receives a special place because injury calls forth resentment in the victim and sympathetic resentment in spectators. Beneficence is admirable, but in Smith's scheme it generally cannot be compelled in the same way that compliance with justice can. A society may survive without extraordinary generosity; it cannot survive if members may injure one another without effective restraint.

### 5. General rules and conscience discipline self-serving judgment

Repeated judgments generate general moral rules. These rules matter because a person evaluating his own case is exceptionally prone to partiality. Habit, conscience, and the imagined impartial spectator help correct the temptation to make one's own interests an exception.

Smith's morality is therefore neither a list of commandments deduced from first principles nor a theory in which every decision is calculated from aggregate utility. Moral rules emerge from judgment, experience, and social correction, yet they can in turn constrain immediate preference.

### 6. Virtue is larger than mere rule-following

The mature sixth edition gives a new Part VI to the character of virtue. Smith discusses **prudence**, **justice**, **beneficence**, and **self-command**. The good person is not merely someone who avoids prohibited acts. Character includes managing one's own affairs intelligently, respecting the claims of others, acting positively for others where appropriate, and governing passions that would distort judgment or conduct.

### 7. Utility matters, but it is not the sole source of approval

Smith gives utility an important but limited role. We often admire the fitness, order, and useful consequences of institutions and arrangements. But he argues that moral approval cannot be reduced to a detached calculation of usefulness. We ordinarily judge intentions, passions, propriety, gratitude, resentment, and character as well as consequences.

### 8. Custom and fashion influence moral judgment

Social norms do not operate in a vacuum. Custom and fashion affect what people find admirable or shameful. Smith therefore builds social distortion into the theory itself rather than assuming that a society's actual judgments automatically converge on the impartial standpoint.

### 9. Wealth and rank can corrupt moral judgment

This is one of the most important passages for Blackball. Smith argues that people have a powerful tendency to admire wealth and social greatness and to neglect poverty and weakness. The danger is not simply envy. **Visible success can acquire the respect that should attach to wisdom and virtue.** Conversely, poverty can attract contempt that morally belongs only to vice or folly.

Smith treats this status bias as deep and socially consequential. It helps maintain distinctions of rank, yet it also produces what he calls a major corruption of moral sentiments. The chapter is a direct warning against using prestige, wealth, office, or public attention as easy proxies for merit.

For a repository concerned with credentials, status, institutional prestige, and authority, this is not a modern critique being projected backward onto Smith. It is an explicit problem inside Smith's own moral psychology.

## What the book does **not** say

- **"Sympathy" does not mean universal benevolence.** It is the imaginative mechanism by which we enter another person's situation, and it can operate with many passions.
- **Smith does not say that whatever society approves is right.** Actual spectators may be badly informed or partial; the impartial spectator is a corrective ideal.
- **The book is not a utilitarian maximizing algorithm.** Smith discusses utility but grounds moral judgment in a richer structure of sentiments, intentions, propriety, merit, resentment, rules, and character.
- **It is not simply the "nice" book contradicted by the "selfish" *Wealth of Nations*.** Both works assume socially embedded people with mixed motives. Commercial exchange can often proceed through self-interest while moral judgment, trust, justice, reputation, vanity, sympathy, and the pursuit of approval continue operating at the same time.
- **The 1759 text should not be silently treated as identical to the 1790 text.** Smith revised the book through six editions; Part VI of the sixth edition was new.

## Relation to *The Wealth of Nations*

*The Theory of Moral Sentiments* supplies much of the social and moral psychology within which Smith's later political economy operates. *The Wealth of Nations* asks, among other things, how specialization, exchange, prices, institutions, law, state policy, and self-interest organize commercial society. TMS asks how creatures seeking approval and judging one another develop standards of conduct and how those standards can be distorted.

There is therefore no need to invent an "Adam Smith problem" in which one book assumes altruism and the other assumes selfishness. A merchant can bargain from self-interest, care intensely about reputation and social standing, resent injustice, seek approval, admire wealth, sympathize selectively, and still consult moral rules. Smith's two books emphasize different parts of the same complicated social animal.

## Why this is filed under economics

Smith was not an economics professor. He was Professor of Moral Philosophy, and the boundaries among ethics, jurisprudence, political economy, rhetoric, history, and philosophy were not the modern departmental boundaries. Glasgow describes TMS as the wider social and moral context on which *The Wealth of Nations* rests.

That institutional fact matters. Reading only the later economics canon can make Smith look like the founder of a discipline already recognizable as modern economics. In 1759 he was instead a moral-philosophy professor writing a book about spectators, passions, virtue, justice, status, and social judgment.

## Edition and access note

- First edition: **1759**, London, A. Millar.
- Smith issued multiple revised editions during his lifetime.
- Sixth edition: **1790**, substantially revised; Smith's advertisement says Part VI was entirely new in that edition.
- Econlib hosts a public-domain transcription of the 1790 sixth edition.

When a claim depends on wording or structure added late in Smith's life, it should be cited to the sixth edition rather than lazily attributed to the 1759 first edition.
