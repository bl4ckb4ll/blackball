# Public school as labor-market infrastructure

Public schooling should not be analyzed only as instruction delivered to children. During the school day it also supplies supervision/care that changes what parents are able to sell to employers: their time and availability.

That does **not** mean schooling is merely childcare, nor that employers are the only beneficiaries. Children, parents, communities, governments, and employers can all receive different benefits from the same institution. The point is to keep the employer-side benefit visible.

## Employer-side mechanism

A simple mechanism is:

1. a child requires supervision;
2. a parent or another caregiver must otherwise provide or purchase it;
3. in-person school absorbs a substantial block of that supervision during ordinary working hours;
4. the parent can therefore spend more hours at paid work or accept jobs whose schedules would otherwise be impossible;
5. employers gain access to labor that is more available because part of the worker's caregiving constraint has been socialized.

This is an indirect subsidy/infrastructure claim, not a claim that an individual employer receives a check from the school district.

It also changes bargaining conditions. A parent who needs wages and must simultaneously arrange care may have less practical freedom to refuse schedules, overtime, commutes, or attendance requirements than a worker without the same caregiving constraint. That should be tested rather than assumed case by case.

## Causal evidence: what happened when school disappeared?

COVID school closures give unusually useful evidence because they abruptly removed in-person schooling while leaving many parents attached to jobs.

Garcia and Cowan, using U.S. school-closure data merged with the Current Population Survey, report reductions in parents' work hours and full-time work, with effects concentrated among parents without college degrees and workers in jobs poorly suited to telework. They also report reduced earnings among parents of school-aged children.

- NBER Working Paper 29641: https://www.nber.org/papers/w29641
- NBER digest: https://www.nber.org/digest/202204/pandemic-school-closures-and-parents-labor-supply

Hansen, Sabia, and Schaller analyze reopenings and find that the return of in-person K-12 schooling increased employment and hours for married women with school-aged children in their sample. The published version appears in the *Journal of Human Resources*.

- NBER Working Paper 29660: https://www.nber.org/papers/w29660

A 2026 NBER paper using Canadian panel data likewise estimates employment losses among partnered mothers when in-person schooling was restricted.

- NBER Working Paper 35432: https://www.nber.org/papers/w35432

The Federal Reserve has also used the summer break as a recurring natural contrast: when schools close, working parents must replace roughly the weekday hours in which children were previously in school, and women's employment falls closely with the timing of school summer closures.

- Federal Reserve note: https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/gender-gaps-in-the-labor-market-widen-every-summer-20230324.html

## Why this matters for the question "what is a teacher?"

A teacher can simultaneously be:

- someone who teaches a child;
- a paid worker selling skilled labor;
- part of a public institution;
- part of the care/supervision infrastructure that makes other paid labor possible;
- a worker whose own employment conditions become a political question through unionization, staffing, class size, hours, and public budgets.

Those roles should not be collapsed.

This makes teacher-union arguments easier to state honestly. An employer, parent, taxpayer, student, teacher, and administrator can value the school for different reasons. A dispute about teacher pay or strikes is therefore also partly a dispute over who bears the cost of maintaining the social infrastructure that keeps millions of parents available for paid work.

## Questions to track

- How many hours of parental paid work are enabled by school attendance?
- Who loses hours or earnings when schools close unexpectedly?
- How do effects differ by income, occupation, gender, marital status, telework access, and availability of relatives/private childcare?
- Do employers change attendance/scheduling expectations when schools close?
- How much private childcare would be required to reproduce the supervision hours supplied by public schools?
- Do employer groups lobby on school calendars, reopening, after-school care, or childcare policy, and what reasons do they give?
- How should the value of supervision/care be separated from the value of instruction?

Do not replace these questions with an average-parent story when causal variation is available. The closure/reopening evidence is much stronger than guessing from the average age or income of parents.
