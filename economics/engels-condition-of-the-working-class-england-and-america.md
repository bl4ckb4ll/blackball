# Friedrich Engels — *The Condition of the Working Class in England* and the American working-class question

## Scope correction

The book is **Friedrich Engels, *The Condition of the Working Class in England***, written in 1844–1845 and first published in German in 1845. It is not a book called *The Condition of the Working Class in North America*.

There is, however, a real American connection worth keeping rather than treating “North America” as simply a mistaken title. For the American edition Engels wrote a new 1887 preface, **“The Labor Movement in America,”** and an appendix discussing the emergence of a permanent American wage-working class. Those texts make a useful bridge from his Manchester investigation to the United States.

“America” in Engels's 1880s discussion means overwhelmingly the **United States**, not a systematic treatment of Canada, Mexico, or North America as a whole.

That is a useful instruction for reading it: neither dismiss it as youthful propaganda nor treat every empirical generalization and prediction as settled fact.

## What the book is doing

At the most basic level, Engels is asking a question that later economics often divides into separate boxes:

> What does an economic system do to the total life of the people who have to sell their labor in order to live?

His unit of analysis is not merely the wage. He follows industrial capitalism outward from the factory into housing, food, sanitation, disease, mortality, family life, education, alcohol, crime, migration, political organization, urban geography, and the worker's degree of security about tomorrow.

That is the enduring methodological strength of the book. Engels refuses to let a labor contract be evaluated only at the instant when employer and worker formally agree to exchange work for money. He asks what alternatives each party actually has, how the labor market was historically created, what unemployment does to bargaining power, what happens to a worker after the shift ends, and what social costs are displaced outside the firm's accounts.

The book is therefore simultaneously:

- an eyewitness description of early industrial England;
- a synthesis of government inquiries, medical reports, newspapers, and other contemporary investigators;
- a theory of how a proletarian class is produced;
- a moral indictment of bourgeois society;
- and a political argument that workers will organize as a class.

Those layers should not be confused. A quotation from a parliamentary sanitary report is a different kind of claim from Engels predicting revolutionary political development.

## 1. Industrialization creates a proletariat, not just more factories

Engels begins with the industrial revolution: machinery, factories, division of labor, enlarged markets, and the concentration of production. His main claim is not merely that machines replace particular jobs. The deeper transformation is that older forms of partial independence are destroyed or subordinated and an increasingly large population comes to possess little that can sustain it independently of wages.

The important category is therefore **wage dependence**.

A worker may be legally free and may change employers, but if continued access to food, rent, fuel, and clothing depends on finding someone who will purchase his or her labor, then the labor market has coercive force even without a master legally owning the worker.

This is one reason the book remains more interesting than a catalogue of nineteenth-century misery. Its central object is a social relation: one class owns or controls productive property and another must repeatedly enter the labor market to gain access to the means of living.

---

## 2. Competition among workers is a source of employer power

The chapter titled **“Competition”** is one of the clearest economic sections.

Engels argues that workers do not confront capital as a naturally unified class. They also compete with one another for jobs. Unemployment, technological displacement, migration, differences in skill, and the ability of employers to substitute one worker for another all put downward pressure on the worker's bargaining position.

He consequently treats unemployment not as an accidental group standing outside the economy but as part of the mechanism by which the labor market operates. In prosperous periods firms draw more people into employment; in contraction workers are expelled; the existence of people seeking work disciplines those who remain employed.

This is close to what Marx would later develop as the **reserve army of labor**. Engels already describes a recurring surplus population that expands in crises and contracts in booms.

His account also links working hours to unemployment. If nine people can be made to perform the work formerly done by ten, the tenth becomes superfluous. Longer hours are therefore not only a question of how much an already-employed person suffers; they affect the number of people who can obtain employment.

The political corollary is important: trade unions matter because they are an attempt to suppress or limit competition among workers. Collective bargaining changes the situation from individual workers bidding against one another toward workers negotiating as an organized body.

---

## 3. The boom-and-bust cycle is experienced as a household catastrophe

Engels describes industrial crises as recurring features of commercial society. Demand expands, production rises, markets become glutted, firms cut production or fail, workers lose hours or jobs, wages fall, savings disappear, charitable institutions are overwhelmed, and poverty spreads.

Whether every detail of his crisis theory is accepted is less important than his way of measuring the event. A commercial crisis is not only a graph of output. It is also:

- a rent problem;
- a food problem;
- a health problem;
- a bargaining-power problem;
- a family problem;
- and a political event.

This is a recurring theme throughout *Condition*: macroeconomic quantities eventually become bodily facts.

---

## 4. The city is part of the economic system

The chapters on London, Manchester, Liverpool, Leeds, and other industrial towns are among the most famous parts of the book.

Engels treats urban geography as class geography. Wealth and poverty can exist extremely close together while being arranged so that the wealthy move through the city without seeing much of the worst working-class housing. Back-to-back houses, courts, cellar dwellings, crowding, poor ventilation, filth, drainage failures, polluted air, and contaminated water are not scenery around industrialization. They are part of the cost structure of industrial society as workers actually experience it.

The analytical point is broader than “slums are bad.” A firm can pay a wage and leave the reproduction of the worker — housing, food, sanitation, child care, health, transport — to the household and city. If those systems fail, the resulting disease and early death may never appear as a cost on the firm's balance sheet.

This is why Engels's discussion belongs in economics as much as urban history.

---

## 5. “Social murder”: preventable death as a social outcome

In the chapter **“Results,”** Engels makes his most severe accusation. When institutions knowingly place large numbers of people in conditions that predictably shorten their lives, he argues that the resulting deaths should not be treated as if they were merely natural events. He calls this **“social murder.”**

The argument depends heavily on sanitary and medical evidence: overcrowding, bad ventilation, inadequate drainage, poor nutrition, infectious disease, dangerous work, exhaustion, and sharply unequal mortality.

Whatever one thinks of the word “murder,” the conceptual move is important. Engels asks us to distinguish:

1. the immediate medical cause of death; and
2. the social arrangements that made exposure to that cause much more likely.

That distinction remains central to work on occupational health, environmental inequality, public health, and the social determinants of health.

One should nevertheless be cautious with individual numerical claims in the 1845 book. Engels often reports contemporary official or medical statistics, but nineteenth-century registration, categories, samples, and causal inference do not have modern consistency. The safest use is to preserve the original source and date rather than silently converting every reported number into a timeless fact.

---

## 6. He tries to explain behavior materially rather than morally

Engels repeatedly attacks explanations that begin and end by calling poor people improvident, drunken, criminal, dirty, ignorant, or immoral.

His response is not that destructive behavior never occurs. It is that an explanation should ask what conditions systematically produce it.

If a person works exhausting hours, lives in an overcrowded and unpleasant dwelling, has little security, few forms of recreation, poor schooling, and almost no prospect of accumulating property, then alcohol and immediate consumption cannot be analyzed adequately as isolated failures of character. Likewise, theft during starvation, family conflict under extreme crowding, or children's neglect when parents work punishing schedules are connected to material conditions.

This is one of the most modern parts of the book: an insistence on mechanisms rather than moral labels.

It can also become too deterministic. At times Engels writes as if human behavior follows mechanically from conditions. That can flatten differences among workers and underestimate agency, culture, institutions, and contingency. Still, the demand to explain rather than merely condemn is a genuine contribution.

---

## 7. Women, children, and the household

Engels gives extensive attention to the employment of women and children and to the way factory labor rearranges household life. He records long hours, injury, fatigue, child labor, disrupted child care, pregnancy and factory work, and the effects of having multiple household members drawn into wage labor.

But the framework has limitations. Engels often evaluates family changes against nineteenth-century assumptions about husbands, wives, mothers, and domestic roles. He sees that the household is economically necessary but does not yet provide a full analysis of unpaid reproductive labor as labor.

A modern reading should therefore retain his key insight — the labor market reaches inside the household — while not inheriting all of his gender assumptions.

---

## 8. Irish immigration: analytically important and badly contaminated by prejudice

Engels sees Irish migration as economically central. Poor Irish workers supply labor to rapidly expanding English industry and, because they are desperate and excluded, can be employed at very low wages. He therefore treats migration, labor-market segmentation, and competition among national groups as part of class formation.

That analytical question is important.

The chapter itself also contains crude ethnic stereotypes and degrading claims about Irish people. Those passages should not be cleaned up until they sound modern. They are evidence of Engels's own prejudices and of a serious weakness in his analysis: conditions generated by colonial domination and poverty are repeatedly redescribed as characteristics of the people subjected to them.

The useful question is not “were the Irish culturally inferior?” It is how employers and labor markets use groups with different legal status, resources, networks, languages, and fallback options, and how those divisions can weaken solidarity among workers.

That distinction becomes essential when applying Engels to the United States.

---

## 9. Factory hands, miners, trades, and agricultural laborers

Engels does not treat “the working class” as identical to one Manchester cotton mill. He moves through multiple occupations and forms of labor:

- textile factories;
- hand trades disrupted by mechanization;
- clothing and domestic/outwork arrangements;
- metal and other manufacturing;
- mining;
- agricultural labor.

The details differ, but he is looking for recurring mechanisms: dependence on wages, employer control, injury and health hazards, technological displacement, long hours, weak bargaining power, cyclical unemployment, and the ability of capital to move or reorganize production.

That occupational breadth is useful for modern reading because “working class” should not mean only one visual stereotype of a male factory operative.

---

## 10. Labor organization is not an afterthought

The chapter on labor movements is the political answer to the chapter on competition.

If employer power rests partly on workers competing individually, then unions, strikes, combinations, and political organization are attempts to turn isolated sellers of labor into a collective actor. Engels therefore values organization even when a particular strike fails to win its immediate demand. Organization itself changes consciousness, communication, and the capacity for future collective action.

He discusses Chartism as a major expression of an independent working-class political movement. His larger prediction is that common economic circumstances will increasingly produce class consciousness and ultimately political confrontation with the owners of capital.

The history after 1845 is much messier than this developmental sequence suggests. Working classes have organized along trade, religion, race, nation, region, party, occupation, and many other lines; rising wages or political reforms can stabilize capitalist institutions; labor parties can moderate; unions can exclude other workers; workers can support parties not organized around class.

So class formation should be treated as a problem to explain, not an automatic consequence of wage labor.

# Engels on the United States

## The American “exception” as a historical condition, not a permanent law

The most directly relevant material for the user's “North America” recollection is Engels's **1887 preface to the American edition**.

Engels begins from the then-common claim that the United States had no working class in the European sense and therefore no real European-style class struggle. He argues that events of the mid-1880s — especially coal-mining strikes, the eight-hour movement, labor upheaval in Chicago and Milwaukee, and electoral labor organization — showed that this belief was becoming untenable.

His explanation for why the United States had long looked different is especially interesting. In the appendix associated with the American edition he names two historical “safety valves”:

1. access by many white Americans to relatively cheap land; and
2. large-scale immigration, which supplied much of the population doing hard wage labor.

In his model, a worker who could leave wage employment and become a farmer, trader, or small employer was less likely to become a lifelong proletarian. By the 1880s, Engels thought this route of exit was disappearing as land passed into private ownership and industrial production expanded. The United States was therefore producing a permanent, increasingly hereditary class of wage workers.

That is probably the closest thing Engels wrote to a *Condition of the Working Class in America* thesis.

### A necessary correction to Engels's American land story

The “cheap land” story cannot now be repeated innocently as if the continent had been an empty economic resource. U.S. settler expansion depended on conquest, treaty violations, removal, and dispossession of Indigenous peoples. Nor can American class formation be understood while treating slavery as merely a stage that ended and then left the model.

The racial organization of slavery, Reconstruction, landholding, citizenship, migration, labor markets, union inclusion/exclusion, housing, and state violence shaped the American working class itself.

Engels notices the abolition of slavery and immigrant labor but does not build race and settler colonialism deeply enough into his theory of U.S. class formation.

---

## Eight hours, Chicago, and the sudden visibility of class conflict

Engels was struck by the speed of the American labor upsurge. He describes the eight-hour movement and the 1886 conflicts as evidence that workers dispersed over a huge country were beginning to recognize common grievances and common interests.

The key thing for Engels is **class self-recognition**: workers cease to understand an injury as merely “my employer pays me badly” or “my trade is in trouble” and begin to understand many local conflicts as expressions of a shared relation between wage workers and capital owners.

That transformation is not guaranteed. It is a political achievement.

---

## Henry George

Engels devotes substantial attention to **Henry George**, whose land-centered program had strong support in New York labor politics.

Engels agrees that land monopoly matters but argues that George's diagnosis is too narrow. For Engels, the decisive problem is dispossession from the **means of production generally**, not land alone. Mines, railways, factories, machinery, and other productive assets belong in the same analysis.

This is an instructive dispute because it separates two radical diagnoses of inequality:

- inequality as fundamentally a land-rent / land-monopoly problem;
- inequality as a broader ownership-of-production problem.

The distinction remains useful in current debates about housing and land values versus capital ownership and labor power.

---

## Knights of Labor

Engels treats the **Knights of Labor** as the most important raw material of a genuinely national American working-class movement. He is impressed by its enormous geographic spread and its attempt to organize far beyond a narrow craft.

His prediction was wrong in an important respect: the Knights declined rapidly after their 1880s high point and did not become the durable national form of American labor politics he anticipated.

That failure is analytically valuable. It demonstrates why one should not make the existence of common material interests do explanatory work that must actually be done by organization, institutions, race, ethnicity, law, employer resistance, political parties, repression, internal strategy, and historical contingency.

---

## Socialist Labor Party and immigrant socialism

Engels also discusses the **Socialist Labor Party**, noting both its theoretical sophistication and its heavily German-immigrant character. His advice is revealing: immigrant socialists cannot expect American workers simply to come to them; they have to participate in the actually existing movement and become intelligible within American political life.

This is consistent with the passage from the *Communist Manifesto* that he invokes: communists should not act as a sect with interests separate from other working-class movements but should participate in immediate struggles while maintaining a broader analysis.

Whatever one thinks of the politics, it is a more flexible strategy than the caricature in which Marxism consists only of announcing a finished doctrine to workers.

# What transfers to contemporary North America?

Not the literal Manchester conditions. The useful transfer is a set of questions.

## 1. How dependent is the worker on the next paycheck?

A high nominal wage does not by itself answer this. Relevant variables include savings, debt, health insurance, rent or mortgage, child care, transportation, immigration status, scheduling, access to unemployment insurance, and whether losing one job threatens several other parts of life simultaneously.

## 2. How much real power does “exit” give the worker?

Formal freedom to quit matters, but quitting has different meanings for a person with six months of savings and portable health insurance than for a person facing eviction, medication costs, an employer-linked visa, or a schedule that makes job searching difficult.

Engels's question is therefore not “is the worker legally free?” but “what are the worker's feasible alternatives?”

## 3. What role does unemployment and underemployment play in discipline?

Modern employers do not need a nineteenth-century factory gate to benefit from a queue of applicants. Temporary staffing, subcontracting, variable scheduling, layoffs, gig work, and automated hiring systems can all affect the worker's sense of replaceability.

This does **not** mean every unemployed person is intentionally maintained as a disciplinary device. It means the state of the labor market changes bargaining power even when no individual employer controls the whole market.

## 4. Which costs are outside the wage calculation?

Housing near work, commuting time, vehicle costs, occupational injury, pollution, irregular sleep, child care, unpaid preparation, medical costs, and time lost to unpredictable schedules may all matter to the actual price at which labor is reproduced.

Engels's urban chapters are a reminder that the economic life of work extends beyond the workplace boundary.

## 5. How are workers divided?

A modern North American analysis has to include race, citizenship, language, gender, incarceration history, immigration status, credential requirements, geography, and occupational licensing alongside skill and occupation.

The point is not to reduce all these distinctions to class. It is to ask when institutions turn differences among workers into different fallback options and therefore different bargaining positions.

## 6. What institutions counteract the market relation?

Compared with 1840s Manchester, modern North America has labor law, occupational-safety regulation, public schools, sanitation systems, social insurance, anti-discrimination law, minimum wages, unemployment insurance, pensions, health programs, unions, and many other institutions Engels did not face in the same form.

These are not footnotes. They materially alter what wage dependence means.

Some arose from working-class political struggle; some from broader reform coalitions; some also support the stability and productivity of capitalism. A serious use of Engels has to analyze them rather than assuming nineteenth-century conditions simply persist underneath unchanged.

## 7. Where is the modern industrial city?

A contemporary analogue to Manchester may be less visually concentrated. Production can be distributed across international supply chains while logistics concentrates workers in warehouses, ports, trucking corridors, fulfillment centers, meatpacking plants, hospitals, call centers, kitchens, retail chains, and data-mediated service work.

The worker may never see the owner of the relevant capital. Corporate ownership, contractors, franchises, platforms, staffing companies, and property owners can split what looked in Engels's world like one relationship into several legal relationships.

That makes his basic question harder, not obsolete: **who has effective control over the conditions under which the worker can earn a living?**

# What in Engels should not simply be carried forward

1. **Revolution is not an automatic output of industrialization.** The subsequent history of Britain and the United States alone is enough to reject any mechanically inevitable sequence from wage labor to unified revolutionary politics.
2. **The working class is not homogeneous.** Workers have conflicting interests as well as common ones, and institutions can organize those divisions for long periods.
3. **Race and settler colonialism are under-theorized in his American discussion.** They are constitutive parts of U.S. economic history, not secondary complications.
4. **The Irish chapter contains explicit ethnic prejudice.** Its labor-market question can be retained only if its stereotypes are rejected rather than laundered.
5. **Gender and unpaid household labor need a fuller treatment.** Engels sees the household being reorganized by industry but does not provide an adequate modern account of care and reproductive labor.
6. **Historical statistics require source criticism.** His use of official inquiries was unusually empirical for a polemical work, but nineteenth-century measurements should be preserved with their provenance and definitions.
7. **Institutions change the system.** Sanitation, labor standards, unions, mass democracy, welfare programs, education, regulation, public health, and technological abundance make a literal equation between 1844 Manchester and present North America false.

# Condensed Blackball reading

The strongest way to read *The Condition of the Working Class in England* is **not** “Engels proved that capitalism always makes every worker poorer.” That is too crude and historically false.

A stronger formulation is:

> Industrial capitalism should be evaluated not only by aggregate production or the formal freedom of labor contracts, but by the bargaining position, security, health, housing, time, family life, and political capacity of the people who must sell labor to live.

Engels's particular achievement is to show that those things form a system. Factory organization changes the labor market; the labor market changes wages and hours; wages and hours change housing and household organization; housing and work alter disease and mortality; insecurity changes behavior; competition among workers weakens bargaining power; organization tries to reverse that competition; and political institutions determine which of these costs remain private misfortunes and which become public questions.

The 1887 American material adds a second insight: a country can look exempt from European class relations for historically specific reasons — abundant exit opportunities for some workers, immigration, territorial expansion, different political institutions — without being permanently exempt. Engels thought the closing of the land “safety valve” and the rise of mass industry were creating a permanent American wage-working class.

He was right that the United States developed a huge durable wage-working population. He was wrong that this development would naturally produce the unified national labor politics he expected. That gap between the economic formation of a class and its political organization is one of the most interesting things to take from the American edition today.
