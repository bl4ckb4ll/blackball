## Why this matters for Blackball

This report directly supports a worker-side distinction that job-search advice routinely blurs:

**being able to do the job != surviving the employer's hiring process.**

The report studies workers who are effectively hidden from employers by the hiring systems employers themselves use. Its examples include caregivers, veterans, people with disabilities, people with employment gaps, formerly incarcerated workers, and others whose resumes do not fit standard filters.

The important evidence is not merely that applicants complain about automated screening. Employers themselves report that their hiring systems eliminate people they believe could successfully perform the work.

## Key finding

Among surveyed employers using recruiting-management systems:

- **94%** said their processes sometimes, often, or always filtered out qualified **middle-skill** candidates because those candidates did not fit the exact criteria in the job description.
- **88%** said the same for qualified **high-skill** candidates.

The report's Figure 10 asks employers whether their hiring processes filter out potential candidates who could successfully perform the job but do not fit exact job-description criteria, such as specified professional experience.

This is unusually important evidence because the admission comes from the demand side itself: corporations acknowledge that their selection mechanisms reject some people who could do the work.

## Hiring-system mechanisms discussed

The report describes employers' increasing reliance on recruiting and applicant-management systems to filter large applicant pools. Filters may operationalize requirements such as:

- exact years of experience;
- a college degree;
- particular credentials or skills;
- uninterrupted employment histories;
- narrowly matching job titles or resume language.

Those filters can turn a preference or proxy into an effective hard gate before a human evaluates the applicant.

## Worker-side interpretation

For a worker, rejection therefore does not establish:

> I could not have done this job.

It may instead establish only:

> My application did not satisfy the corporation's current screening mechanism.

That distinction matters because the remedies differ. If a person genuinely lacks a necessary skill, training may be useful. If a person is being eliminated because a brittle filter requires an exact credential, job title, date pattern, or phrase, buying additional education may be expensive and irrelevant to the real problem.

## What this study can and cannot tell us

It establishes that employers themselves believe qualified candidates are frequently filtered out by exact-match hiring processes.

It does **not** give us the applicant-level data needed to calculate a person's probability of being hired by a particular corporation. We still generally lack:

- the full applicant pool;
- who failed each filter;
- recruiter rankings;
- interview scores;
- referrals and internal-candidate status;
- salary expectations;
- offers and negotiations;
- the reason one surviving candidate beat another.

So the study documents a major failure mode without fully identifying the hidden corporate hiring rule.

## Blackball metrics suggested by this study

For worker-facing analysis, convert the report's criticism into measurable quantities wherever data permits:

1. **filter exclusion rate** — share of apparently capable applicants removed before substantive review;
2. **requirement brittleness** — how often one missing listed attribute causes rejection despite strength elsewhere;
3. **employment-gap penalty** — change in advancement probability associated with a gap;
4. **degree-gate effect** — difference in advancement between otherwise similar degree and non-degree candidates;
5. **human-review probability** — probability that an application reaches a person rather than ending in automated screening;
6. **posting-to-hire mismatch** — frequency with which actual hires fail advertised criteria.

The goal is not to help corporations optimize screening. It is to help workers distinguish **lack of ability** from **failure to satisfy a corporation's gate**, and to decide where additional effort, applications, credentials, or strategy are actually worth the cost.
