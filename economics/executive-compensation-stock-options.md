# Academic economics, executive compensation, and the stock-option turn

## Question

Did academic economists have a significant hand in the movement toward compensating senior executives with stock and stock options?

## Current answer

**Yes, with a causation qualification.** Academic financial economists supplied an influential intellectual justification for stronger equity-based executive incentives, and some leading economists explicitly advocated increasing executives' exposure to equity and stock options. That is evidence of a significant intellectual and policy role. It is not evidence that economists alone caused the 1990s option boom.

The strongest currently inspected retrospective evidence comes from Kevin J. Murphy, writing in 2024 about his work with Michael C. Jensen. Murphy says that their 1990 *Harvard Business Review* article, “CEO Incentives—It's Not How Much You Pay, But How,” has often been identified as helping launch the 1990s explosion in executive stock-option grants. More importantly for attribution, Murphy says that he and Jensen **did advocate increased reliance on stock options and other equity-based pay**. He also says their expectation was that equity compensation would substitute for other compensation; instead, firms largely layered large option grants on top of existing packages.

Murphy and Jensen's own later account is more complicated than a simple “economists caused it” story. Murphy argues that the option boom also reflected congressional efforts to constrain executive pay, tax treatment, accounting conventions that made options appear cheap, and board misunderstanding of option cost. This means the defensible Blackball claim is **material intellectual advocacy and legitimation**, not sole or sufficient causation.

## Primary and near-primary evidence

### Jensen and Murphy, 1990

Michael C. Jensen and Kevin J. Murphy's *Journal of Political Economy* article, “Performance Pay and Top-Management Incentives,” estimated that CEO wealth changed only about $3.25 for each $1,000 change in shareholder wealth. Their concern was that executive incentives were too weak.

Their companion *Harvard Business Review* article, “CEO Incentives—It's Not How Much You Pay, But How,” argued that attention to the absolute level of CEO pay distracted from the structure of incentives and called for compensation systems that produced large rewards for superior performance and meaningful penalties for poor performance. RePEc's summary of the article identifies substantial CEO equity ownership as one of their three recommendations.

Sources:

- Jensen, Michael C., and Kevin J. Murphy. “Performance Pay and Top-Management Incentives.” *Journal of Political Economy* 98, no. 2 (1990): 225–264. https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/261677
- Jensen, Michael C., and Kevin J. Murphy. “CEO Incentives—It's Not How Much You Pay, But How.” *Harvard Business Review*, May–June 1990. https://hbr.org/1990/05/ceo-incentives-its-not-how-much-you-pay-but-how
- RePEc record and summary of the latter article: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jacrfn/v3y1990i3p36-49.html

### Murphy's 2024 retrospective

Kevin J. Murphy's “Mike Jensen on CEO pay” is unusually useful because it is a retrospective by one of the economists involved. Murphy says that many have identified the 1990 HBR article with the subsequent explosion in executive stock-option grants. He then states directly that he and Jensen had advocated greater reliance on stock options and other equity-based pay, while explaining that they had expected equity pay to replace, rather than simply augment, salary and bonuses.

Murphy also reports their later reassessment: boards often treated options as nearly costless because they required neither a cash outlay nor, under then-prevailing accounting treatment, an ordinary compensation charge. He says they came to believe that too many options had been granted to too many people.

Source:

- Murphy, Kevin J. “Mike Jensen on CEO pay.” *Journal of Applied Corporate Finance* (2024). https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jacf.12625

### Bebchuk and Fried's contemporary framing

Lucian A. Bebchuk and Jesse M. Fried's 2004 book *Pay without Performance* explicitly places this intellectual history inside its argument. In the publicly released introduction, they say that prominent financial economists, naming Jensen and Murphy, urged shareholders in the early 1990s to accept large pay packages in exchange for stronger managerial incentives. They then argue that much of the resulting increase in compensation was not actually tied to managers' own performance.

This is useful evidence for the historical question, but it is also an argument by critics of prevailing compensation practice and should be kept distinct from Murphy's retrospective admission above.

Source dossier:

- [Lucian A. Bebchuk and Jesse M. Fried — *Pay without Performance*](bebchuk-fried-pay-without-performance.md)

## Causation boundary

Do **not** turn this record into the stronger statement that academic economists single-handedly created option-heavy executive compensation.

At minimum, the causal history also includes:

- tax treatment and the 1993 changes associated with Internal Revenue Code §162(m), which favored qualifying performance-based compensation;
- accounting rules under which conventional employee options could often be granted without recognizing the same ordinary compensation expense that cash compensation would produce;
- boards and compensation committees;
- compensation consultants;
- executive influence over boards;
- the long 1990s equity-market boom;
- broader corporate-governance norms.

The current evidence supports this narrower proposition:

> Academic financial economists were not bystanders. They supplied influential theory and explicit advocacy for stronger equity-based executive incentives, including increased reliance on stock options, and that advocacy became part of the institutional environment in which option-heavy compensation expanded.

## Retrieval / follow-up

A fuller causal history should separately inspect:

1. the legislative history and practical effect of §162(m);
2. APB Opinion No. 25 and the accounting treatment of at-the-money employee stock options before FAS 123R;
3. compensation-consulting practice during the 1980s and 1990s;
4. evidence tracing citations or direct uptake of Jensen–Murphy by boards, consultants, regulators, and business media;
5. empirical work decomposing the growth of executive pay into option design, stock-market growth, governance, tax, and accounting mechanisms.
