# Colonial Virginia — land, labor status, trades, and economic power, c. 1760–1782

This note reconstructs part of the social and economic structure surrounding George Washington in late-colonial Virginia. It is meant to answer a more useful question than “how many acres did Washington own?”:

> **Who owned productive assets, who controlled labor, who sold labor, who rented access to land, who could leave, and how many people occupied each kind of position?**

The evidence is uneven. Virginia did not conduct a modern census that classified everyone simultaneously as freeholder, tenant, hired hand, journeyman, apprentice, shopkeeper, indentured servant, enslaved worker, and planter. Different records illuminate different parts of the system: land and quit-rent rolls, tithable lists, deeds and leases, probate inventories, tax lists, account books, newspapers, apprenticeship contracts, and later historical reconstructions.

The categories below therefore **must not be collapsed into one social ladder**. Enslavement, indenture, apprenticeship, tenancy, and wage employment were radically different legal institutions. A person could also move between some of the free statuses over a lifetime.

For intuitive acreage comparisons, this note sometimes uses the working unit from the discussion that produced it:

- **1 parcel = 80 acres**
- **1/2 parcel = 40 acres**
- **640 acres = 1 square mile = 8 parcels**

That is only a comparison unit, not an eighteenth-century Virginia legal category.

## 1. Start with the population: slavery was not a marginal labor system

Thomas Jefferson's *Notes on the State of Virginia*, Query VIII, reproduced a 1782 population return and then estimated the counties and age/sex groups that the surviving returns omitted.

His reconstructed totals were:

| Status | Jefferson's 1782 estimate |
| --- | ---: |
| Free population | 296,852 |
| Enslaved population | 270,762 |
| Total | 567,614 |

The enslaved population in Jefferson's reconstruction was therefore just under half of Virginia's population.

This is **not a modern complete census**. Jefferson explicitly had to estimate missing counties and missing demographic groups. The value of the table is its scale: any model of late-colonial/revolutionary Virginia that imagines the countryside as principally large landowners plus a mass of free hired farmhands is wrong. Free wage workers existed, but an enormous part of Virginia's agricultural and craft labor force was enslaved.

Primary/institutional transcription:

- Thomas Jefferson, *Notes on the State of Virginia*, Query VIII, Monticello: https://www.monticello.org/notes-on-the-state-of-virginia/query-viii
- Yale Avalon transcription: https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/jeffvir.asp

## 2. The ordinary independent farmer was much smaller than Washington, but often larger than 80 acres

Jackson Turner Main used tax lists and land records to reconstruct the social structure of places such as Lunenburg County. Lunenburg in 1764 is especially useful because it was a developing Southside county rather than an old Tidewater county.

Main's reconstruction shows:

- more than half of the adult men held land;
- nearly three-quarters of the landholders held **100–500 acres**;
- the median holding was about **340 acres**;
- six estates exceeded 2,000 acres.

Using the 80-acre comparison unit:

- 100 acres = 1.25 parcels;
- 340 acres = 4.25 parcels;
- 500 acres = 6.25 parcels;
- 2,000 acres = 25 parcels.

So an 80-acre holding was meaningful property, but in this particular county it was below the median established freeholder's acreage. The common rural proprietor in this sample was neither landless nor remotely comparable to a Washington-scale planter.

The regional qualification matters. Lunenburg was still developing. Older Tidewater counties had different distributions, more established estates, and more landless men. Do not turn one county into a colony-wide percentage.

Source:

- Jackson Turner Main, *The Social Structure of Revolutionary America* (Princeton University Press, 1965), especially the discussion of Lunenburg County and mobility. JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt183pk38

## 3. Landless free men were not all “hired hands”

The Lunenburg records also prevent a common category error.

Among men without land, many were **members of landowning families**: sons and other dependents who had not yet become separate proprietors. Of the remaining landless men, some were independent heads of households and some appeared as dependent workers attached to other households. Those dependent workers are where hired laborers, servants, and overseers become visible in the tax records.

Main's longitudinal work also shows substantial movement into landownership among traceable landless men. The point is not that mobility was automatic. It is that in a developing county, “free hired hand” could often be a **life stage** rather than a permanent hereditary class position.

This differs from the social world suggested by later nineteenth-century itinerant shearers or swagmen. Virginia certainly had mobile free labor, but it did not have to supply the entire large-estate agricultural workforce because slavery supplied so much coerced labor.

Source:

- Main, *The Social Structure of Revolutionary America*, cited above.

### A closer analogue to the mobile hired crew

The Potomac Company gives a more recognizable hired-hand picture. Its 1785 charter contemplated hiring as many as **100 “good Hands”**, with wages, provisions, and spirits. The company's actual workforce mixed **hired hands, indentured servants, and enslaved workers**.

That is important because even on one project, people performing related physical work could occupy completely different legal statuses.

Source:

- George Washington's Mount Vernon, “The Potomac Company”: https://www.mountvernon.org/george-washington/the-potomac-company

## 4. Tenant farmers were not the same thing as hired laborers

A tenant farmer rented access to land rather than merely selling hours or days of labor.

That distinction could be economically large. A tenant might:

- control the cultivation of a farm;
- own tools, livestock, household goods, or other movable property;
- hire or command other labor;
- in slaveholding Virginia, even own enslaved people while renting the land itself.

So “landless” did not automatically mean “wage laborer,” and “tenant” did not automatically mean “poor farmhand.”

Beth Mitchell's reconstruction of Fairfax County in 1760 is particularly valuable because it maps landowners, leaseholders, tenants, enslavers, acreage, mills, ordinaries, roads, ferries, and other economic institutions in Washington's immediate region.

The exact Fairfax tenant tables should be transcribed directly before Blackball publishes county-wide counts from them.

Source to inspect directly for that next step:

- Beth Mitchell, ed. Donald M. Sweig, *Fairfax County, Virginia in 1760: An Interpretive Historical Map* (Fairfax County Office of Comprehensive Planning, 1987).

## 5. Williamsburg shows the small urban economy: masters, shopkeepers, merchants, and tavern keepers

James H. Soltow reconstructed occupations for **148 identifiable white residents of Williamsburg in 1770–1775**. This is not a census of Virginia and it is not even a complete census of Williamsburg. It is useful precisely because the surviving town records allow occupational detail that rural records usually do not.

Soltow found:

| Identified occupation group | Count |
| --- | ---: |
| Artisans | 63 |
| Merchandisers, total | 39 |
| — storekeepers | 19 |
| — merchants | 8 |
| Tavern / inn / ordinary keepers | 12 |

The remaining occupational groups included professionals, public officials, college personnel, resident planters, and others.

Soltow's especially important observation is that **most identifiable artisans were independent entrepreneurs or proprietors**. “Tradesman” therefore should not automatically be translated into “wage worker.”

The 63 artisans included people in building, clothing, transportation, metalwork, furniture, printing, grooming, and other crafts. A small Virginia town could contain cabinetmakers, carpenters, blacksmiths, shoemakers, tailors, wigmakers, silversmiths, printers, coachmakers, wheelwrights, saddlers, and related occupations.

Source:

- James H. Soltow, “The Occupational Structure of Williamsburg in 1775,” Colonial Williamsburg Research Report, 1956: https://research.colonialwilliamsburg.org/DigitalLibrary/view/index.cfm?doc=ResearchReports%5CRR0128.xml

## 6. Apprentice, journeyman, and master were economically different positions inside the same trade

The familiar sequence is useful if it is not romanticized:

### Apprentice

An apprentice was bound for a term under a training relationship. Virginia terms varied substantially. Colonial Williamsburg's blacksmith research records terms from **3 to 13 years**, often ending at age 21, and examples of courts binding children to craftsmen. Other Williamsburg research finds woodworking apprenticeships commonly lasting four or five years, with considerable variation.

The bargain could include:

- instruction in the trade;
- food and lodging;
- clothing;
- literacy instruction;
- tools or end-of-term benefits.

But those obligations must be checked in the actual contract. “Apprenticeship” does not by itself prove that good training was delivered.

### Journeyman

A journeyman had finished the apprenticeship stage but did not own the shop. He could work for wages or job-to-job for masters while accumulating skill, reputation, tools, and capital.

That is a closer urban analogue to a skilled wage worker.

### Master

In the colonial American setting, “master” often meant the person who **owned or ran the enterprise**, not necessarily the single most technically accomplished worker in the shop.

This distinction matters for class analysis. Two men might perform the same skilled operation at the same bench while one owned the tools, inventory, customer relationships, and premises and the other depended on wages.

Sources:

## 7. One shop could contain workers with radically different legal rights

Do not picture a workshop as a homogeneous “artisan class.”

A single eighteenth-century enterprise might contain:

- a proprietor/master;
- a free journeyman earning wages;
- an apprentice bound for training;
- an indentured servant bound for a term;
- an enslaved skilled worker.

They could be making the same cabinet, shoe, barrel, wagon, lock, or building component.

The technical task therefore does not tell us the worker's economic position. To reconstruct power, ask separately:

1. Who owns the shop and tools?
2. Who owns or controls the inventory and customer relationship?
3. Who receives the sale proceeds?
4. Who can leave?
5. Who can bargain over compensation?
6. Who can be sold or assigned?
7. Who acquires the skill and who captures the return from it?

This is why Blackball should keep **skill** distinct from **ownership, bargaining power, and legal freedom**.

## 8. Washington used free hired workers, indentured servants, and enslaved workers simultaneously

Mount Vernon is an unusually well-documented mixed labor system.

Mount Vernon's research summarizes **upwards of forty indentured servants acquired by Washington between 1760 and 1786**. They included skilled workers such as shoemakers, joiners, tailors, and bricklayers. Surviving agreements show that Washington could purchase a person's remaining term of service for a lump sum.

Examples also show transitions between statuses. John Askew worked as a joiner and later was hired to oversee enslaved carpenters. John Knowles completed an indenture and subsequently worked for wages. Caven Bowe, an indentured tailor, later operated his own shop in Alexandria.

Those trajectories matter because “servant,” “wage worker,” and “proprietor” could describe different stages of one free person's life. Enslavement was fundamentally different because it was inheritable property status rather than a term contract.

Sources:

- Mount Vernon, “John Askew”: https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/john-askew

## 9. Overseers were free employees, but they exercised delegated power over an enslaved workforce

Each of Washington's five farms eventually had an overseer. The overseer supervised crops, livestock, and workers and reported to Washington.

A surviving 1799 agreement for Roger Farrell paid **$133.33 per year plus board, lodging, and washing**. Other compensation packages could combine cash, provisions, housing, and livestock privileges.

So “wage” in this economy should not be imagined as a modern hourly paycheck. Compensation might be:

> cash + food + housing + clothing or maintenance + use of animals or land

The overseer is also a useful reminder that **selling one's own labor and exercising authority over other workers are not mutually exclusive**. A person can be an employee relative to an owner and a manager relative to people below him.

Source:

- Mount Vernon, “Overseer's Quarters”: https://www.mountvernon.org/the-estate-gardens/location/overseer-s-quarters

## 10. Washington's 1764 list puts land and labor on the same page

Washington's own 1764 memorandum is one of the clearest snapshots because it records taxable land and tithables together.

His listed land was:

| County | Acres |
| --- | ---: |
| Fairfax | 5,118 |
| Frederick | 2,498 |
| King George | 1,250 |
| Loudoun | 275 |
| Hampshire | 240 |
| **Total** | **9,381** |

That is about **117.3 of the 80-acre comparison parcels**.

The same memorandum lists **76 tithables** associated with his Fairfax operations and sorts people under headings including household servants, tradesmen, the home plantation, Muddy Hole, Dogue Run, River Side, and Creek Quarter.

A tithable list is a tax record, **not a complete household census**. Do not read “76” as the total number of people living on Washington's properties. Its value is structural: Washington's own bookkeeping puts extensive landholding, household service, skilled trades, and multiple plantation workforces inside a single economic organization.

Primary source:

- “Memorandum List of Tithables and Taxable Land and Property,” c. 17 June 1764, *Founders Online*, National Archives: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/02-07-02-0189

The 1762 predecessor is also useful because it labels occupational groups more specifically, including carpenters, smiths, a miller, and a ditcher:

- “Memorandum List of Tithables,” 9 June 1762, *Founders Online*: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/02-07-02-0082

## 11. By 1799 the scale of labor under Washington's control is even clearer

At Washington's death, **317 enslaved people** lived at Mount Vernon:

| Legal status in 1799 | People |
| --- | ---: |
| Owned by George Washington | 123 |
| Owned by the Custis estate | 153 |
| Rented from local landowners | 41 |
| **Total under the Mount Vernon labor system** | **317** |

This distinction is essential.

If the question is **legal title**, Washington personally owned 123.

If the question is **economic command over labor at Mount Vernon**, the relevant population is 317.

The difference between ownership and control is not a technicality. Washington could direct and profit from the labor of people whom he did not legally own outright.

Sources:

- Mount Vernon, “George Washington and Slavery”: https://www.mountvernon.org/george-washington/slavery
- *Founders Online*, “Washington's Slave List, June 1799”: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/06-04-02-0405

## 12. Where did Washington sit among rich Virginians?

Jackson Turner Main's 1954 article **“The One Hundred”** reconstructed the hundred richest Virginians of the 1780s from state tax records.

Main's method is useful but should not be mistaken for a modern Forbes list. Virginia taxed major forms of planter wealth—especially land and enslaved people, along with horses and cattle—but the records did not capture every relevant asset or liability. Main explicitly notes that productivity, market conditions, inherited debt, money lent at interest, and other factors could change actual income and net wealth.

The safe conclusion is:

- Virginia contained a very small stratum of great planters with enormous concentrations of land and enslaved labor;
- Washington belonged to that great-planter world;
- he was **not uniquely rich** among Virginia's elite families;
- an exact ordinal rank for Washington should not be published until Blackball transcribes the relevant Main table and the underlying tax entries directly.

Source:

- Jackson Turner Main, “The One Hundred,” *William and Mary Quarterly*, 3rd ser., 11, no. 3 (1954): 354–384: https://doi.org/10.2307/1943311

This is a useful future source-spider target: transcribe the hundred, acres, enslaved people, livestock, county, inherited/self-made status where Main provides it, and then compare Washington with nearby Mason, Fitzhugh, Lee, Carter, and Randolph fortunes without turning a partial tax reconstruction into false precision.

## 13. An economic-power matrix is more informative than a prestige ladder

The categories can be compared without pretending they were morally or legally equivalent:

| Position | Main productive asset/control | Labor relationship | Exit/bargaining position |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Enslaved person | little or no legally protected claim to own productive property; person treated as property | coerced labor | no contractual right to exit slavery |
| Indentured servant | labor committed for a term | bound service | limited by contract and law until term ends |
| Apprentice | training relationship plus bound service | labor exchanged partly for training/support | depends on contract, court, age, and local law |
| Free hired hand | owns own labor; usually little capital | sells labor for wages/piece rate/provisions | can bargain or leave subject to contract and economic necessity |
| Journeyman | owns skill and perhaps tools, not shop | skilled wage/job work | more skill bargaining power, but dependent on employers/customers |
| Tenant farmer | controls production on rented land | household/farm production subject to rent | more autonomy than a hired hand, but no title to land |
| Independent freeholder | owns land | family labor and possibly hired, bound, or enslaved labor | asset ownership increases outside options |
| Master artisan / shopkeeper | owns shop/tools/inventory/customer relationship | may work personally and employ/bind/control others | captures enterprise residual |
| Merchant / great planter | land, inventories, credit, commercial networks, and often large enslaved labor force | commands many workers and transactions | greatest financial and political outside options |

This table is analytical, not a census. It is designed to keep **ownership, control, skill, legal freedom, and income** from being confused with one another.

## 14. The modern Blackball connection: do not copy elite consumption while ignoring the elite balance sheet

The historical systems above are not direct analogues for modern college students. A student loan is not slavery, indenture, or apprenticeship, and Blackball should reject any rhetoric that collapses those institutions.

The useful continuity is narrower and more concrete:

> **The visible thing someone possesses does not tell you the balance-sheet position from which they acquired it.**

A wealthy family's child and a low-asset family's child can display the same university name on a sweatshirt and the same credential on a résumé while having radically different economic exposures.

One household may finance four years of education from accumulated assets and current income, retain substantial reserves, possess professional networks, and be able to absorb a failed major, weak job market, unpaid internship, geographic move, graduate school, or several months without work.

Another seventeen-year-old can be invited to purchase the **same visible status object** by taking on six figures of debt and committing years of future earnings.

Those are not the same transaction.

This is the precise version of the intuition sometimes expressed as **“aping one's betters.”** It should be treated as a hypothesis to test rather than an insult:

> Is a young person being encouraged to copy the consumption, credential, or career pattern of a much wealthier reference group **without possessing the asset base that makes that choice low-risk for the reference group?**

That question changes the educational decision ledger. Before imitating an elite pathway, ask:

1. **Who pays from assets, and who borrows?**
2. **Who bears the downside if the expected job does not appear?**
3. **What family resources remain after the purchase?**
4. **What income distribution does the credential actually lead to—not merely its best-known success stories?**
5. **How much freedom does the borrower retain to refuse a bad employer, move, retrain, care for family, or accept low-paid but desirable work?**
6. **What cheaper pathway buys the same productive skill, and what part of the price is purchasing status, network, or institutional access instead?**
7. **Would the transaction still look attractive if the prestigious name were hidden?**

This is a balance-sheet question before it is a culture-war question.

Adam Smith gives Blackball an unusually close eighteenth-century companion argument. In *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* he analyzes both the admiration directed toward wealth and rank and the “poor man's son” who reorganizes his life around obtaining the visible conditions of the rich. Blackball's point should not be “wealth is bad” or “education is bad.” It is:

> **Do not infer that a costly status-bearing path has the same economics for the imitator as for the person whose wealth made the path attractive in the first place.**

See:

- [Adam Smith — wealth, status, work, and tranquillity](adam-smith-wealth-status-work-and-tranquillity.md)
- [Robert Frost — “The Death of the Hired Man”](robert-frost-death-of-the-hired-man-1914.md), for a later literary treatment of seasonal wage labor, bargaining power, practical skill, and dependence.
- [Apprenticeship](../history/apprenticeship/README.md), for the rule that a training institution must be evaluated by its actual contract, costs, instruction, exit rights, and outcomes rather than its advertised form.

### “Second Gilded Age” is a research label, not a conclusion established here

It may be useful to compare contemporary wealth concentration and status consumption with the Gilded Age or other periods of concentrated wealth. But this Virginia note does **not** establish that modern America is a “Second Gilded Age,” nor would eighteenth-century planter Virginia be the right direct comparison for every modern institution.

A separate modern source note should quantify:

- household wealth concentration;
- billionaire wealth;
- tuition and student-debt distributions;
- parental wealth by institution/selectivity;
- borrowing by family wealth/income;
- completion and earnings distributions;
- how much debt changes occupational and geographic options after graduation.

Only then should Blackball make quantitative claims about the modern analogy.
