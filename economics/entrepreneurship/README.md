# Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship belongs in Blackball as a set of institutions to investigate, not as an automatic answer to the failures of college, graduate education, apprenticeship, or wage employment.

The first distinction is between ordinary livelihood enterprise and startup mythology. A family restaurant, nail salon, corner store, small motel, repair shop, franchise, neighborhood hardware store, machine shop, or home business is not simply a smaller and less successful venture-backed technology company.

Keep separate where evidence allows:

1. household livelihood enterprise;
2. owner-operator business;
3. family enterprise;
4. franchise;
5. growth-oriented small or medium firm;
6. venture-backed startup.

For ordinary enterprises, measure startup capital, financing, rent versus ownership, equipment and inventory, margins, owner and family hours, implicit wages, debt, household cash flow, asset accumulation, autonomy, exit costs, failure consequences, and succession.

Family and ethnic networks should be analyzed as concrete mechanisms rather than stereotypes. Pooled savings, family labor, housing, childcare, sector-specific training, supplier knowledge, customer introductions, credit guarantees, licensing knowledge, and succession may materially change whether a business is feasible. The same analysis must count coercive family authority, unpaid labor, concentrated risk, and weak exit rights when they exist.

Do not treat "white" as the absence of ethnicity. Where relevant, apply the same analysis to European-descended regional, religious, occupational, and migration communities.

## Core comparison question

When someone proposes "start a business" as an alternative to college or wage employment, ask:

**What exact enterprise is being proposed, what does the household put at risk, what does it receive, who supplies the work and capital, what remains if the business fails, and how does that compare with doing nothing?**

## Evidence rule

This index does not presume that entrepreneurship is superior or inferior to wage employment, college, or apprenticeship. Specific ethnic, immigrant, family-business, franchise, and industry cases require source-backed case files with definitions, dates, denominators, financing evidence, labor inputs, household returns, and competing interpretations.
