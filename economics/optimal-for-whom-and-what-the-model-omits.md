# Optimal for whom, and what did the model omit?

Economic language often moves too quickly from a conditional calculation to a claim about how the world works or what people ought to do.

Keep three questions separate:

1. **What behavior does the model describe?**
2. **What action maximizes the stated objective under the model’s assumptions?**
3. **Why should that objective and those included costs govern the real decision?**

A calculation can be internally correct and still answer the wrong person’s question.

## “Optimal” is incomplete

Whenever a source says that an action is optimal, record:

- optimal for whom;
- with respect to what objective;
- over what time horizon;
- under which constraints;
- using whose discount rate;
- counting which people’s gains and losses;
- assigning risk to whom;
- treating which effects as external to the calculation;
- assuming what bargaining power, mobility, information, and legal enforcement.

Without those terms, “optimal” can smuggle one party’s objective into a universal recommendation.

## Sunk cost: rule versus description

“Ignore sunk costs” is a decision rule under a specified objective. It is not a description of how people ordinarily behave.

People often honor sunk costs, organize identity around prior commitments, and continue trying to make an expensive professional path work. An economics course that teaches the rule as though it described ordinary behavior has changed subjects without saying so.

The descriptive fact matters institutionally because sellers can benefit from predictable commitment and exit resistance. See [sunk-cost lock-in and lies](../education/sunk-cost-lock-in-and-lies.md).

## Net present value does not choose the moral universe

A net-present-value calculation discounts an assumed stream of future cash flows. It does not establish:

- that the cash flows were obtained justly;
- that the decision improves social welfare;
- that workers, communities, creditors, customers, or future owners were counted;
- that risk was assigned to the party receiving the gain;
- that layoffs, injuries, environmental damage, service deterioration, or lost institutional capacity are negligible;
- that loading an organization with debt is harmless because the transaction pays its investors;
- that the discount rate reflects the interests of everyone affected.

If a leveraged acquisition creates a positive return for the buyer while workers lose jobs, the company bears debt, suppliers go unpaid, or a community loses an employer, the NPV may be perfectly correct **for the buyer’s selected cash flows**. It has not shown that the transaction was rational for everyone or good in any broader sense.

The user’s Mitt Romney/private-equity example belongs here as a research lead: inspect particular transactions and identify exactly whose cash flows made the deal look successful, where debt was placed, who received fees or distributions, who lost employment or claims, and what happened to the operating company afterward. Do not use the name as a substitute for transaction-level evidence.

## Education return calculations have the same problem

An education NPV can be made favorable by:

- comparing graduates with nongraduates without adequately handling selection;
- using average or median earnings while hiding the lower tail;
- counting all graduate earnings as caused by the degree;
- excluding interest, foregone work experience, delayed housing, family effects, geographic constraint, and repeated further schooling;
- treating any employment as success;
- combining employment with continued education;
- ignoring what work the graduate performs and whom it serves;
- assigning no cost to regret, resentment, or lost trust;
- using a lifetime horizon to overwhelm severe early-career losses;
- counting the institution’s survival and faculty employment as evidence of student benefit.

The arithmetic cannot repair a bad causal comparison or an incomplete cost ledger.

## Description, recommendation, and excuse

For each use of economic analysis, label the claim:

- **description:** a statement about observed behavior or outcomes;
- **prediction:** what the model expects under stated conditions;
- **private decision rule:** what maximizes one actor’s objective;
- **social-welfare claim:** what supposedly improves collective outcomes;
- **legal or moral justification:** why the action should be permitted or approved;
- **retrospective excuse:** a calculation produced after the decision to rationalize who won.

These are not interchangeable. A formula can supply a precise answer after the analyst has hidden the decisive political and class questions in the choice of objective and boundary.

## Plain-language translation

Every formal calculation used in Blackball should be accompanied by an ordinary-language statement:

> This calculation counts these people, these cash flows, and these risks. It does not count these other people, harms, obligations, or alternatives. Under those choices, this action has the larger number.

That translation makes it harder to turn a local optimization into a universal claim.
