# Reading beside future authority figures

## Thesis under examination

Reading Shakespeare may not make a person powerful. Reading Shakespeare beside people who later become judges, legislators, senior administrators, executives, professors, donors, or other authority figures may place that person inside relationships through which power is later organized.

The scarce product may therefore be neither Shakespeare nor even instruction considered alone. It may partly be admission to a socially selected cohort and prolonged proximity to people likely to exercise authority.

This is a hypothesis for investigation, not a settled conclusion.

## Two different expectations of a degree

A student who needs college to pay for itself quickly may reasonably expect a short chain:

> degree → recognized capacity → job → wages

A family with substantial resources can afford a longer chain:

> selective institution → prestigious peers → shared culture and trust → introductions and opportunities → authority accumulated over decades

This distinction need not depend on different intellectual tastes. The same literature course can occupy different economic positions in different lives. A working-class student may need an immediate labor-market return. A wealthy family can finance uncertain internships, geographic mobility, further degrees, low-paid prestige work, business experiments, political work, and years of relationship-building.

Accordingly, the maxim that education should not be judged as vocational training may operate differently by class. It can describe a genuine cultural good while also obscuring the economic risk borne by students who lack a financial cushion.

## Claims this note does not establish

This hypothesis does not by itself establish that:

- reading Shakespeare causes power;
- every affluent student consciously attends college to acquire a network;
- every working-class student treats a degree only as job training;
- classmates actually help one another later;
- elite education creates advantages rather than merely selecting people who already possess them;
- cultural study has no intrinsic value;
- social proximity is the only or principal mechanism connecting elite schools to authority.

Family wealth, prior schooling, legacy status, admissions selection, institutional reputation, geographic concentration, marriage, discrimination, and occupational licensing are plausible competing or interacting explanations.

## What would count as evidence

Blackball should look for evidence at several distinct stages:

1. **Selection:** Who enters particular institutions, courses, residential houses, clubs, and graduate programs?
2. **Proximity:** Who studied, lived, clerked, organized, or worked together?
3. **Later authority:** Which participants later occupied positions with institutional power?
4. **Activation:** Is there evidence that an earlier relationship later supplied an appointment, recommendation, investment, client, job, marriage, political alliance, or other opportunity?
5. **Comparison:** Do similarly qualified people without that proximity experience different outcomes?
6. **Class horizon:** How do debt, family transfers, unpaid work, additional schooling, and time-to-stable-employment alter which students can wait for long-term returns?

The Volden–Wai–Wiseman congressional education data can help identify later authority figures and educational stacking, but it cannot by itself show that classmates knew one another or that proximity caused an outcome. Course rosters, yearbooks, residential records, student organizations, clerkships, employment histories, acknowledgments, correspondence, appointments, and biographies may supply additional links.

## Computer-generated criticism

This thesis should be exposed to repeated computer-generated criticism. The purpose is not to manufacture agreement or replace human judgment. A useful criticism pass should:

- state the thesis in its strongest defensible form;
- identify hidden assumptions and ambiguous terms;
- propose rival explanations;
- search for counterexamples and negative cases;
- distinguish selection, treatment, mediation, and outcome;
- identify evidence that would falsify or substantially weaken the thesis;
- flag claims that exceed the available sources;
- suggest feasible comparisons and data joins;
- preserve disagreements rather than averaging them into a false consensus.

Generated criticism must be treated as a source of questions and candidate objections, not as evidence. Every factual objection still requires a traceable source, and every inference should be labeled as an inference.

## Initial research question

When people encounter the same cultural curriculum at institutions occupying different positions in the class structure, how much of their later difference in authority is associated with what they studied, the credential they received, whom they studied beside, the resources that allowed them to wait, or selection that occurred before admission?
