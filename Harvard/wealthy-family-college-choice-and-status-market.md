# Wealthy-family college choice and the elite admissions status market

This note is a source ledger for a narrow question: **what does college choice look like inside the unusually wealthy private-school / private-consultant market, and what can that tell us about Harvard and peer institutions as objects of status, culture, and family strategy?**

It is not a claim that wealthy families are homogeneous, that students share their parents' politics, or that any reported shift away from Harvard or another Ivy is permanent.

## Unresolved source lead: the X/Twitter thread

A reported X/Twitter thread appears to have described a private college-admissions consultant saying that many clients no longer wanted Harvard or similar schools, possibly using language such as "woke" schools. The exact thread has **not yet been identified**.

Do not silently substitute one of the sources below for that thread. They are useful because they document closely related claims and may help identify the author, but the thread remains an unresolved primary-source lead.

The strongest candidate so far is **Christopher Rim**, founder and CEO of Command Education, a high-end college-admissions consultancy.

## Christopher Rim / Command Education: the market itself

Caitlin Moscatello's January 2024 New York Magazine profile of Rim describes a consulting business aimed explicitly at very wealthy families. At the time, Command Education had about 190 clients, mostly private-school students, and Rim's quoted annual price was $120,000. The service extended well beyond proofreading applications: mentors helped manage coursework, extracurriculars, internships, essays, testing strategy, and the overall narrative presented to admissions offices.

The profile is useful for Blackball because it exposes a social environment that most applicants never encounter:

- Rim said some clients saved his number under a fake name and required NDAs.
- A parent at Trinity School reportedly offered him **$1.5 million not to work with another student in the child's class**.
- A private-school parent described close friends ceasing to discuss consultants and summer programs once admissions competition intensified.
- Rim summarized one part of the market as helping one billionaire's child compete against another billionaire's child.

This is not ordinary "college counseling." It is a market in which already advantaged families purchase additional differentiation against other advantaged families.

Source: Caitlin Moscatello, "Inventing the Perfect College Applicant," *New York Magazine*, January 29, 2024, republished by Command Education:
https://www.commandeducation.com/press/college-admissions-educational-consultants-command-education/

## A documented shift in college lists before the 2024 protest cycle

A 2022 *Town & Country* article described conservative-leaning wealthy families reconsidering the usual Northeastern/elite-college path. Rim gave one concrete example: a family with children at Dalton shifted attention away from Stanford, Duke, and Georgetown toward Washington University in St. Louis, Emory, and SMU. The family's stated concern, as reported by Rim, was finding a community where opposing viewpoints could be debated without social cancellation.

This predates the 2023-2024 Gaza-war campus protests, so it should not be collapsed into the later controversy. It documents a broader cultural sorting process already underway in at least part of this client market.

Source: Nicole LaPorte, "Why Are More and More Northern Kids Heading South For College?" *Town & Country*, October 2, 2022:
https://www.townandcountrymag.com/education-college/a41397404/southern-colleges-admissions-boom/

## Harvard: rejection, then partial return

In early 2024, Rim told reporters that some clients had become strongly opposed to Harvard during the controversies following October 7, 2023. By March, however, he said he was already seeing some of those same clients "easing back into" considering Harvard.

That reversal is analytically more interesting than a simple "the rich abandoned Harvard" story. Harvard's attraction appears capable of surviving substantial political or reputational objections. Prestige, selectivity, networks, family expectations, and perceived career value can pull in one direction while political or cultural objections pull in another.

Source: PRSA, "Despite Reputational Blows, Harvard Regains 'Dream School' Status," April 2024:

## Columbia and Penn: a small but striking 2024 consultant sample

In May 2024, *The Straits Times* reported that Command Education said parents had raised concerns about protests at Ivy League schools. Rim said that in that admissions cycle:

- 12 Command clients had offers from Columbia; only 1 was considering attending.
- 8 had offers from Penn; 4 planned to attend.
- The firm described this as a departure from its historical experience, in which acceptance to these schools often meant attendance.

The same article profiled a student offered Harvard who instead chose the National University of Singapore after parental objections to the US campus climate.

These numbers are **not a representative sample of Ivy League admits**. They are a small, selected set of clients of one consultancy, reported by the consultancy itself. Their value is ethnographic/contextual: they show that declining an Ivy offer is a live option in a wealthy consulting market and that political/cultural concerns can enter that decision.

Source: *The Straits Times*, "Some Singapore students decide against going to elite US colleges over campus protests," May 2024:
https://www.straitstimes.com/singapore/campus-protests-at-elite-us-colleges-cause-singapore-students-to-reconsider-going-there

## What not to infer

The material above does **not** establish:

- that Harvard ceased to be desirable among wealthy families;
- that wealthy applicants are generally conservative;
- that students share their parents' reasons for selecting or rejecting a school;
- that "woke," "weird," "political," "intellectually serious," and "hostile to donors" are interchangeable descriptions;
- that choosing a Southern or non-Ivy university is necessarily a political decision;
- that the consultant's clients represent the overall applicant population.

Until the X/Twitter thread is found, its wording should remain unknown rather than reconstructed from adjacent reporting.

## Why this belongs in Blackball

The useful question is not whether one anonymous teenager or parent is admirable. It is to document a social system that is normally invisible from outside it.

In this system, families can spend six figures per year on application strategy, conceal which consultant they use from close friends, compete for exclusive access to a consultant, and treat a set of highly selective universities as both educational institutions and positional goods. They can also decide that an institution's culture is objectionable while continuing to value its credential strongly enough to reconsider it a few months later.

That tension is worth recording without deciding in advance whether the people involved are enviable, ridiculous, prudent, frightened, ideological, loving parents, status competitors, or some mixture of those things.

## Adam Smith: is the prize actually good for the person pursuing it?

For the older version of precisely this question, see [Adam Smith — wealth, status, work, and tranquillity](../economics/adam-smith-wealth-status-work-and-tranquillity.md).

Smith's sharpest passage here is in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), not primarily *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). He describes an ambitious person who imagines wealth and greatness will finally provide ease, then spends a lifetime working, competing, flattering, and sacrificing present tranquillity to obtain that imagined future repose.

*The Wealth of Nations* supplies a complementary argument: the persistent effort of individuals to improve their condition is a major engine of private and national opulence. Put together, the two passages create a tension useful for Blackball: **status striving can help produce aggregate wealth while still being a bad bargain, or at least a deeply ambiguous one, for the person doing the striving.**

That does not prove that rich people are miserable, and Smith's example is initially an ambitious climber rather than a person born rich. It does block the lazy inference that greater wealth and rank automatically mean a better lived life.

## Research questions

1. Locate the exact X/Twitter thread and preserve its original wording, date, author, and context.
2. Determine whether the thread was by Rim or another admissions/private-school consultant.
3. If the thread used "woke," identify which schools the author placed in that category and what observable criteria, if any, were given.
4. Separate parent preference from student preference whenever reporting permits it.
5. Track revealed preference: where did students actually enroll after rejecting Harvard, Columbia, Penn, Stanford, etc.?
6. Compare consultant anecdotes with institution-level yield data; do not infer a population shift from a handful of wealthy clients.
7. Look for similar accounts from private-school counselors, family offices, alumni networks, and consultants serving wealthy international families.
8. Keep the descriptive question separate from moral ranking of private individuals.
