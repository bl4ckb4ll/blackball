# MIT: employment promise and academic self-reproduction

The question is not merely whether an MIT graduate is eventually “employed.” Ask what the education actually converted into, how long the conversion took, what had to be paid for it, what work the graduate ultimately performed, and whether the resulting job is independent evidence that outside employers valued the training.

## The customer question

A direct version of the challenge is:

- You take the compiler course. Can you actually get a compiler job?
- You do substantial compiler work in a laboratory or master’s program. Can you then get the compiler job?
- If even an unusually advantaged, hard-working MIT-trained candidate cannot make that conversion cleanly, what should a state-school student or someone watching the same material online infer about their own chances?
- If the eventual destination is another university, why should that be folded into the same outcome category as an outside employer independently purchasing the skill?
- What did MIT do for the student who paid it, separately from what MIT did for professors, laboratories, and its own prestige?

The compiler examples remain research leads until attached to named people and primary records. The questions are retained because they identify what evidence would decide whether instruction actually converts into the occupation used to justify it.

## Do not let “employed” erase the route

Record at least:

1. target occupation sought;
2. courses and laboratory work represented as relevant;
3. whether the target occupation was obtained;
4. elapsed years between entering the pathway and stable work;
5. temporary, postdoctoral, contingent, and further-degree stages;
6. debt incurred and repayment terms;
7. whether the eventual employer is inside higher education;
8. compensation, hours, location, and family constraints;
9. whether the job required additional credentials beyond the original pathway;
10. actual clients, users, students, and ordinary work products;
11. whether the graduate would choose the route again with full knowledge.

A graduate becoming a professor is real employment, but it is partly endogenous evidence: the education system is employing its own output. Elite institutions also have an unusually strong route into academic employment, so this outcome should not be treated as interchangeable with general external demand.

## Academic self-reproduction

Report separately:

- faculty positions;
- postdoctoral appointments;
- academic research staff;
- teaching-only and contingent positions;
- university administration and other internal employment;
- employment by an outside organization purchasing the trained skill for a noneducational purpose.

At the top of the academic hierarchy, elite graduates can occupy professor jobs that are much less accessible to graduates of ordinary state schools. That can make elite placement statistics look stronger without showing that the underlying field provides comparable external work for everyone being encouraged to study it.

The circular risk is:

**the school produces graduates → some graduates obtain jobs producing and teaching more graduates → those jobs are used as evidence that the original education produced occupational value**

That loop may support real teaching and research. It still must be identified rather than hidden inside “employed.”

## Justin Curry case

Justin Curry earned an MIT bachelor’s degree in mathematics in 2008 and later pursued a mathematics PhD at the University of Pennsylvania, completed in 2014. His thesis, *Sheaves, Cosheaves and Applications*, explicitly aims both to introduce the subject pedagogically and to present new mathematics.

The user read the thesis around 2011–2012 and found it unusually comprehensible and useful. Its diagrams, explanation of sheaf and cosheaf ideas, and entrance-path-category material helped the user understand work that other mathematicians had not made accessible. Preserve that as a positive first-person judgment about exposition.

Do not use that judgment to answer a different question: whether the educational route, debt, labor market, academic placement, or resulting service to students justified its price and promises.

Curry later held a Duke mathematics postdoctoral appointment and became faculty at the University at Albany, SUNY. This is a successful academic trajectory. It is also an example of academic employment and must be classified as such rather than treated as direct evidence that a mathematics degree reliably converts into external occupational demand.

## Debt: enforceable downside, unenforceable upside

The asymmetry is stark when the student reaches the end of a long path and discovers that the labor market is weak while the debt remains enforceable.

- Tuition, principal, interest, fees, and repayment are contractual.
- The anticipated compiler job, professor job, research career, salary, and social value usually are not.
- The institution receives its money before occupational conversion is known.
- The graduate bears the need to make the specialized path work after years of sunk investment.

The user’s formulation is:

- repayment is written in blood;
- predicted success is written in invisible ink.

Do not let a school’s confident possibility language acquire the status of a guarantee that the contract withholds.

## Fun, novelty, worth, and productivity are different claims

A later Curry post about playing with Reeb graphs prompted an important correction in this project.

From the fact that an academic is working with Reeb graphs, one cannot infer that the activity is novel, difficult, mathematically valuable, socially useful, economically productive, or worth public or student subsidy. Reeb graphs are an older construction. Modern activity involving them may be original research, exposition, software, teaching, recombination, or enjoyable exploration. The subject label alone decides none of those questions.

Likewise, an activity can be fun without producing an occupational or economic output. Do not defend academic work reflexively with prestige-loaded phrases such as “intellectually serious” or “mathematically worthwhile” unless the relevant standard and evidence are supplied.

For each activity, separate:

- personal enjoyment;
- pedagogical usefulness;
- novelty;
- mathematical result;
- software, data, theorem, explanation, or other output;
- identifiable users;
- external willingness to pay;
- student value;
- public or institutional purpose.

No single item automatically establishes the others.

## Academic success is not automatically student value

If the eventual escape from a weak external labor market is an academic job, do not stop at “success.” Ask what the resulting department and faculty member actually deliver to the students who now pay them.

Measure:

- teaching clarity;
- course access;
- availability and feedback;
- mentoring;
- comprehensible notes and diagrams;
- completion;
- usable knowledge;
- downstream student outcomes;
- whether students are mainly being prepared to reproduce the same academic hierarchy;
- how faculty time is divided among teaching, research, grants, administration, consulting, and job seeking.

A research career can be successful for the researcher without answering the customer’s question:

> What did this department do for the student who paid it?

There is also an incentive conflict to investigate rather than assume away. After a long and precarious qualification process, faculty may be under strong pressure to protect publication, grant, promotion, and next-job prospects. Test whether those incentives crowd out teaching or student service.

## Strongest-case ceiling

An unusually advantaged and capable graduate can serve as a stress test. When someone with an elite credential, strong mentors, specialized work, unusual effort, and access to the top of the hierarchy still encounters a long qualification chain, debt pressure, weak target-job conversion, or dependence on academic employment, that weakens the easy answer that less successful students merely failed to work hard enough or attend a good enough school.

One case does not establish a population rate. It can reveal the ceiling of the pathway and change the prior for people with fewer advantages.

## Evidence still to pin down

Attach named people and primary sources before presenting these as factual case histories:

- MIT compiler-course graduate unable to obtain compiler work;
- substantial compiler laboratory or master’s work followed by inability to obtain compiler work;
- any unusually long mathematics-PhD trajectory being used as an MIT-alumnus labor-market example;
- statements by the people involved about job-market expectations, debt, target work, and later compromises;
- Curry’s Reeb-graph post and the particular activity the user was discussing;
- student evaluations, course materials, and other evidence bearing on what faculty actually provide students.

The intended comparison is not MIT versus doing nothing. It is MIT versus cheaper ways of acquiring the same knowledge, state-school study, self-study, direct employment, trades, entrepreneurship, housing, and other uses of the same years and capital.
