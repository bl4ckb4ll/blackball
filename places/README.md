# Place dossiers

## Purpose

These dossiers describe particular American local social worlds without turning a place name into a verdict about every resident.

The motivating question is simple:

> What does life actually look like in places that repeatedly feed selective schools, professional-managerial careers, expensive extracurricular systems, and elite institutions?

The answer should be built from ordinary observable material: population and income, housing, schools, college-counseling infrastructure, admissions visits, private-school tuition, summer programs, youth sports, domestic labor, childcare, philanthropy, clubs, local businesses, transportation, travel programs, employment, and nearby inequality.

The point is **description before judgment**. A reader can decide what to make of the resulting world.

## Historical vocabulary

Andrew Hacker's 2005 New York Review of Books essay, while discussing selective-college admissions, quotes Ross Douthat describing Harvard students as coming from the country's **"upwardly mobile enclaves."**

Source:
- Andrew Hacker, "The Truth About the Colleges," *New York Review of Books*, 3 Nov. 2005: https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2005/11/03/the-truth-about-the-colleges/

This is useful historical vocabulary, not a classification Blackball should impose automatically.

## Evidence states

Every dossier should keep these categories separate:

- **public primary** — census tables, school profiles, municipal records, budgets, tax records, official program pages, etc.
- **public secondary** — reporting, scholarship, histories, ethnographies, books.
- **institutional self-presentation** — what a school, club, city, or organization says about itself.
- **private lead** — a firsthand or secondhand anecdote that may suggest a question but is **not public evidence and must not be cited as establishing a population claim**.
- **interpretation** — an inference or possible explanation, explicitly separated from the observations beneath it.
- **unknown** — a question for which no adequate evidence has yet been found.

## Boundary rule

Do not silently substitute a whole city for the narrower social world under discussion.

This matters especially for:

- **Aurora, Illinois** — a large and heterogeneous city; an affluent east-Aurora / Naperville / Indian Prairie school ecology may need to be separated from Aurora as a whole.
- **Richmond, Virginia** — the city as a whole is not an affluent enclave; West End neighborhoods and the independent-school network may be the relevant unit.
- **Evanston, Illinois** — affluent lakeshore and Northwestern-centered milieus coexist with substantial economic and racial diversity.
- **Greenwich, Connecticut** — town, central CDP, public-school district, and private-school network are different measurement units.
- **Skaneateles, New York** — village, town, and lakefront property markets should not be conflated.

## What to measure

Each dossier should eventually contain:

1. **Geographic unit** — city, town, CDP, school district, neighborhood, ZIP code, or private-school catchment.
2. **Population and money** — income distribution, per-capita income, poverty, home values, rents, property taxes, wealth proxies.
3. **Occupations and employers** — where household income comes from and which institutions structure the local labor market.
4. **Public schools** — enrollment, course offerings, AP/IB, college counseling, admissions visits, matriculation when public, extracurricular infrastructure.
5. **Independent schools** — tuition, financial-aid share, college counseling, matriculation, facilities, fundraising, parent associations.
6. **Childhood infrastructure** — camps, sports, tutors, lessons, travel, enrichment, after-school schedules.
7. **Domestic and care labor** — childcare, nanny and housecleaning markets where observable; wages and agencies rather than stereotypes about workers or employers.
8. **Adult status infrastructure** — clubs, charity benefits, galas, country clubs, professional associations, real-estate marketing, lifestyle publications.
9. **Stress and health** — only where defensible public data exist. Prescription or survey data must not be converted into claims about motives or particular social classes without evidence.
10. **Marriage / household formation** — only through public demographic, legal, ethnographic, or other defensible sources; anecdotes from divorce lawyers are case material, not prevalence estimates.
11. **Nearby inequality** — who performs service work, where workers live, commuting, school boundaries, and how affluent institutions relate to surrounding populations.
12. **Counterevidence** — financial aid, heterogeneity, ordinary lives, public institutions, and anything else that prevents caricature.

## Private-lead rule

Blackball can preserve the *question raised* by a private anecdote without publishing the anecdote as fact.

Examples now in the queue:

- accounts of extensive nanny / household-staff use among affluent families;
- an account of high-pressure Aurora-area parents combining stimulants and sedatives to sustain work/family schedules;
- an account from someone associated with an elite private school suggesting that some children experienced themselves more as parental status objects than as loved family members;
- impressions of highly transactional spouse selection among wealthy professional households.

These are **research prompts only**. They do not establish that a named place, school, sex, occupation, or income group behaves this way.

For each, seek public evidence that can answer a narrower observable question: household staffing, childcare expenditures, working hours, psychotropic prescribing, student well-being, parental-pressure surveys, marriage patterns, prenups, divorce cases, or ethnographic reporting.

## Relationship to Hacker and Dreifus

The place dossiers are a companion surface to Andrew Hacker and Claudia Dreifus's *Higher Education?* and to Hacker's earlier writing on selective admissions.

The useful connection is not "these towns are bad." It is that selective-college admissions operate through actual high schools, counselors, parent expectations, sports, money, travel, private services, and recruiting circuits. Place dossiers make those inputs visible.
