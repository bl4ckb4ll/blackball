# Harriet Taylor Mill: when the wife was a philosopher

**Case status:** 18 October 2026. The edited primary text of Mill's 1851 marriage statement, Taylor's *On Marriage*, and critical apparatus of *Collected Works* vol. XXI have been inspected online. The complete Mill–Taylor letters and manuscript images have not been examined side by side.

**Why this matters:** The title “The Philosopher's Wife” can conceal a second philosopher. Harriet Taylor Mill wrote original arguments about equality, marriage and freedom; her agency and authorship matter independently of John Stuart Mill's devotion.

## Primary authored text, visible in a critical edition

University of Toronto Press, John M. Robson (ed.), [*Collected Works of John Stuart Mill*, vol. XXI, *Essays on Equality, Law, and Education* (1984)](https://oll-resources.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/oll3/store/titles/255/Mill_0223-21.html) includes:

- **Harriet Taylor, “On Marriage” (1832–33?)**, appendix A, pp. 375ff. The editor reports a holograph manuscript in the Mill–Taylor Collection, London School of Economics, undated/unsigned but in Taylor's hand. A clear source for **her philosophy of marriage** independent of John's later narrative.
- **John Stuart Mill, “On Marriage” (1832–33?)**, pp. 35ff, also in his hand, to read alongside hers rather than substituting one author for the other.
- **John Stuart Mill, “Statement on Marriage,” signed 6 March 1851**, pp. 97–99. Mill declared the English law of marriage to give a husband coercive legal powers over a wife's property, person and actions, and promised not to exercise them. Editorial note points to a manuscript facsimile published in Hugh S. R. Elliot, *The Letters of John Stuart Mill* (1910), vol. I, opposite pp. 159–160.
- **Harriet Taylor Mill, “The Enfranchisement of Women” (1851)**, appendix C, pp. 393ff; originally anonymous in the *Westminster Review* (July 1851), later issued under her name. Editorial discussion explicitly notes an attribution problem: her original contribution is substantial but the nature of John's editing is disputed. Do not claim exclusive or joint authorship beyond specific evidence.

This case reverses the pattern found in Russell and Einstein: Mill recorded an **explicit refusal of unjust marital powers**, though the document alone does not establish that he fulfilled it in every act. Contrast assertions with records of money, residence, work, conflict and relations with children.

## Critical perspective

The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Harriet Taylor Mill](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/harriet-mill/) distinguishes her philosophical works from later admirers' claims about influence, including Mill's own strong public attribution of ideas to her and scholarly doubts about the exact division of authorship.

In 2026, Meredith E. Tusan published [“What Liberalism Requires: The Very Victorian Marriage of J. S. Mill and Harriet Hardy Taylor”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-british-studies/article/what-liberalism-requires-the-very-victorian-marriage-of-j-s-mill-and-harriet-hardy-taylor/53D1B6D2A01A1EC6F794DC0499F9CB1B), a scholarly approach to the relationship as historical practice rather than sentimental icon. Abstract/bibliographic record checked, **article not read in full**.

Harriet was married first to John Taylor; all three individuals and Harriet's three children merit careful attention. Her and Mill's happiness cannot establish that the earlier marriage or the family's adjustments were painless. A gender-equality principle should not be measured only by whether the famous man was satisfied.

## Claims supported / not supported

**Supported:** Harriet authored philosophical manuscripts; in March 1851 Mill condemned legally unequal marriage and pledged not to invoke the legal powers; subsequent scholarly work disputes simplistic attributions of authorship.

**Not yet established:** the full allocation of housework and money, all effects on Harriet's children, whether Mill consistently acted on his pledge, whether Harriet considered the marriage unqualified happiness, or the exact percentage of each published argument written by Harriet.
