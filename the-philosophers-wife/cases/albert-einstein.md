# Albert Einstein and Mileva Marić: the marriage conditions in writing

**Case status (2026-10-17):** A July 1914 memorandum has been located in a public scholarly transcription, with bibliographic confirmation from the Einstein Papers Project. **The annotated critical edition has not been read page by page in this pass.**

## The evidence

Albert Einstein and Mileva Marić met while studying physics and married in 1903. They had children; by 1914 the marriage was breaking down. In Berlin, Einstein produced a written list of terms for continuing to share a household.

The [transcription of the July 1914 memorandum and follow-up letter](https://scientiamag.org/mileva-and-albert-einstein-love-and-loathing/) records Einstein requiring Marić to maintain his clothes and work space and to bring him three meals a day in his room. He also required her to forgo nonessential personal relations, not expect intimacy, stop talking when he asked, and leave his study on demand. In the follow-up letter, he explained that his interest in maintaining the shared home was principally to remain near their sons.

**This is a primary document in a later translation/transcription**, rather than a diagnosis of Einstein's personality. The [Einstein Papers Project, Volume 8](https://www.einstein.caltech.edu/what-we-do/published-volumes/volume-8) covers the relevant 1914–18 correspondence, and the [CERN catalogue](https://cds.cern.ch/record/634426?ln=en) lists a letter from Einstein to Marić ca. 18 July 1914. The English translation should be checked against the editorial notes and German original before quoting exact sentences as an authoritative edition.

Marić and the children returned to Zurich in July 1914, and the couple divorced in 1919. These dates are described in the retrospective review and her biographical scholarship. This case does not rest merely on knowing that the marriage ended; it rests on the **specific demands in a surviving letter**.

## What this does and does not demonstrate

- The memorandum establishes what Einstein **wrote he wanted from his wife**, not how often any particular housework was performed or exactly how Marić felt on each day.
- The sequence of affectionate early letters and later hostile demands matters: the marriage changed over time, and Marić was an educated physicist with interests of her own.
- A hurtful marriage **does not settle** the disputed historical question of whether Marić coauthored, assisted, or independently contributed to particular Einstein scientific results. Those claims require technical and documentary evidence, not sympathy or prestige. Likewise, lack of proven coauthorship would not justify the domestic demands.
- This case is especially suitable for Blackball because the document comes from Einstein himself: a direct comparison between an admired public life and instructions for the household is possible without using gossip.
