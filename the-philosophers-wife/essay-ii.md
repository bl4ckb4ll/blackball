# The Philosopher's Wife, II: The Missing Replies

*A source-based second movement; private conversations are not reconstructed. The evidentiary chain and reading status are recorded in the [source register](source-register.md).*

In an account of a great man's life, the wife usually arrives in the middle of someone else's sentence. She is introduced because he married her, she disappears while he thinks, and she returns because the marriage has begun to interfere with the thinking.

Sofia Tolstaya could be made to fit this kind of biography. For almost half a century she lived with a man who had an ever more elaborate theory of how life should be lived. She copied manuscripts, cared for children, managed property, and tried to live her own intellectual and emotional life among his convictions. In 1913 and 1915 she published collections of the letters he had sent to her. The editors of a 2017 critical edition say that those earlier collections omitted **her 644 letters back to him**.

It is an extraordinary literary problem. A husband speaks, and the reader never hears the sentence that provoked his reply. His complaints or tenderness appear self-contained. The wife's share in making the conversation happen has disappeared. The 2017 book restores the alternating correspondence and includes eleven of Sofia's letters never previously published. The letters themselves remain to be read carefully: their recovery does not prove one party was always kind or always cruel. It establishes that a one-sided archive can make an entire marriage sound like a monologue. [Source: Donskov's edited *Tolstoy and Tolstaya*, 2017](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1wbf841).

The philosopher's wife might be working all the while. Jenny Marx's letter of 27 February 1852 explains that Karl's eyes were sore from laboring into the night and that she had taken up his secretarial correspondence. She was handling publishing arrangements, not merely making life comfortable. Four years later she wrote Engels about politics, manuscripts and her inheritance. Then, in a letter to Karl in March 1860, affection coexists with business. A cruelly selected biography could quote only the hard years; a sentimental one could quote only the fondness. Her dated words require both. [Jenny's 1852 letter](https://www.dhspriory.org/kenny/PhilTexts/Marx/www.marxists.org/archive/marx/letters/jenny/52_02_27.htm); [1856 letter](https://dhspriory.org/kenny/PhilTexts/Marx/www.marxists.org/archive/marx/letters/jenny/56_03_28.htm); [1860 letter](https://dhspriory.org/kenny/PhilTexts/Marx/www.marxists.org/archive/marx/letters/jenny/60_03_16.htm).

In a different household, an astronomer was alleged to have explained exactly how long a wife was wasting: twenty seconds a day. Carl Sagan's son Nick reportedly described a quarrel about breakfast. Linda could put bread in the toaster, then make a shake, rather than complete the tasks in the other order. Multiply the seconds by the years and time accumulates. So, too, would the labor of preparing breakfast in the first place. The son remembered that Linda was furious. We have this account only through a later review quoting Keay Davidson's biography; to report the exact dialogue as established fact, we must read the original printed page. But even as a reported anecdote it points to a question with mathematical clarity: **whose time is counted as precious?** [Source trail, Davidson p. 287 as reproduced by reviewer](https://sheseeksnonfiction.blog/2022/06/05/carl-sagan-a-life-review/).

His former wife Lynn Margulis lost research opportunities to childcare. In her own later interview, she explained that she could not attend a laboratory semester required for her graduate degree; she completed the doctorate after the marriage ended. She did not thereby stop being a scientist. A man's scientific career and his wife's scientific career were not competing abstractions. They were claims on the same day, the same children, the same hours. [Margulis interviewed in *University of Chicago Magazine*, 2004](https://magazine.uchicago.edu/0402/features/speed-print.shtml).

The evidence does not require every family to be unhappy. The edited letters of Emma and Charles Darwin show affection, conflict over belief and deep trust. Charles handed Emma instructions about preserving and publishing his emerging species theory if he died. He expected her to exercise judgment over manuscripts, readers, printers and a substantial publication fund. This was serious responsibility, and work too. The letters tell a history of help and confidence without proving an equal division of free time. [Charles to Emma, 5 July 1844](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/view/letters/DCP-LETT-761).

Harriet Taylor Mill wrote philosophy about marriage herself. In 1851 John Stuart Mill signed a document protesting the husband's legal powers and promising never to use them against her. That was an unusual promise to make explicitly. Its moral merit is a separate question from whether he always kept it. Unlike an after-dinner toast to equality, the signed text gives us a proposition that a life could confirm or refute. [*Collected Works*, vol. XXI, pp. 97–99](https://oll-resources.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/oll3/store/titles/255/Mill_0223-21.html).

And then the historian meets a less comforting kind of document. In 1934 Bertrand Russell filed an affidavit recounting his separation from Dora. In the McMaster archive's detailed description he admits his growing relationship with Patricia Spence while charging Dora with infidelity and speaking of the children and their school. Here at least the famous philosopher has written for an audience other than posterity: the court. A sworn narrative is a valuable first-person source. It is also a motivated one. Dora's answer cannot be supplied by his affidavit. [McMaster BRACERS record 116247](https://bracers.mcmaster.ca/index.php/116247).

These families are not a sample of the world's intellectual households. Their letters are incomplete; the books demand careful reading; motives remain partially hidden. But they already yield a more exact story than “genius is cruel.” **The great man has a work. The people who live with him have work too.** The question is who gets to describe it, who gets credit, who receives a wage, and which person's exhausted hours are understood to matter.

*Reading limitations: the 2017 Tolstoy book and the Davidson biography are not directly read in full; the Russell affidavit is inventoried but its original text is not accessible on the archive webpage. The Jenny, Darwin and Mill passages are read in edited/transcribed primary texts, not manuscript autographs. No invented speech is presented as fact.*
