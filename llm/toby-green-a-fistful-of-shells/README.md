## What the book argues

Green reconstructs West and West-Central African economic and political history from the early Atlantic period into the nineteenth century. The central move is to reject a story in which Africa enters world history only when Europeans conquer it. West African states were already deeply connected to trans-Saharan, Atlantic, Islamic, European, and American worlds through gold, cloth, slaves, shells, diplomacy, religion, art, and state formation.

A crucial part of Green's explanation is **currency and value**. Cowrie shells and nzimbu shells circulated through wide trading networks. Green argues that transformations in Atlantic slavery and global exchange gradually produced widening inequalities and political changes that left African states in a weaker relative position by the nineteenth-century age of European imperial expansion.

The book is organized in two large parts: causes of economic divergence, then political/social consequences. It moves from the Sahel through Senegambia and Sierra Leone, the Gold Coast, the Bights of Benin and Biafra, and Kongo, then into slavery and value, fiscal-military politics, new religious/social formations, transnational African worlds, revolution from below, Islam, and aristocratic crisis.

## Source base

The publisher and reviews make the method unusually explicit. Green does not rely on one national archive or on European written records alone. The book draws on:

- archival research in **nine countries**;
- fieldwork across **eight West African states**;
- written primary sources and letters;
- African and European documentary collections;
- oral history and performance culture;
- local collections;
- archaeology;
- material culture and art;
- currency evidence, especially shell money;
- African literary/oral traditions, explicitly including the **Sunjata epic**.

That mix is one reason to keep the notes and bibliography visible rather than reducing the book to its thesis.

## Source trail: people and books to follow alongside Green

The following are not claimed here as a complete transcription of Green's bibliography. They are a **source-neighbourhood to verify against the book's notes** and are useful because current African-history teaching places Green directly beside them:

- **John K. Thornton**, *Africa and Africans in the Making of the Atlantic World, 1400–1680*; also reviewer of Green.
- **Paul E. Lovejoy**, *Transformations in Slavery* and work on jihad and slavery; discussant for Green's 2019 UCL seminar.
- **Jan S. Hogendorn and Marion Johnson**, *The Shell Money of the Slave Trade* — particularly close to Green's currency argument.
- **Robin Law**, *The Oyo Empire* and work on Atlantic West African political economy.
- **Jan Vansina**, *Paths in the Rainforests* and methodological work on oral tradition and Central African history.
- **Ivor Wilks**, work on Asante and Akan political/economic history.
- **T. C. McCaskie**, work on precolonial Asante.
- **Mariana Candido**, work on Benguela and the Atlantic world.
- **Linda Heywood**, work on Angola and West-Central Africa.
- **Joseph C. Miller**, work on slavery and West-Central Africa.
- **Walter Rodney**, especially the long argument about development/underdevelopment that later teaching explicitly puts into conversation with Green.

An LSE 2026–27 course on West African history assigns Green alongside Campbell/Miers/Miller, Candido, Heywood, Hogendorn/Johnson, Law, McCaskie, Thornton, Vansina, and Wilks: https://www.lse.ac.uk/resources/calendar2026-2027/courseGuides/HY/2026_HY333.htm
