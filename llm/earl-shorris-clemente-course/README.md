# Earl Shorris — the Clemente Course in the Humanities

## Purpose

This is a Blackball institutional-case dossier for Earl Shorris's original Clemente Course in the Humanities and the claims that grew out of it.

The main anchor is Shorris's own contemporaneous account, **"II. As a weapon in the hands of the restless poor,"** published in *Harper's Magazine* in September 1997. Later Bard, National Endowment for the Humanities, Clemente Course, and scholarly sources are used to track what the experiment became. Where later retellings disagree with the 1997 account, the disagreement is preserved rather than normalized away.

Checked 2026-08-31.

## Institutional anchor

- Founder: Earl Shorris.
- First course: 1995–96.
- First site: Roberto Clemente Family Guidance Center, Lower Manhattan, New York City.
- Named for the center, not for Roberto Clemente as a curriculum subject.
- Original aim: test Shorris's claim that serious study of the humanities could give poor adults habits of reflection and participation in public life rather than leaving them trapped in what he called a "surround of force."
- Bard College subsequently supplied academic sponsorship and college credit.

## Origin of the idea

Shorris was researching poverty for the book that became *New American Blues: A Journey Through Poverty to Democracy*. At Bedford Hills Correctional Facility he interviewed Viniece (often rendered Veniece in later institutional pages) Walker. Her answer to his question about poverty redirected his project toward culture and the humanities: she argued that poor people needed access to the "moral life of downtown" — museums, plays, lectures, concerts, and the reflective life associated with them.

Shorris converted that suggestion into a political theory. In his usage, "political" meant participation with other people in family, neighborhood, community, and civic life, not merely voting. His proposed mechanism was:

**continuous external pressures → reactive life → little time or space for reflection → weak participation in public life**

and, experimentally:

**humanities study → reflection and argument → greater ability to negotiate rather than merely react → political agency**

This mechanism is Shorris's theory. The existence and history of the course are factual; the stronger causal claim that humanities education breaks poverty is not established merely by the first cohort's later outcomes.

## Recruitment and selection — first course

Shorris's 1997 account gives unusually concrete recruitment provenance.

His stated initial criteria were:

- age 18–35;
- household income below 150 percent of the Census Bureau's official poverty threshold;
- ability to read a tabloid newspaper;
- stated intention to complete the course.

Recruitment routes included:

- Dr. Jaime Inclán and the Roberto Clemente Family Guidance Center;
- Lynette Lauretig and **The Door**;
- Angel Roman and **Grand Street Settlement**;
- Nancy Mamis-King and **Neighborhood Youth & Family Services** in the South Bronx;
- other social-service contacts around New York City.

Shorris reports roughly fifty candidates for thirty places. His narrative also matters because it shows who was filtered out: some applicants exceeded the income cutoff, some were too young, some could not read at the threshold he had set, and some could not make work schedules fit. He also writes that some people were, in his judgment, "too poor" to sustain participation because immediate pressures consumed too much time and energy. That is an important limitation of the experiment: the course did not sample all poor adults, and it selected for people able and willing to undertake an intensive academic program.

## What students were promised

The first course was not framed as job training. Shorris told recruits they would receive college-level humanities work comparable in intellectual ambition to what first-year students might encounter at elite universities. The course supplied practical supports — books and materials, transit fare, food, and child care — because without those supports many students could not attend.

Classes ran in the evening. Shorris described an October-to-May schedule with a Christmas break. The classroom model was discussion-centered and consciously borrowed from the University of Chicago's Hutchins-era general-education ideal.

## Original faculty

Shorris's September 1997 article identifies the first faculty and their intended areas:

- **Charles Simmons** — novelist, former assistant editor of *The New York Times Book Review*, previously taught at Columbia; poetry.
- **Grace Glueck** — *New York Times* art critic; art history.
- **Timothy Koranda** — graduate work at MIT and publications in mathematical logic; logic.
- **Earl Shorris** — American history through primary documents and political/moral philosophy.
- **Dr. Jaime Inclán**, assisted by Patricia Vargas — program evaluation rather than one of the core humanities strands.

Shorris also advertised rhetoric as part of the program, but the surviving *Harper's* narrative does not supply a comparably complete stand-alone rhetoric syllabus. Do not silently invent one.

## Reconstructable first-course curriculum

The following is not a modern Clemente syllabus. It is the material explicitly named in Shorris's own account of the original experiment.

### Moral and political philosophy

- Plato, *Republic* — Allegory of the Cave;
- Plato, *Apology*;
- Plato, *Crito* — selections;
- Plato, *Phaedo* — selections;
- Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*;
- Thucydides, Pericles' Funeral Oration from *History of the Peloponnesian War*;
- Sophocles, *Antigone*, treated as moral and political philosophy as well as drama.

Shorris says the philosophy sessions used maieutic/Socratic dialogue rather than lectures.

### American history

Shorris says he taught history through documents, beginning with:

- Magna Carta;
- John Locke, the second of the *Two Treatises of Government*;
- Declaration of Independence;
- documents continuing through the Civil War.

His narrative also records a class discussion of Gordon S. Wood's *The Radicalism of the American Revolution*. That proves the book entered at least one discussion; it does not by itself establish a full assigned-reading list.

### Poetry / literature

Simmons planned a sequence beginning with relatively accessible short poems, including Housman, and moving toward Latin poetry. Shorris describes sessions using poems as puzzles, jokes, love poems, seduction poems, and later satirical responses. The *Harper's* article does not provide a complete title-by-title literature syllabus.

### Art history

Glueck's sequence began with cave painting and was intended to reach the late twentieth century. The article specifically records:

- Lascaux/cave painting;
- Egyptian art;
- a Metropolitan Museum of Art visit including the Temple of Dendur and Egyptian galleries;
- Mali and Benin;
- Pacific Islands art.

### Logic

Koranda taught mathematical/logical reasoning through problems and symbolic work at the blackboard. The article documents the strand but does not preserve enough detail to reconstruct a complete sequence of readings.

### Orientation timeline — context, not necessarily assigned reading

Shorris says he drew a cultural timeline from myth and *Gilgamesh* through the Old Testament, Confucius, Greek texts, the New Testament, the Koran, the Epic of Son-Jara, and Nahuatl and Maya poetry. This should be indexed as classroom context; the article does not establish that every item on that timeline was assigned in full.

## First-course outcomes and disagreement ledger

Shorris's *Harper's* account says:

- 30 students were admitted;
- 16 completed the course;
- 14 earned Bard College credit;
- a year after graduation, 10 of the 16 graduates were in four-year college or nursing school;
- four had full scholarships to Bard;
- the remaining graduates were in community college or working full time except one person fired from a fast-food job after trying to organize a union.

The same article says the second year enrolled 28 and graduated 14.

Other retellings give slightly different first-cohort counts. *Publishers Weekly*'s 1997 review of *New American Blues* says 31 began and 17 completed. Later newspaper and institutional accounts also often use 31/17. Blackball should retain both figures and privilege the September 1997 first-person account when describing what Shorris himself reported at that time.

Shorris also reports pre/post questionnaire results developed by Inclán: increased self-esteem and problem-solving measures and decreased verbal aggression. In the sources checked here, this appears as Shorris's report of an internal evaluation, not as an independently replicated causal study. It should not be upgraded into proof that the humanities caused the later education or employment outcomes.

https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780393045543

## Publication context

The Clemente experiment and Shorris's writing about poverty developed together:

1. **1995–96:** first Clemente Course in Lower Manhattan.
2. **September 1997:** *Harper's* publishes Shorris's "As a weapon in the hands of the restless poor" as part II of the magazine package **"On the Uses of a Liberal Education."** It tells the story of the experiment's origin, recruitment, teaching, and first two years.
3. **1997:** W. W. Norton publishes *New American Blues: A Journey Through Poverty to Democracy*, the larger poverty study from which the Clemente experiment emerged. *Publishers Weekly* identifies *Harper's* as one of the book's first-serial outlets.
4. **2000:** Norton publishes the revised paperback as *Riches for the Poor: The Clemente Course in the Humanities*, explicitly refocusing the book around the Clemente project.
5. **2013:** Norton's posthumous *The Art of Freedom: Teaching the Humanities to the Poor* returns to the project and its later development.

## Later institutionalization

Bard College became the principal academic sponsor and later described the program as a free, multi-strand humanities course for adults facing economic hardship. The current Bard description specifies more than 100 instructional hours in literature, art history, moral philosophy, U.S. history, and critical thinking/writing.

A 1999 Bard press release already described a program expansion under Martin Kempner and Bard, with goals of bridging low-income students into higher education and supporting fuller economic and political participation.

Bard 1999 release:
https://www.bard.edu/news/releases/pr/fstory.php?id=8792

Current Bard curriculum description:

A 2025 *Public Humanities* article describes the later standard form as a five-strand, six-credit, eight-month night course with more than 100 contact hours, and notes that tuition, books, transportation, and child care are provided. This is useful evidence about the mature program, not a substitute for the 1995 syllabus.

## Blackball assessment

The Clemente Course is useful for Blackball because it makes several normally vague claims testable.

- It separates **academic content** from **credentialing**: the first course was built before credit was guaranteed, and Bard credit arrived as institutional sponsorship developed.
- It separates **humanities instruction** from **attendance infrastructure**: transit, food, books, child care, scheduling, and recruitment were part of the intervention.
- It makes selection visible. Participants were screened for age, income, reading ability, willingness, and practical ability to attend.
- It exposes a causal-identification problem. Positive later outcomes among completers cannot by themselves distinguish the effects of the humanities from selection, support services, faculty attention, Bard affiliation, or the ambitions of people who persisted.
- It also supplies a concrete counterexample to the claim that canonical humanities material was historically reserved only for already-privileged students: Shorris deliberately used elite-college material with poor adults and rejected a remedialized curriculum.

The strong claim to test later is not "Plato cures poverty." It is whether sustained access to high-status intellectual material, discussion, logistical support, and institutional recognition changes educational participation, civic behavior, or other measurable outcomes relative to plausible alternatives.
