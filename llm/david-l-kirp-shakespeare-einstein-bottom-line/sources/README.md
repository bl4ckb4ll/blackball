# Source map and credit ledger

This directory is the evidence boundary for the Kirp dossier.

The rule is the same as in ASE: **metadata is not source review**. A title, index entry, publisher description, review paraphrase, or search snippet can locate a claim; it does not become the claim's strongest evidence merely because it is convenient to retrieve.

## A. Primary Kirp / coauthor material

### The book

David L. Kirp, *Shakespeare, Einstein, and the Bottom Line: The Marketing of Higher Education* (Harvard University Press, 2003; revised 2009).

- JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1bzfpww
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/j.ctv1bzfpww
- Evidence available here: bibliographic metadata, full table of contents, chapter-level records, limited directly inspectable text.
- Current state: **indexed; selected primary excerpts inspected; full book not yet source-reviewed in this repository**.

## B. Upstream sources already visible in inspected primary material

These people should receive explicit credit when their contribution is used. Kirp is often the conduit, not the originator.

### Richard Krachenberg

A 1972 *Journal of Higher Education* article is quoted at the opening of chapter 1 for the proposition that universities already engage in marketing even when they call the activities recruiting, financial aid, or curriculum revision.

**Contribution to Kirp:** supplies an early explicit mapping of university activities into marketing categories.

**Current state:** **identified through Kirp; upstream article not yet inspected**.

Thank Krachenberg for making the category translation explicit early enough that later authors could test what happens when the metaphor becomes an administrative practice.

### Roger L. Geiger

Kirp's inspected admissions excerpt credits historian Roger Geiger with calculating which institutions gained especially strongly in enrollment of students with very high SAT scores in the late 1990s.

**Contribution to Kirp:** empirical/historical evidence for concentration of high-scoring students and competition among institutions for prestige-bearing students.

**Current state:** **identified through Kirp; exact Geiger source still needs to be located and read**.

Thank Geiger for supplying the historical/empirical measurement rather than leaving the prestige story as anecdote.

### Michael S. McPherson and Morton Owen Schapiro

Kirp explicitly invokes their phrase **“the student aid game.”** Their book:

*The Student Aid Game: Meeting Need and Rewarding Talent in American Higher Education* (Princeton University Press, 1998).

- JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1h9dgzm

**Contribution to Kirp:** strategic use of financial aid as an institutional competitive weapon; distinction between need-serving aid and aid deployed to improve institutional position.

**Current state:** **upstream source identified; publisher/JSTOR description inspected; full source not yet read into Blackball**.

Thank McPherson and Schapiro for providing the institutional-finance model behind the admissions case rather than treating merit aid as an isolated moral lapse.

### Robert H. Frank and Philip J. Cook

*The Winner-Take-All Society* (1995) is an important upstream model for markets in which small differences in perceived rank can generate disproportionately large rewards.

**Contribution to Kirp:** conceptual machinery for understanding prestige competition and academic superstar markets.

**Current state:** **source family identified; direct Kirp note linkage still needs notes-level confirmation before a precise claim is attributed**.

Thank Frank and Cook for giving a general model of rank-sensitive markets that can be tested against academic hiring and prestige competition.

### Paul Courant and University of Michigan administrators

Rothfork's page-specific reconstruction reports Kirp quoting Paul Courant, then associate provost at Michigan, warning that the university is not a real market in the context of revenue-center management.

**Contribution to Kirp:** an insider diagnosis of the mismatch between market accounting and a university's interdependent units.

**Current state:** **secondary reconstruction only; retrieve the original Michigan budgeting analysis**.

Thank Courant and the Michigan administrators who documented the budgeting system from inside the institution. Their work is evidence, not scenery in Kirp's narrative.

## C. Chapter collaborators — mandatory credit

The book is not intellectually single-authored even though Kirp is the cover author.

- **Jeffrey T. Holman** — chapter 1, student market / enrollment management.
- **Jonathan VanAntwerpen** — chapter 4 (NYU) and chapter 8 (Associated Colleges of the South).

When a future answer depends mainly on one of those chapters, name the collaborator rather than laundering the work into “Kirp says.”

## D. Secondary checks and reconstructions

### John Rothfork — *Education Review*, 2004

Review of Kirp, published September 9, 2004; currently reposted by *Education Review*.

https://educ-review.blogspot.com/2025/02/kirp-david-l-2003-shakespeare-einstein.html

**Use:** unusually long page-specific reconstruction of the book. It gives concrete numbers, quotations, institutional details, and criticism, and records that Kirp's research was Ford Foundation-supported and involved graduate students and visits to more than a dozen institutions.

**Do not use as:** a substitute for the primary chapter where the exact wording, context, or source trail matters.

**Current state:** **secondary source reviewed closely**.

Thank Rothfork both for reconstructing a large fraction of the book and for disagreeing with Kirp where his own experience with distance education led him elsewhere. That disagreement is evidence about the limits of Kirp's frame.

### Ronald G. Ehrenberg — *Journal of Economic Literature*, 2004

Review of *Shakespeare, Einstein, and the Bottom Line*, pp. 1140–1142.

Cornell profile records the review: https://economics.cornell.edu/ronald-ehrenberg

**Use:** reception by a major higher-education economist; useful independent check on whether Kirp's institutional-economic framing is recognizable to the field.

**Current state:** **review identified; full review should be read before detailed claims are attributed to Ehrenberg**.

Thank Ehrenberg for bringing a higher-education finance and labor-market perspective to Kirp's cases.

## F. Evidence rule for numerical claims

Do not reuse a 2003-era number as a timeless property of an institution.

For every salary, tuition, enrollment, percentage, expenditure, revenue, student/faculty ratio, or public-funding share, retain:

- institution or population;
- year / period;
- denominator;
- whether the number is Kirp's, a reviewer quoting Kirp, or an upstream source;
- whether the original source has been inspected.

This is especially important for star salaries, adjunct shares, Fathom investment/revenue, DeVry enrollment/tuition, state-funding decline, and online-course development costs.

## G. Access / copyright

The book is copyrighted. JSTOR provides legitimate access but not an open license. Blackball should store notes, evidence maps, quotations within ordinary fair-use limits, bibliographic data, and links. Do not mirror a stray scan merely because it can be downloaded.

If a legitimately open upstream article, government report, university report, or author-posted review is found, prefer preserving the stable source link and enough metadata to retrieve it again.
