# Institutional cases

This file is a mechanism index for a future model. It is not a chapter-summary substitute. Each case records what the institution is doing, what question Kirp uses it to expose, and the present evidence state.

## 1. Student market / enrollment management

**Institutions and actors:** multiple selective colleges; Brown appears prominently in inspected material.  
**Chapter:** 1, “This Little Student Went to Market,” David L. Kirp and Jeffrey T. Holman.  
**Mechanism:** recruiting → advertising; financial aid → strategic pricing; curriculum/identity → product design; yield/selectivity/rankings → competitive targets.  
**Question:** when aid and admissions are optimized for institutional position, whose need is the aid system serving?  
**Evidence state:** **primary excerpt inspected**.

Directly inspected text identifies Richard Krachenberg's 1972 higher-education marketing argument as an important precursor and later connects the admissions competition to Roger L. Geiger and to Michael S. McPherson and Morton Owen Schapiro.

## 2. University of Chicago

**Chapter:** 2, “Nietzsche's Niche.”  
**Mechanism:** financial pressure versus a distinctive academic identity; expanding undergraduate enrollment as a response to deficits; faculty resistance to managerial change.  
**Question:** can a university respond to financial constraint without turning the intellectual distinctiveness that makes it valuable into merely a brand?  
**Evidence state:** **secondary reconstruction** through Rothfork; full chapter and notes not yet inspected.

## 3. Dickinson College

**Chapter:** 3, “Benjamin Rush's ‘Brat.’”  
**Mechanism:** a small college's dependence on attracting academically desirable students and articulating a marketable identity.  
**Question:** how much of institutional “quality” is downstream of the students an institution is able to attract, and how much of that attraction is itself produced by marketing?  
**Evidence state:** **secondary reconstruction**.

## 4. New York University philosophy / academic star market

**Chapter:** 4, “Star Wars,” Jonathan VanAntwerpen and David L. Kirp.  
**Mechanism:** buying celebrated faculty to gain prestige while much undergraduate teaching is done by lower-paid contingent labor.  
**Question:** what happens to the idea of an academic community when reputational capital and instructional labor are purchased on radically different labor markets?  
**Evidence state:** **secondary reconstruction**.

Do not generalize salary numbers, adjunct shares, or labor conditions beyond the place and period until the primary chapter and underlying data are checked.

## 5. New York Law School

**Chapter:** 5, “The Dead Hand of Precedent.”  
**Mechanism:** organizational reform under inherited professional and academic rules.  
**Question:** which university practices are genuine educational commitments and which persist simply because institutions are difficult to change?  
**Evidence state:** **indexed**. The earlier summary was too confident relative to currently inspected evidence; full chapter review is required.

## 6. USC and the University of Michigan — revenue-center management

**Chapter:** 6, “Kafka Was an Optimist.”  
**Mechanism:** allocate revenues/costs to schools or units and make each responsible for its financial result.  
**Question:** what false incentives appear when interdependent academic units are made to behave as though they are independent sellers?  
**Reported cases:** units chase enrollments; teaching crosses ordinary disciplinary boundaries to capture revenue; central institutional purpose becomes harder to enforce.  
**Evidence state:** **strong secondary reconstruction** with page-specific quotations in Rothfork.

A crucial upstream witness is **Paul Courant**, then associate provost at Michigan, quoted warning that the university is not a real market. The next pass should retrieve Courant/Michigan's own budgeting analysis and USC's RCM documentation rather than leave Kirp as the sole intermediary.

## 7. Darden / University of Virginia

**Chapter:** 7, “Mr. Jefferson's ‘Private’ College,” David L. Kirp and Patrick S. Roberts.  
**Mechanism:** reduced public subsidy → self-financing pressure → high-priced professional programs and custom executive education → stronger client leverage over what is taught or held proprietary.  
**Question:** when a public university unit can survive by selling directly to wealthy clients, what becomes of cross-subsidy and public mission?  
**Evidence state:** **secondary reconstruction**.

## 8. Associated Colleges of the South classics collaboration

**Chapter:** 8, “Rebel Alliance,” Jonathan VanAntwerpen and David L. Kirp.  
**Mechanism:** institutional cooperation among small classics programs.  
**Question:** can shared infrastructure preserve fields that would look nonviable under a narrow campus-by-campus enrollment test?  
**Evidence state:** **indexed / partial secondary reconstruction**. Do not present detailed claims until the chapter is inspected.

This is an important counterexample to a simple “marketization destroys everything” story: Kirp also looks for organizational devices that let institutions protect low-volume academic goods.

## 9. Columbia Fathom and MIT OpenCourseWare

**Chapter:** 9, “The Market in Ideas.”  
**Mechanism:** commercial enclosure of online educational material versus open distribution.  
**Question:** is the highest-value institutional strategy necessarily the one that charges directly for access?  
**Reported contrast:** Columbia's Fathom consumed large investment and generated little direct revenue; MIT chose open course materials partly as public service and partly as reputational strategy.  
**Evidence state:** **secondary reconstruction**.

Next source pass: retrieve Columbia's contemporary Fathom records and MIT's original OpenCourseWare launch materials.

## 10. Open University in the United States

**Chapter:** 10, “The British Are Coming—and Going.”  
**Mechanism:** transplanting a mature British distance-education institution into a different American market and institutional ecology.  
**Question:** does a model that works educationally in one national system survive when the surrounding subsidy, alternatives, geography, credentials, and student expectations change?  
**Evidence state:** **secondary reconstruction; contested**.

John Rothfork strongly disputes Kirp's interpretation and argues that Kirp underestimates the already-developed American distance-education market and overstates the superiority/transplantability of the British model. Preserve that disagreement.

## 11. UC Berkeley / industry research

**Chapter:** 11, “A Good Deal of Collaboration,” David L. Kirp and Elizabeth Popp Berman.  
**Mechanism:** university-industry research centers, sponsorship, proprietary databases, confidentiality, patents, and publication.  
**Question:** under what contract terms does outside research money begin to change the circulation of scientific knowledge or the university's research agenda?  
**Evidence state:** **primary opening inspected + secondary reconstruction**.

A prominent reported arrangement is the Novartis-Berkeley relationship. Do not reduce it to a corruption slogan; retrieve the actual agreement, oversight reports, publication terms, and later scholarship.

## 12. Silicon Valley IT certification

**Chapter:** 12, “The Information Technology Gold Rush,” David L. Kirp, Robert Ness, and Debra Solomon.  
**Mechanism:** short-cycle occupational training and vendor certifications competing with slower university programs.  
**Question:** where does a university add value when employers and vendors can define a credential directly, and where does institutional slowness make universities a poor fit?  
**Evidence state:** **secondary reconstruction**.

This case is useful precisely because it shows a domain where rapid market feedback may carry real information. Do not treat every vocational response as evidence of educational decline.

## 13. DeVry

**Chapter:** 13, “They're All Business.”  
**Mechanism:** centralized curriculum, explicit career orientation, placement promises, customer-service management, and managerial supervision of faculty.  
**Question:** what does the university become when occupational return and customer satisfaction are the dominant objectives?  
**Evidence state:** **secondary reconstruction**.

Kirp uses DeVry as a limiting case but also acknowledges that conventional universities may have something to learn from its responsiveness. Preserve both halves.

## Cross-case mechanisms

A future model should cluster questions by mechanism rather than merely by institution:

- **prestige feedback:** admissions, rankings, star faculty, philanthropy;
- **price/aid strategy:** merit aid, yield management, tuition discounting;
- **labor segmentation:** stars, tenure-track faculty, graduate workers, adjuncts;
- **internal transfer pricing:** revenue-center management and unit competition;
- **public subsidy withdrawal:** self-financing and professional-school autonomy;
- **ownership of knowledge:** patents, confidentiality, proprietary course/research material;
- **open distribution:** OpenCourseWare and other commons strategies;
- **credential markets:** vendor certification, career programs, for-profit schooling;
- **institutional mission:** which activities are protected even when no unit can capture their full value.
