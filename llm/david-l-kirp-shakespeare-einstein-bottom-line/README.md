# David L. Kirp — *Shakespeare, Einstein, and the Bottom Line*

## Purpose

This is an LLM-facing source dossier, not a syllabus entry.

Use it to answer questions about the commercialization, marketization, prestige economy, internal budgeting, labor structure, research ownership, distance education, and institutional governance of American higher education as Kirp describes them.

**claim → mechanism → institutional case → inspected evidence → upstream source → disagreement / limitation**

## Evidence states

Borrowed deliberately from the ASE source-review convention:

- **indexed** — title, table of contents, bibliographic metadata, index entry, or other discovery metadata only.
- **primary excerpt inspected** — text written by Kirp/coauthor has been read directly, but not necessarily the whole chapter.
- **secondary reconstruction** — a review or scholarly discussion gives page-specific detail about Kirp's argument or evidence.
- **chapter inspected** — the complete chapter has been read closely.
- **notes inspected** — the chapter's notes/citations have been read and mapped.
- **upstream source inspected** — a source Kirp relies on has itself been retrieved and read.
- **corroborated** — the claim has been independently checked against another strong source.
- **contested** — a serious source gives a materially different interpretation or exposes a limitation.

Do not silently upgrade one state into another.

## Working argument map

### 1. Universities are not merely spending money; market categories can become governing categories

Kirp's central problem is not that universities have budgets. It is that categories such as customer, product, price, brand, profit center, proprietary asset, competitive advantage, and return can become rules for deciding what education and research should be.

This matters because universities also claim to produce goods whose value is collective, delayed, difficult to price, or deliberately open: basic research, scholarly communities, broad education, preservation of fields, public service, and knowledge that others may use without paying the producer.

**State:** primary/secondary support; thesis strongly supported by JSTOR description, introduction material, chapter 1, and Rothfork.

### 2. Prestige is itself an economic mechanism

At selective institutions, Kirp treats prestige not as decorative reputation but as a resource that affects applications, yield, student credentials, faculty recruitment, gifts, and future reputation.

A key feedback loop is:

**prestige → stronger applicants / celebrated faculty / gifts → measured institutional strength → more prestige**

The competition can cause aid, admissions, branding, and curricular choices to be optimized for position rather than educational need.

**State:** primary excerpt inspected for admissions/prestige; upstream source work partly identified.

### 3. Student demand is not an exogenous fact

Kirp and Holman describe enrollment management as a system that segments applicants, changes aid offers, manages yield, markets identity, and alters the product being sold. What later appears as “student demand” is partly produced by institutional advertising, rankings, aid strategy, admissions policy, and available curricula.

**State:** primary excerpt inspected.

### 4. Academic labor can split into a prestige market above and a contingent teaching market below

The NYU case links expensive star hiring to the institutional use of graduate students, adjuncts, and other contingent teachers. The point is structural: the university may pay heavily for reputational capital while purchasing much routine instruction on a low-cost labor market.

**State:** secondary reconstruction; chapter 4 still needs full inspection.

### 5. Internal budgeting can create artificial markets inside one cooperating institution

The USC / University of Michigan chapter is crucial. Revenue-center management assigns units revenue and costs in ways intended to make them financially responsible. Kirp's warning is that a university is not simply a shopping mall of independent firms: departments teach one another's students, maintain prerequisites and shared intellectual infrastructure, and produce benefits that do not stay inside accounting boundaries.

Reported failure modes include units chasing enrollments, duplicating teaching that another department ordinarily provides, and designing courses to capture tuition revenue.

**State:** strong secondary reconstruction with page references; upstream Michigan/USC material still to be followed.

### 6. Withdrawal of public subsidy changes what schools have to sell

The Darden case links declining state support to pressure for self-financing. Professional schools able to charge high tuition or sell executive education have more routes to autonomy than fields without wealthy clients. That can change who has leverage over curriculum, research, confidentiality, and institutional priorities.

**State:** secondary reconstruction.

### 7. Knowledge can be treated either as a commodity to enclose or as a commons to circulate

Kirp contrasts attempts to sell online educational content with MIT OpenCourseWare's decision to release course material openly. The relevant variable is not simply “free versus paid”: open distribution can create public value and reputational value simultaneously, while commercial enclosure can fail even by ordinary market tests.

**State:** secondary reconstruction; MIT/Columbia primary institutional sources should be followed.

### 8. Corporate research support can create a conflict between secrecy and academic publication

The UC Berkeley / industry case asks what happens when sponsored research, proprietary databases, patents, or confidentiality agreements enter a setting whose scientific norm has been circulation and publication.

Do not simplify this to “industry money corrupts research.” Kirp uses concrete arrangements and asks where contractual control begins to alter the university's public role.

**State:** chapter 11 opening directly inspected; rest secondary reconstruction.

### 9. For-profit higher education exposes the customer model in unusually explicit form

DeVry supplies a limiting case: standardized curricula, centralized managerial control, career placement, and students understood openly as customers seeking occupational return. Kirp does not treat responsiveness to students or careers as automatically bad; the case clarifies what disappears when occupational service becomes the institution's dominant purpose.

**State:** secondary reconstruction.

## Do not flatten Kirp into a slogan

Avoid these reductions:

- “Kirp says markets are bad.” He repeatedly recognizes useful entrepreneurship and financial constraint.
- “Universities used to be outside markets.” They were never moneyless institutions.
- “Students are just customers.” Kirp is analyzing what changes when that metaphor governs decisions.
- “Prestige is fake.” Prestige has real institutional effects even when rankings and branding help manufacture it.
- “RCM is just capitalism.” The important issue is the construction of internal price signals and unit boundaries inside an organization whose activities are interdependent.
- “Corporate research is automatically invalid.” The dossier should identify the actual contract, ownership, secrecy, publication, and incentive mechanism.
