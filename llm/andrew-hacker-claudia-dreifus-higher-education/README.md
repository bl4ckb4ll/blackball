# Andrew Hacker and Claudia Dreifus — *Higher Education?*

## Purpose

This is an LLM-facing source dossier for Andrew Hacker and Claudia Dreifus, *Higher Education?: How Colleges Are Wasting Our Money and Failing Our Kids—and What We Can Do About It* (2010).

The book is unusually useful to Blackball because it combines three things that should not be collapsed into one evidence category:

1. published statistics and earlier scholarship;
2. reporting based on campus visits, classroom observation, interviews, and conversations;
3. the authors' own judgments about what undergraduate education ought to be.

Use it as a map of claims, institutional cases, observations, upstream sources, and hypotheses to test. Do not turn vivid reportage into a representative survey merely because the authors traveled widely.

## Research method: reporting as well as reading

The user's recollection is substantially right: travel and observation were a major part of the project.

Hacker and Dreifus later wrote that they spent **three years researching the book**. Their publisher describes the project as a road trip through American higher education. The opening of chapter 1 repeatedly refers to what they encountered “in our travels.” A contemporary academic reviewer describes their evidence, beyond scholarly reading, as visits to a large and varied set of universities and conversations with professors around the country.

Hacker himself explained the division of labor in especially useful terms: Dreifus proposed the project; he agreed to coauthor it because she was an experienced journalist who would keep the book focused on actual people rather than only academic abstractions. Dreifus's career supports that description: interviewing and reported observation were central to her journalism long before this book.

### What that method can establish

A firsthand visit can strongly establish a bounded claim such as:

> Hacker and Dreifus observed this practice, classroom, administrative arrangement, or conversation at institution X during their reporting period.

It cannot by itself establish:

> This is typical of American higher education.

The dossier should preserve the distinction between **case evidence** and **prevalence evidence**.

## Working argument map

The book's central argument is not simply “college is too expensive.” Hacker and Dreifus argue that undergraduate education has ceased to be the organizing purpose of many institutions. Research prestige, administrative expansion, professional training, athletics, institutional branding, and the accumulation of facilities and programs can all become ends in themselves.

Their positive ideal is broad undergraduate education: students should encounter unfamiliar ideas, arguments, disciplines, and people rather than treating ages eighteen to twenty-two primarily as occupational certification.

This creates a recurring test throughout the book:

**Does this institution organize its money, faculty time, curriculum, and status system around educating undergraduates, or around something else?**

That question is useful even where the authors' proposed answer is weak.

# Chapter-by-chapter dossier

## Introduction — “Higher Education?”

**State:** primary excerpt inspected.

The introduction defines the book's target as the undergraduate years. Hacker and Dreifus distinguish **education** from **training** and reject the idea that adolescents must begin narrow career preparation immediately. They point to the long decline in the proportion of degrees awarded in fields such as English, history, and mathematics while vocationally oriented programs grow.

Their normative premise is expansive: college should enlarge a student's intellectual world rather than merely certify job skills. They explicitly refuse to blame students first. Adults who have made higher education their profession have the greater responsibility to construct environments in which intellectual curiosity can develop.

The introduction also establishes an important methodological feature: they claim to have sat in classrooms and watched unusually effective teachers. Their case is therefore partly comparative reportage — what teaching looked like where they thought it worked, versus the structures they criticize elsewhere.

**Questions to verify upstream:** exact federal degree-series tables; Diane Ravitch source for the job-training criticism; institutions/classes directly observed for the introductory positive examples.

## 1. The World of the Professoriate

**State:** primary excerpt inspected; remainder partly secondary reconstruction.

The chapter begins with a Queens College faculty search. A research-oriented candidate impresses on conventional academic credentials but appears uninterested in the actual students or institution that would employ him. The episode introduces the chapter's mechanism: universities say they hire professors to educate students, while the professional labor market rewards research reputation and disciplinary standing.

Hacker and Dreifus then use observations from academic social events, faculty conversations, sabbaticals, publication expectations, and faculty absences to argue that professors are socialized to think of themselves primarily as disciplinary researchers rather than teachers attached to a particular body of undergraduates.

Hacker later summarized this chapter by pointing to the frequency of sabbaticals at institutions such as Williams and to the enormous production of scholarship on already heavily studied writers such as William Faulkner. Their inference is not merely that research exists, but that **publication volume and professional prestige can become institutional goals detached from educational value**.

This is one of the book's most contestable moves. Counting publications cannot establish that the work is worthless, and determining the social value of research requires much more than noting its abundance. Preserve the stronger structural claim — incentives privilege research — separately from the weaker value judgment — much of the resulting research is unnecessary.

## 2. Administrative Overload

**State:** secondary reconstruction.

The chapter argues that the number and variety of nonfaculty positions expanded much faster than enrollment. A contemporary review reports their comparison of roughly 32 nonfaculty employees per 1,000 students in 1976 with roughly 63 in 2007, alongside the multiplication of specialized administrative titles.

Their mechanism is **organizational accretion**: once a university creates offices and functions, those units develop constituencies, budgets, reporting structures, and reasons to continue. Presidents increasingly become fundraisers and organizational executives rather than academic leaders. Administrative payroll and executive compensation then become part of the cost structure students support.

The exact national staffing series needs to be traced to its upstream owner before reuse. Descriptive growth in administrators also does not by itself prove how much of tuition inflation it caused. Some administrative growth reflects legal compliance, disability services, financial aid, technology, mental-health services, research administration, and other functions that require separate evaluation.

The chapter is still a valuable retrieval map: identify each administrative category, why it appeared, its headcount and compensation trajectory, and whether it changes undergraduate outcomes.

## 3. Contingent Education

**State:** substantial primary excerpt inspected.

The chapter opens with Dreifus's own experience teaching as an adjunct without office space. When she asks for a private place to meet students, an administrator suggests public/common space, making the institutional hierarchy between “regular” faculty and contingents concrete.

Hacker and Dreifus argue that adjuncts and other contingent instructors perform a growing share of undergraduate teaching while receiving less pay, security, office space, and institutional standing. The *Scientific American* excerpt gives extreme institutional snapshots: Florida Keys Community College with 18 permanent professors and 123 adjuncts, and Moberly College with only a handful of professors alongside hundreds of adjuncts/instructors. They also report cases in which advanced undergraduates supervise sections at Penn/Wharton and discuss similar possibilities at Yale and NYU.

Their important structural point is that contingent labor is not merely a cheap-input story. **Research prestige and reduced teaching expectations for permanent faculty create teaching work that someone else must perform.** Cheap adjunct labor then makes the prestige structure financially sustainable.

This chapter should be connected to independent IPEDS/AAUP faculty-status series and institution-level payroll records. Its extreme examples should not be treated as national averages.

## 4. The Golden Dozen

**State:** secondary reconstruction, with a strong primary precursor in Hacker's 2005 NYRB essay.

The “Golden Dozen” are the eight Ivy League schools plus Stanford, Duke, Amherst, and Williams. Hacker and Dreifus ask what families are actually buying when they compete for these brands and whether selectivity and reputation imply superior undergraduate education.

They examine admissions, price, elite professional-school pipelines, and later careers. Their best-known exercise follows Princeton's class of 1973 and looks for evidence of nationally prominent careers, including *Who's Who in America*. They conclude that far fewer graduates become nationally distinctive figures than elite rhetoric might lead families to expect.

Peter Brooks attacks this as weak outcome measurement, and he has a point: “distinctive life” is not well operationalized, and *Who's Who* is a crude sampling frame. Preserve the exercise as evidence about **how Hacker and Dreifus tried to test prestige claims**, not as a definitive estimate of elite-college value.

A stronger line of evidence is Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger's work comparing students who attended highly selective institutions with students who were admitted to such institutions but attended elsewhere. Hacker had already cited this research in 2005 because it directly attacks selection bias: strong students may prosper because they were strong applicants, not because the brand transformed them.

## 5. Teaching: Good, Great, Abysmal

**State:** secondary reconstruction, supported by Hacker's 2005 primary precursor.

This chapter challenges the standard claim that research automatically improves teaching. Hacker and Dreifus compare classroom environments at high-spending research universities with less famous institutions and argue that undergraduate students often receive little benefit from the research prestige surrounding them.

They use student-reported material including the National Survey of Student Engagement and Princeton Review teaching ratings. Their recurring observation is that smaller, less research-dominant institutions often score better on faculty helpfulness, availability, and classroom engagement than large research universities.

They also discuss practical teaching behavior: course design, assignments, seminar interaction, presentation technology, and whether professors can tell when students are disengaged.

The source discipline here matters. Student satisfaction is not identical to learning, and neither is professor popularity. Conversely, publication counts and grants are not teaching measures. A future upstream pass should separate:

- student-reported availability;
- observed teaching practice;
- measured learning;
- research output;
- institutional incentives.

## 6. The Triumph of Training

**State:** introduction partly primary; chapter itself secondary reconstruction.

The chapter expands the introduction's distinction between education and occupational training. Hacker and Dreifus document the long shift in degree fields toward business, health, and other career-oriented programs and away from many liberal-arts disciplines.

Their criticism is unusually broad. They do not merely object to weak vocational programs; they question whether undergraduate business and some engineering education belong in the first degree at all. Their preferred model gives students broad intellectual formation first and leaves more specialized professional preparation for later.

This is both one of the book's clearest values and one of its largest blind spots. Students with little family wealth may reasonably demand that an expensive degree improve their labor-market position quickly. Blackball should therefore preserve two questions separately:

1. Does vocationalization displace broad education?
2. Given the actual labor market and cost of college, can students afford the authors' preferred sequence?

The chapter reportedly uses engineering retention/teaching examples and degree-field statistics; exact upstream citations need the printed bibliography.

## 7. Why College Costs So Much

**State:** secondary reconstruction plus primary author summaries.

Hacker and Dreifus argue that real tuition rose dramatically because institutions continually add costs while competing for prestige. Their candidate mechanisms include administrative growth, higher compensation at the top of faculty and executive hierarchies, amenities, athletics, research infrastructure, and the tendency of institutions to charge what families and the loan system will support.

The chapter treats price itself as part of the prestige system: institutions are not ordinary low-price competitors, because a high sticker price can signal status while aid allows differentiated effective prices.

Their practical recommendation is much more concrete than the rhetoric: families should compare actual undergraduate teaching and debt burden rather than assume the expensive brand is worth the difference. Public honors programs, regional public campuses, and lower-cost schools can be rational alternatives.

Do not treat their list of expensive inputs as a complete causal decomposition of tuition inflation. Alternative higher-education cost theories — labor intensity/Baumol effects, declining state appropriations, expanded student services, regulation, and changes in aid — require independent testing.

## 8. Fireproof: The Tangled Issue of Tenure

**State:** secondary reconstruction.

Hacker and Dreifus treat tenure as a caste-like division between protected senior academics and insecure junior or contingent labor. They question whether tenure is either necessary or sufficient for academic freedom and use historical and contemporary cases to argue that supposedly protected institutions can still punish controversial speech while the tenure process also rewards conformity to disciplinary seniority.

They report low faculty mobility at some colleges and interviews suggesting that junior academics learn to avoid antagonizing the people who will vote on their careers.

Their proposed alternative is renewable multi-year contracts, commonly described as roughly five to seven years, rather than permanent appointment. They point to institutions such as Hampshire and Evergreen as possible alternatives.

This is an argument where case evidence is especially insufficient for causal claims. A few failures of tenure protection do not show that tenure has no protective effect. A serious follow-up needs comparative evidence about dismissals, speech, research independence, contract renewal, and faculty governance under different employment systems.

## 9. The Athletics Incubus

**State:** secondary reconstruction plus primary author summary; chapter later republished separately.

The chapter argues that intercollegiate athletics consume money, management attention, admissions preferences, and institutional identity while contributing little to undergraduate education. Hacker later summarized the case with figures showing that only a small minority of football programs covered their costs and with striking per-athlete spending examples in nonrevenue sports.

They reject the defense that sports “pay for themselves” merely because a handful of nationally visible programs generate revenue. Their broader target includes the large number of varsity teams whose expenses are effectively subsidized by the institution.

They also discuss the claim that successful teams increase donations. The right upstream question is not whether some athletic success affects giving, but whether expected incremental gifts exceed the full economic and educational cost of maintaining the system.

Keep athletics finance year-specific. Conference structures, media contracts, NIL rules, athlete compensation, and NCAA governance have changed dramatically since 2010.

## 10. Student Bodies

**State:** secondary reconstruction.

This chapter turns from institutional structure to who attends college. It discusses race, ethnicity, sex, class, admissions, and completion. Contemporary reviews identify sections on rising Asian enrollment at elite institutions, the growing female share of enrollment and graduation, possible admissions pressure to maintain gender balance, and the strong relationship between family income and degree attainment.

The authors use sources attributed to ETS, Brookings, and other demographic datasets. Exact reports still need to be recovered from the printed bibliography.

This chapter is especially time-bound. The legal environment for race-conscious admissions, college demographics, sex ratios, and elite admissions practices have all changed since 2010. Do not use the chapter as a current description without updating every empirical claim.

The durable Blackball question is stronger: **which groups get access to which institutions, how much do they pay, and how much of the observed post-college difference is selection rather than treatment?**

## 11. Visiting the Future in Florida

**State:** secondary reconstruction; chapter pagination independently corroborated as pp. 193–204.

The case is Florida Gulf Coast University. Hacker and Dreifus examine a relatively low-cost, technology-mediated approach: self-paced software, outsourced or technologically assisted assessment, and other attempts to deliver instruction without reproducing the most expensive research-university model.

They are not simple evangelists for online education. Their liberal-arts ideal makes them wary of replacing human teaching. But FGCU functions as a limiting comparison: if conventional universities increasingly give undergraduates large lectures and adjunct labor anyway, then low-cost technological models cannot be dismissed merely because they look less traditional.

The useful mechanism is **unbundling**. Which parts of teaching require a professor's sustained personal attention, and which can be standardized, automated, shared, or purchased externally? The chapter should be revisited now because educational technology changed enormously after 2010.

## 12. The College Crucible: Add Students and Stir

**State:** secondary reconstruction; lowest-confidence chapter summary in the current dossier.

This chapter asks what college actually does to students, rather than what characteristics college graduates possess. Hacker summarized the question later as whether the classroom-and-campus years themselves change individuals in the ways institutions claim.

The methodological problem is selection. College graduates differ from non-graduates before they arrive; students at selective colleges differ from students at open-admission institutions before either group takes a class. Hacker's earlier use of Dale and Krueger is important here because it supplies a quasi-comparative strategy for separating student attributes from institutional brand.

Secondary discussions also associate the book's later argument with comparisons of political/social attitudes among college and noncollege populations. Cross-sectional differences cannot by themselves show that college caused those attitudes.

Until the full chapter is inspected, do not assign every later-book claim about student transformation, politics, or selection to chapter 12 with certainty. Treat the current summary as a retrieval hypothesis.

## 13. Schools We Like — Our Top Ten List

**State:** secondary reconstruction plus several primary author/institutional confirmations.

The ten schools praised are:

1. University of Mississippi (Ole Miss)
2. Raritan Valley Community College
3. University of Notre Dame
4. Cooper Union
5. Berea College
6. Arizona State University
7. University of Maryland, Baltimore County (UMBC)
8. Massachusetts Institute of Technology
9. Western Oregon University
10. Evergreen State College

The point of the list is not a conventional ranking. The schools are heterogeneous by design. Hacker and Dreifus praise institutions for specific mechanisms: attention to undergraduate teaching, low or zero tuition, unusual institutional missions, honors programs embedded in large public universities, serious undergraduate research, or experimentation with curriculum and pedagogy.

Their reporting at Arizona State is especially concrete. Dreifus publicly described ASU as unusually experimental, praised Barrett Honors College and the Polytechnic campus, and referred to laboratory work they personally saw during a visit.

Peter Brooks criticizes the list as an implausible substitute for elite brands. That criticism partly misses the authors' purpose: they are trying to break the assumption that one prestige hierarchy measures every educational virtue. But it does expose a weakness: the list has no common scoring rule and mixes affordability, teaching, mission, experimentation, and institutional character.

# What the book is strong evidence for

- The existence of particular practices the authors directly observed and reported.
- The language professors, students, and administrators used in interviews or conversations, subject to normal reporter-source limitations.
- The authors' own theory of what undergraduate education should be.
- A map of institutions and mechanisms worth checking against upstream data.
- A 2007–2010 snapshot of elite-college prestige, contingent labor, administration, athletics, teaching, and college costs as perceived by two unusually well-traveled critics.

# What it is weak evidence for without upstream checking

- National prevalence inferred from campus visits.
- Causal explanations for tuition growth.
- Claims that specific bodies of scholarship have little value.
- The causal effect of tenure on academic freedom.
- The causal effect of college on values, politics, or adulthood from cross-sectional comparisons.
- The lifetime “value” of elite education measured by prominence in *Who's Who*.
- Present-day conditions: the book is a 2010 artifact, not a 2026 survey.

# Main criticisms to preserve

Peter Brooks's NYRB review is useful because it agrees with several diagnoses while attacking the evidence used for others. He particularly objects to the Princeton-class / *Who's Who* test of whether elite graduates live “distinctive” lives and regards the final reform package as an uneven mixture.

Sherm Folland's academic review is useful in a different way. He emphasizes that *Higher Education?* is not a formal data-collection study and characterizes much of its evidence as campus visits and conversations. That limitation should become metadata, not a reason to discard the book.

The best Blackball use is therefore:

**reported case → authors' inference → upstream source → alternative explanation → present-day update**

rather than either “Hacker and Dreifus proved it” or “it was only anecdotal.”

# Relationship to Andrew Hacker's earlier work

Hacker's 2005 NYRB essay “The Truth About the Colleges” is a major precursor. It already contains many of the book's later concerns:

- national prestige brands and the schools later called the Golden Dozen;
- admissions selectivity and yield;
- Dale/Krueger on selective-college earnings;
- Asian and Jewish representation in elite admissions;
- Princeton Review teaching ratings;
- Ken Bain and Patrick Allitt on teaching;
- Carnegie classifications and undergraduate teaching;
- research-university staffing;
- NCES/IPEDS employment data;
