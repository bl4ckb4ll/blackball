## Publication context

The book is best understood as the second major book-form state of one project rather than as an unrelated 2000 title.

1. Shorris spent the early-to-mid 1990s researching poverty in the United States through extensive interviews.
2. During that research he interviewed Viniece Walker at Bedford Hills Correctional Facility. Her argument that poor people needed access to the cultural and reflective life of the city became the conceptual trigger for the Clemente Course.
3. Shorris ran the first Clemente Course in 1995–96 at the Roberto Clemente Family Guidance Center in Lower Manhattan.
4. In September 1997, *Harper's Magazine* published his first-person account of the experiment as "II. As a weapon in the hands of the restless poor," part of the package "On the Uses of a Liberal Education."
5. W. W. Norton then published *New American Blues: A Journey Through Poverty to Democracy* in 1997. The hardcover was 432 pages and treated the Clemente experiment as the culminating public-life section of a broader investigation of poverty.
6. Norton published the revised paperback in 2000 under the new title *Riches for the Poor: The Clemente Course in the Humanities*. Google Books labels it a revised edition and states that it was originally published in hardcover as *New American Blues*; CiNii likewise identifies it as a revised edition of the 1997 book.

This publication history matters because citations to *Riches for the Poor* and *New American Blues* can point into overlapping but not necessarily pagination-identical texts.

## Original summary

Shorris's central claim is that poverty is not adequately described by low income alone. Poor people, he argues, are surrounded by recurrent forces — violence, hunger, insecure housing, illness, racism, policing, unstable work, family crisis, and other demands — that compel continuous reaction. He calls this the **surround of force**.

His political claim follows from that diagnosis. A person forced into constant reaction has less opportunity to step outside immediate necessity, reflect, deliberate with others, and take part in the public world. Shorris uses "politics" in this broad classical sense: activity among people in family, neighborhood, community, and civic life. Poverty therefore reproduces itself partly by excluding people from reflective political agency.

The book's pivotal episode is Shorris's interview with Viniece Walker at Bedford Hills Correctional Facility. Walker argues that poor people need access to the "moral life of downtown": museums, plays, concerts, lectures, and the life of ideas. Shorris interprets this not as a request for cultural decoration but as a route toward reflection.

He then turns the claim into an experiment. The Clemente Course gives poor adults sustained instruction in moral philosophy, literature/poetry, art history, American history, logic/critical thinking, and writing/rhetoric, using serious canonical material rather than a remedialized vocational curriculum. The program also pays attention to the material conditions of attendance by providing books, transit, food, and child care.

The book's most important move is therefore from a theory of poverty to an institutional intervention:

**surround of force → reactive life → exclusion from reflection and politics**

versus

**humanities + dialogue + practical access supports → reflection → negotiation and public agency**

Shorris presents the first Clemente cohorts as evidence that the intervention can matter. Some graduates continued into college, received Bard scholarships, entered nursing school, or worked full-time. He also reports internal questionnaire changes in self-esteem, problem solving, and conflict behavior.

The evidence is suggestive but not equivalent to a controlled causal study. The program selected participants for minimum reading ability, interest, and capacity to attend; it supplied unusually attentive faculty, logistical support, and later Bard credit; and the outcomes of people who completed the course are not automatically outcomes caused by humanities instruction alone.

## The poverty argument

The first part of the book attempts to replace explanations of poverty that rely on a single variable. Shorris is interested in interacting forces and in the distinction between private survival and public participation. His concept of the surround of force makes poverty partly temporal and cognitive: necessity monopolizes attention.

This is where the book is strongest as a generator of testable questions. The relevant empirical questions are not simply whether poor people "lack culture." They include:

- how much time and cognitive bandwidth are consumed by unstable housing, transport, work schedules, caregiving, court involvement, health crises, and bureaucratic compliance;
- whether reducing those burdens changes educational persistence;
- whether discussion-heavy humanities courses produce effects distinguishable from equally intensive instruction in other subjects;
- whether institutional sponsorship and college credit account for some later educational continuation;
- whether the course changes civic participation, conflict behavior, writing ability, college enrollment, employment, or income, and over what time horizon.

## The Clemente experiment in the book's argument

The course was deliberately non-vocational. Shorris's point was not that poor adults needed simplified job skills while wealthy students received philosophy. He explicitly tried to eliminate that distinction by offering demanding college-level humanities material.

The original course used Plato, Aristotle, Thucydides, Sophocles, documentary American history, poetry, art history, and mathematical logic. The first faculty included Charles Simmons, Grace Glueck, Timothy Koranda, and Shorris himself. The course relied on Socratic dialogue and museum visits as well as conventional reading and writing.

## The political meaning of "riches"

The title is deliberately polemical. The "riches" are not a claim that reading Plato directly raises income. Shorris treats the humanities as access to forms of reflective and political life that elite education had historically reserved for people already advantaged by class.

The strongest version of his argument is that control over one's attention, language, judgment, and ability to deliberate with others is itself a form of power. On that reading, the book is about unequal access to the habits and institutions that prepare people to participate in public life.

The weaker and more vulnerable version is the suggestion that humanities education itself is a route out of poverty. That claim is much harder to isolate empirically from selection effects, material supports, elite faculty attention, college credit, and the broader social networks attached to Bard and the program.

## "A dangerous corollary"

Shorris does not present political education as a neutral mechanism for making poor people conform more efficiently to existing institutions. The program can produce people who negotiate within the existing system, but reflection can also lead them to challenge it. That is the force of the concluding political claim: education that increases agency may make its students less governable by patrons, social workers, employers, political organizers, or teachers themselves.

This point matters because it distinguishes Clemente from a simple human-capital program. Its stated purpose is not only employability but autonomy and participation.

## Relationship to *A Nation of Salesmen*

The two books attack different mechanisms but share a concern with the conversion of persons into objects of external forces.

- *A Nation of Salesmen* argues that market and sales criteria spread into culture, politics, institutions, and self-presentation.
- *Riches for the Poor* argues that poverty traps people inside a reactive surround of force and denies them access to reflective political life.

In Blackball terms, both are useful as mechanism proposals rather than as final empirical verdicts. One asks how market-mediated judgment spreads; the other asks how material and institutional pressure constrains reflection and civic agency.

### *Publishers Weekly* on *New American Blues* — 1997

The review describes the hardcover as a two-part poverty study in which interview narratives lead into the public-life argument and the Clemente Course. It records the book's first-serial publication in *Harper's*, *Poets & Writers*, and *Networker*, and notes that sections also appeared in weekly installments on NPR's *Marketplace*. Clay Armstrong's review treats *Riches for the Poor* as a revised work that evolved out of Shorris's poverty research and credits the book with connecting an adult-education experiment to a broader theory of public life. ## Blackball assessment

*Riches for the Poor* should be used as three things at once, with the layers kept separate:

1. **A first-person intellectual history** of how Shorris moved from interviewing poor Americans to founding Clemente.
2. **A theory of poverty** centered on the surround of force, reflection, and political participation.
3. **An advocacy account of an educational intervention** whose early outcomes are interesting but do not by themselves identify a causal effect.

The book is especially valuable for questions about class because it makes an explicit claim that a form of education historically associated with elites should be redistributed without being diluted into vocational remediation. The empirical question for Blackball is then what exactly the redistributed package contains: texts, teaching quality, time, money, transport, child care, peer group, institutional prestige, credits, and access to further education.
