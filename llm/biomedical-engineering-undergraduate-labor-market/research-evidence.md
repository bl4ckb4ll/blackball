# Research evidence: undergraduate BME and the labor market

## 1. Ohio State: direct comparison with traditional engineering majors

**Source:** Racheida S. Lewis et al., “Evaluating factors that influence career attainment in biomedical engineering undergraduates compared to other engineering majors,” *Discover Education* 5, 330 (2026), published 13 March 2026. DOI: 10.1007/s44217-026-01339-7.

https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-026-01339-7

This is unusually useful because it compares BME with other engineering majors inside one institution rather than merely quoting the growth rate of the occupation.

The dataset contained **3,292 graduates** from Fall 2012 through Spring 2019:

| Major | Graduates |
| --- | ---: |
| Biomedical engineering | 431 |
| Chemical engineering | 1,192 |
| Materials science and engineering | 305 |
| Mechanical engineering | 1,364 |

### Industry placement

The study reports the following proportion entering industry:

| Major | Entered industry |
| --- | ---: |
| Biomedical engineering | **33%** |
| Chemical engineering | **69%** |
| Materials science and engineering | **59%** |
| Mechanical engineering | **68%** |

The authors describe BME’s industry placement as **noticeably low relative to the other majors**. BME graduates were more likely than the comparison groups to go to medical school or graduate school; about 20% went to medical school.

This matters because “many BME graduates go to graduate or medical school” is not the same proposition as “a BME bachelor’s degree is a strong direct-to-industry credential.” A prospective undergraduate choosing the major because it sounds like the obvious path to medical-device or biomedical-engineering employment needs the latter quantity.

### Pre-graduation engineering work

The same paper reports engineering work experience before graduation at approximately:

| Major | Pre-graduation engineering work experience |
| --- | ---: |
| Biomedical engineering | **34%** |
| Chemical engineering | **58%** |
| Materials science and engineering | **59%** |
| Mechanical engineering | **69%** |

Career-fair attendance was also lower for BME: about 75%, versus roughly 85–90% in the comparison majors.

The introduction reviews the structural problem directly: biomedical industry employers historically hired engineers from traditional disciplines; employers recruiting for biomedical work may want mechanical/electrical skills or accept several majors; and BME students can therefore face a mismatch between the occupational identity implied by their degree and actual hiring practice.

**Important limitation:** these are historical cohorts at one institution. They are not a current national placement rate. They are nevertheless much more informative about the undergraduate-major question than a national occupation growth percentage.

## 2. University of Michigan authors: BME students and difficulty entering industry

**Source:** Cassandra Sue Ellen Jamison, Annie AnMeng Wang, Aileen Huang-Saad, Shanna R. Daly, and Lisa R. Lattuca, “BME Career Exploration: Examining Students’ Connection with the Field,” *Biomedical Engineering Education* 2(1), 17–29 (2022; online 2021).

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8553099/

The first two authors were affiliated with the University of Michigan Department of Biomedical Engineering. The paper opens from a premise prospective students rarely see on a departmental marketing page: BME undergraduates are commonly perceived to **struggle to find industry jobs after graduation**, and the authors state that some statistics support that concern.

The paper discusses several possible disadvantages:

- the unusually broad and diverse nature of BME curricula;
- internship and co-op access;
- the relationship between the number of graduates and available jobs;
- salaries and measures of competitiveness;
- students’ identification with the BME field even when their later employment may not carry the BME label.

This source is significant not because it proves that Michigan BME graduates cannot get jobs—it does not—but because researchers inside a university offering the degree were explicitly studying a labor-market problem that generic undergraduate advising can easily omit.

Michigan currently describes its BME undergraduate program as preparation to work in the biomedical industry and elsewhere, and its curriculum pages use demand-oriented language. Those statements should be read alongside, not instead of, the labor-market literature.

Michigan undergraduate BME: https://bme.umich.edu/academics/undergraduate/

Michigan BME curriculum: https://bme.umich.edu/academics/undergraduate/curriculum/

## 3. A long-running curricular/job-market concern

Jennifer Berglund’s two 2015 *IEEE Pulse* articles show that the concern is not new.

- “The great divide: some worry of a gap between what is being taught in American university undergraduate BME programs and the job market.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25782112/
- “The real world: BME graduates reflect on whether universities are providing adequate preparation for a career in industry.” https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25782113/

The first article is particularly useful as a historical check on promotional narratives built around rapid projected occupational growth. Growth can coexist with a small occupation, a large number of graduates, employers’ preference for traditional engineering specialties, or the need for graduate education.

A later career-attainment study likewise states that recruitment analyses found much greater industry demand for traditional engineering and computer-science fields than for BME and other emerging disciplines:

“Identifying Supports and Barriers in Engineering Students’ Processes Toward Career Attainment” (2023): https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43683-023-00119-1

## 4. The occupation exists, pays well, and is small

**Source:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook, “Bioengineers and Biomedical Engineers.”

https://www.bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/biomedical-engineers.htm

As of the BLS page updated in August 2026:

- **23,800 jobs** in 2025;
- **$109,370 median annual pay** in May 2025;
- **8% projected growth** from 2025 to 2035;
- about **1,800 net additional jobs** over that decade;
- about **1,200 openings per year** on average, including replacement openings.

These numbers are not evidence that the major is worthless. They are evidence that the relevant denominator matters. “8% growth” sounds large when isolated from a base of 23,800 jobs and an annual flow of about 1,200 openings.

BLS also says that entrants may have a bachelor’s degree in **bioengineering, biomedical engineering, or a related engineering field**, and specifically notes that mechanical- and electrical-engineering students can prepare for biomedical work by taking biological-science courses.

That means a BME graduate competes in a market that is not reserved for BME majors. The alternative-degree comparison is therefore essential:

> Would a student seeking medical-device work be better served by mechanical or electrical engineering plus biomedical coursework, because that route preserves access to a much larger non-biomedical labor market as well?

The answer will vary by student and role, but it is a question that should precede school rankings.

**Source:** BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, May 2023 metropolitan-area data for Bioengineers and Biomedical Engineers.

https://www.bls.gov/oes/2023/may/oes172031.htm

The largest metropolitan employment counts included approximately:

| Metro | Employment |
| --- | ---: |
| Boston-Cambridge-Nashua | 2,680 |
| Minneapolis-St. Paul | 1,010 |
| Los Angeles-Long Beach-Anaheim | 860 |
| New York-Newark-Jersey City | 820 |
| San Francisco-Oakland-Hayward | 700 |
| Salt Lake City | 600 |
| Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue | 560 |
| San Diego-Carlsbad | 510 |
| Washington-Arlington-Alexandria | 480 |
| Philadelphia-Camden-Wilmington | 440 |

Boston’s location quotient was about 7.64; Salt Lake City’s about 5.83; Durham-Chapel Hill’s about 5.71; Minneapolis-St. Paul’s about 4.15.

The appropriate conclusion is **geographic concentration in industry hubs**, not the exaggerated claim that all BME jobs are located in two or three hospitals. Several of the major hubs are also expensive housing markets, making geographic concentration relevant to the economic value of the credential.

## 6. Why raw job-board counts are misleading

Current Indeed searches illustrate a measurement problem.

A query for “entry level biomedical engineer” can return hundreds of results, but visible listings include:

- internships;
- clinical-research jobs;
- systems and verification jobs;
- mechanical-design roles;
- positions accepting BME **or** mechanical/related engineering degrees;
- postings nominally called entry-level that still request prior industry experience;
- jobs using biomedical vocabulary without requiring a BME bachelor’s degree.

Examples seen in 2026 searches included a Redan Medical “Biomedical Engineer” posting that was a part-time internship and accepted students pursuing biomedical **or mechanical** engineering; a Boston Scientific R&D Engineer I listing requiring a mechanical-engineering bachelor’s degree; and other BME-titled or BME-adjacent positions asking for one to three years of experience.

Indeed search pages:

- https://www.indeed.com/q-entry-level-biomedical-engineer-jobs.html
- https://www.indeed.com/q-biomedical-engineering-entry-level-jobs.html
- https://www.indeed.com/q-biomedical-mechanical-engineer-jobs.html

### Historical ChatGPT-session observation

In the December 2025 sessions that motivated this dossier, one Indeed query reportedly returned **387** results for “Biomedical Mechanical Engineer.” The visible sample included Sciton in Palo Alto and Precision Neuroscience in Manhattan; the former asked for prior experience despite entry-level framing, and the latter was senior. The prior analysis found no degree-only/no-experience BME job in the visible sample.

That count is **ephemeral historical session evidence**, not a reproducible current statistic. The durable methodological point is the one above: job-board result counts must be manually classified before they are treated as the number of entry-level jobs available to BME bachelor’s graduates.

## 7. Employers can prefer traditional disciplines for biomedical work

A 2021 study explicitly examined what employers requested in **95 BME-relevant job postings**:

Jamison et al., “What are biomedical engineering employers looking for in new hires? A Qualitative Synthesis,” *2021 IEEE Frontiers in Education Conference*. DOI 10.1109/FIE49875.2021.9637148.

Metadata: https://www.researchwithnj.com/en/publications/what-are-biomedical-engineering-employers-looking-for-in-new-hire

A related 2024 paper studied industry feedback on BME student resumes:

https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43683-024-00154-6

These sources are useful because they move the question from “what does the curriculum intend to teach?” to “what do employers actually request and reward?”

## 8. Counterevidence: the outcome is not inevitable

A dossier about mismatch should include programs reporting better direct-industry results.

“BME 2.0: Engineering the Future of Medicine” reports that the University of Minnesota’s redesigned BME program achieved roughly **70% undergraduate employment in industry**, compared with an approximately **40% average across BME departments** discussed by the authors.

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10530648/

The authors argue that increasing engineering depth and redesigning the curriculum can improve outcomes. This is important evidence against a crude claim that the BME label itself makes industry employment impossible.

It also strengthens a more useful hypothesis: **curricular breadth/depth, employer access, internships, specialization, and local industry structure can materially determine whether an undergraduate BME program works as a direct professional degree.**

## 9. Questions a responsible prospective-student analysis should answer

Before recommending an undergraduate BME program, collect at least:

1. Number and percentage of graduates entering full-time industry jobs within six months.
2. Job titles, not just “employed.”
3. Whether those jobs actually require/prefer BME or merely accept it.
4. Percentage entering graduate school or medical school.
5. Percentage still seeking work.
6. Internship/co-op participation and placement rates.
7. Employers attending the engineering career fair specifically recruiting BME students.
8. Geographic distribution of employers.
9. Median starting salary for bachelor’s-only BME graduates.
10. Comparable outcomes for ME, EE, ChE, MSE, and CS students at the same institution.
11. Four-year net price and debt, not just sticker tuition.
12. Whether students can get sufficient depth in mechanics, electronics, controls, computation, materials, or another employer-recognized specialty.

A list of “best BME schools” without these quantities answers the university-ranking question before answering the student’s economic question.
