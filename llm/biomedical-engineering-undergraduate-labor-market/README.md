# Undergraduate biomedical engineering: labor-market mismatch, occupational framing, and cost

**Status:** evidence dossier assembled 2026-08-31.

This dossier asks a narrower question than “is biomedical engineering good or bad?”:

> **What should a prospective undergraduate be told before buying a biomedical-engineering degree on the expectation that the degree is a direct route into biomedical-engineering industry work?**

The evidence does **not** support the categorical claim that biomedical engineering has no jobs. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics counted about **23,800 bioengineer and biomedical-engineer jobs in 2025**, with a median wage of **$109,370** and about **1,200 openings per year** projected over 2025–2035. BLS also explicitly says the occupation can be entered with a bachelor’s degree in biomedical engineering **or a related engineering field**.

The evidence does support a substantial warning about treating the undergraduate BME major as an ordinary direct-to-industry engineering credential:

- A 2026 Ohio State study of **3,292 engineering graduates** found only **33% of BME graduates entered industry**, compared with **68% mechanical engineering, 69% chemical engineering, and 59% materials science and engineering**. Only **34% of BME students** had pre-graduation engineering work experience, versus **58–69%** in the comparison majors.
- A University of Michigan–authored BME education paper begins from the observation that BME undergraduates are commonly perceived to **struggle to find industry jobs**, and says some statistics support the concern. It discusses internship/co-op access, graduate-to-job ratios, salaries, curricular breadth, and employer preferences as possible sources of disadvantage.
- Historical and current literature repeatedly describes a mismatch between broad undergraduate BME curricula and employers that can instead hire mechanical, electrical, chemical, software, materials, or other engineers and teach the biomedical domain afterward.
- Current job-board searches contain many adjacent jobs that accept BME among several degrees, internships, clinical/research jobs, and postings requiring prior experience. A raw search-result count is therefore not a count of degree-specific entry-level jobs.
- BLS employment is concentrated in a limited set of metropolitan hubs. That does not mean BME jobs exist only in two or three places, but location flexibility matters unusually strongly.
- Reddit testimony from students and graduates repeatedly reports the same failure mode in ordinary language: a curriculum broad enough to touch several engineering disciplines but sometimes not deep enough to compete with a specialist for the first job. This is anecdotal evidence, not a prevalence estimate, and contrary experiences are included.
- At several universities offering BME or closely related undergraduate degrees, current published sticker tuition can imply a four-year academic cost well above $100,000 before aid. At private universities in this dossier, a static four-year tuition estimate exceeds **$270,000**.

## The disclosure problem

Universities commonly describe BME programs with explicit occupational language: preparation for biomedical industry, industry-ready engineers, employer demand, career paths, and employer recruiting. That is legitimate if accompanied by clear outcome evidence. The problem worth investigating is whether prospective students are given enough information to distinguish:

1. **the existence and growth rate of the biomedical-engineer occupation;**
2. **the number of jobs and openings;**
3. **the fraction of BME graduates who enter those jobs directly;**
4. **the fact that those jobs often accept other engineering degrees;**
5. **the geographic concentration of employers;**
6. **the value of internships/co-ops and specialization;**
7. **the cost of the BME degree relative to a broader engineering degree that can enter both biomedical and non-biomedical markets.**

Those are different quantities. A program can truthfully say “biomedical engineers are in demand” while still leaving a student with a misleading picture if it does not disclose the denominator: how many graduates are seeking how many entry-level jobs, where those jobs are, what employers ask for, and what competing majors are eligible.

## Evidentiary boundary

This dossier **does not claim** that universities know a BME degree will not lead to a job, that the programs are fraudulent, or that administrators intend to deceive students. The sources collected here do not establish intent, and BME graduates plainly do obtain good jobs.

The supportable claim is narrower and more important: **there is published evidence of a direct-to-industry mismatch for undergraduate BME, including evidence produced inside universities that themselves offer BME degrees, while those universities continue to market the major in occupational and industry-oriented terms at substantial cost.** That combination deserves to be made visible to prospective students.
