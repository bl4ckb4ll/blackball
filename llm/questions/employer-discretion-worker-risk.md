# Employer discretion, worker risk, and the absence of a general fairness guarantee

## Retrieval target

This is a Blackball question, not a conclusion.

The question is not whether employment is literally ruleless. Employment law, contracts, wage-and-hour law, anti-discrimination law, collective bargaining agreements, licensing rules, and other constraints plainly create rules.

The narrower and more important question is:

**What does the employment system actually guarantee to a worker, and what risks is an employer legally and institutionally allowed to leave with the worker?**

A useful working proposition is:

> The law forbids some conduct, but it does not generally guarantee symmetry, truthfulness among competing applicants, continued employability, proportional reward for effort, promotion by merit, or a fair exchange of information and risk between employer and worker.

That proposition must be decomposed and tested rather than treated as a slogan.

## Questions to answer from evidence

- What may employers lawfully ask applicants about prior employment, including firings or other terminations?
- Which questions are forbidden or restricted by federal, state, or local law?
- When an employer asks about a prior firing, what evidence may it seek and how often is the answer independently verified?
- What consequences actually follow when applicants answer inaccurately or lie?
- Does the screening system create an advantage for applicants willing to give the expected answer rather than the most literal or nuanced answer?
- Which screening questions predict actual job performance, theft, attendance, safety, retention, or other relevant outcomes?
- Which questions mainly select for conformity with an employer's preferred response style?
- When an employer rejects an applicant because of a prior termination, who bears the cost of errors, context loss, retaliation, incompatible supervisors, or false records?
- What reciprocal obligations does an employer assume when it demands detailed employment history from an applicant?
- What, if anything, guarantees that exceptional effort will be converted into wages, bonuses, promotion, scheduling preference, training, or greater security?
- When employers give symbolic recognition such as "employee of the month," what measurable economic consequences follow for the worker?
- Are promotion systems tied substantially to documented performance, or can employers use broad managerial discretion while still describing the system as merit-based?
- When schools and career offices advise workers to accumulate credentials, internships, networking, recommendations, perfect work histories, or unpaid extra-role effort, which of those investments come with an enforceable reciprocal commitment from employers?

## Legal baseline: rules exist, but they are not a general fairness guarantee

Federal guidance from the EEOC and FTC is a useful starting point.

The EEOC states that, except for particular restrictions such as medical and genetic information, it is generally not illegal for an employer to ask questions about an applicant's background or to require a background check. Employment-history information is among the categories employers may investigate. Federal anti-discrimination rules still constrain how information is requested and used, and state or local law may impose additional restrictions.

Primary starting sources:

- EEOC, *Background Checks*: https://www.eeoc.gov/background-checks
- EEOC, *Background Checks: What Job Applicants and Employees Should Know*: https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/background-checks-what-job-applicants-and-employees-should-know
- FTC/EEOC, *Background Checks: What Employers Need to Know*: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/background-checks-what-employers-need-know
- FTC, *Employer Background Checks and Your Rights*: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/employer-background-checks-and-your-rights
- EEOC, *Prohibited Employment Policies/Practices*: https://www.eeoc.gov/prohibited-employment-policiespractices

Do not turn this federal baseline into the stronger claim that every question is lawful everywhere. Build jurisdiction-specific records where the exact rule matters.

The relevant distinction for Blackball is:

**a prohibition against particular abuses is not the same thing as a positive guarantee of fairness.**

For example, anti-discrimination law can prohibit using a prior-employment screen in a discriminatory way without creating a general right to be judged only on present ability to perform the job.

## Prior termination as a screening device

The concrete question "Have you ever been terminated from a job?" is useful because it separates several different mechanisms.

### Information about future performance

An employer may believe a prior firing predicts reliability, conduct, safety, attendance, or future termination.

Evidence required:

- validation studies;
- employer policies;
- reference-check practices;
- actual predictive power after controlling for job type, tenure, industry, and reason for termination.

### Risk transfer

The employer can often reduce its own uncertainty by rejecting an applicant with a negative signal. The rejected applicant may bear nearly all of the cost even when the signal is noisy or context-dependent.

Evidence required:

- hiring policies;
- appeal or explanation procedures;
- treatment of disputed terminations;
- reference-check error rates;
- litigation or administrative records showing how mistakes are corrected.

### Bargaining leverage

A blemished work history may reduce outside options and therefore affect wages, willingness to contest conditions, or acceptance of a lower-status job.

This is plausible but should not be asserted from intuition alone.

Evidence required:

- wage effects of displacement and involuntary termination;
- reemployment duration;
- wage scarring;
- hiring experiments or administrative data that distinguish termination from ordinary unemployment.

### Truthfulness asymmetry

A screening question may impose a larger immediate cost on a truthful applicant than on an applicant who supplies the expected answer and is never caught.

Do not convert this into the unsupported claim that dishonest applicants routinely win.

Questions to measure:

- How often are prior terminations verified?
- What records are available to employers?
- How often are discrepancies discovered before hire, after hire, or never?
- What sanctions follow from false statements on applications?
- Are sanctions predictable enough to make truthful and dishonest applicants face comparable expected costs?

The system does not need to reward lying for an asymmetry to exist. It is enough that verification and enforcement are incomplete while a truthful disclosure carries an immediate screening cost.

## The "right answer" problem in applicant assessments

Some hiring assessments use integrity, personality, situational-judgment, or attitude questions whose employer-preferred answer may be obvious.

A candidate who treats a question literally or philosophically may answer differently from a candidate who recognizes the intended workplace norm and supplies the expected response.

That creates several questions:

- Is the assessment measuring the underlying trait it claims to measure?
- Is it measuring knowledge of the socially expected answer?
- Is it measuring willingness to conform to test conventions?
- Does nuanced reasoning create a penalty even when it would not impair job performance?
- Can an applicant infer the scoring rule and simply perform the desired persona?

### Walmart lead

Recover the exact, dated wording of any Walmart hiring-assessment item paraphrased as asking whether there could ever be a justified reason to steal.

Do not preserve the paraphrase as a quotation unless the original assessment or a reliable contemporaneous record is found.

Then determine:

- which assessment instrument contained it;
- whether Walmart or a vendor designed the item;
- what construct the item was intended to measure;
- whether validation evidence exists;
- how the item was scored;
- whether similar integrity-test questions have known susceptibility to coached or socially desirable answers.

## Recognition is not remuneration

"Employee of the month" is a useful test case because it can be either a meaningful signal or nearly pure symbolic payment.

Do not assume that the recognized worker "worked for free." Extra effort performed during paid hours is still paid labor; legally unpaid work is a separate wage-and-hour question.

Instead ask what the recognition converts into.

For a recognition program, measure:

- cash bonus;
- hourly wage increase;
- promotion probability;
- promotion timing;
- access to preferred shifts;
- training opportunities;
- additional paid leave;
- job security;
- performance-review score;
- later reference quality;
- no measurable economic consequence.

The key accounting identity is not "plaque = wage."

It is:

**additional worker contribution → employer value captured → worker compensation received now → worker expected future benefit**.

A plaque can be worth something if it reliably changes the last term. If it does not, it should not be silently counted as compensation merely because the employer labels it recognition.

A landlord's refusal to accept a plaque as rent is a crude but useful reminder that symbolic recognition and fungible compensation are different goods.

## Promotion and the problem of discretionary merit

A company may describe promotion as merit-based while preserving substantial managerial discretion.

Blackball should therefore distinguish:

- stated promotion criteria;
- measured performance;
- awards and recognition;
- manager sponsorship;
- tenure;
- credentials;
- social networks;
- vacancies and organizational need;
- actual promotion outcomes.

The question is not whether performance is "considered." Almost anything can be considered at a negligible weight.

The stronger question is:

**How much of the promotion decision is actually explained by the worker behavior the organization publicly rewards?**

If repeated employee-of-the-month awards strongly predict promotion, pay growth, or durable internal preference, that is evidence that the award is part of a real advancement system.

If the association is weak or absent, the award should be analyzed primarily as recognition, motivation, status, or labor-management practice rather than assumed career advancement.

## The asymmetry Blackball should measure

For each employer demand, record the reciprocal worker protection or benefit, if any.

| Employer demand or discretion | Worker-side reciprocal guarantee to look for |
| --- | --- |
| disclose prior firings | opportunity to explain; correction mechanism; job-related validation |
| provide references | accuracy duties; dispute procedure; limits on defamatory or false information |
| background check | consent, disclosure, copy, adverse-action procedure where applicable |
| personality/integrity assessment | validation; transparent relevance; appeal or accommodation where applicable |
| exceptional effort | bonus, wage growth, promotion, or another measurable return |
| flexibility in schedule | reciprocal schedule security or premium pay |
| loyalty / long tenure | severance, seniority rights, pension, recall rights, or other durable claim |
| credentials and training | preferential hiring, wage premium, advancement rule, or enforceable placement commitment |

An empty right-hand cell is itself evidence worth preserving.

But do not fill it with "nothing" until the relevant contract, law, policy, collective agreement, and actual practice have been checked.

## What Blackball should not say

Avoid these overstatements:

- "There are no rules."
- "Employers can ask anything."
- "Companies can fire anyone for literally any reason."
- "All merit systems are fake."
- "Employee of the month is unpaid labor."
- "Honest applicants always lose to liars."
- "A prior firing permanently prevents employment."

Those claims are either false as universals or require evidence far beyond the anecdote.

Prefer the narrower claim that is both harsher and more defensible:

> Many employment institutions constrain particular abuses without guaranteeing a fair contest, symmetric information, continued employment, proportional rewards, or advancement based on merit. The worker can therefore comply with every stated expectation and still bear risks that no institution has promised to compensate.

## Evidence families to build

### Law and regulation

- EEOC and FTC guidance;
- state and local restrictions on hiring inquiries;
- wage-and-hour law;
- wrongful-discharge and public-policy exceptions;
- collective bargaining agreements;
- public-sector civil-service rules as a comparison group;
- FCRA employment-background procedures;
- cases involving false or disputed employment-history reports.

### Employer primary sources

- actual application forms;
- hiring assessments;
- employee handbooks;
- promotion policies;
- recognition-program rules;
- disciplinary matrices;
- severance policies;
- reference-check policies;
- internal-job-posting criteria where publicly available.

### Worker outcomes

- displacement and wage-scarring studies;
- reemployment duration after termination;
- promotion and wage growth;
- employee-recognition outcome studies;
- experimental audit studies of hiring;
- validity studies for integrity and personality assessments.

### Comparison institutions

Use settings with stronger reciprocal rules as controls:

- unionized workplaces;
- civil service;
- formal apprenticeship contracts;
- tenure systems;
- collectively bargained seniority and grievance systems;
- occupations with statutory just-cause protections where available.

The comparison should identify what changes when workers acquire enforceable procedural or economic claims rather than simply asking whether managers become nicer.

## Relation to Blackball's education and career-office work

This question directly limits what colleges, training programs, and career offices can responsibly promise.

A school can teach useful material and still have no power to guarantee that an employer:

- interviews the graduate;
- ignores a prior firing;
- interprets an assessment charitably;
- rewards exceptional performance;
- promotes the most productive worker;
- pays for every extra credential;
- keeps the worker employed after the training investment is sunk.

Therefore Blackball should keep separate:

**education supplied by the school**

from

**employment discretion retained by the employer**

from

**risk left with the worker**.

That separation matters whenever a school presents education as though it were a reliable route to an employment outcome the school does not control and the employer has not promised.
