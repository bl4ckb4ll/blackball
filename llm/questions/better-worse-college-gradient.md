# Better, worse, and what college is for

Blackball should not terminate at “everything is bad.” Its evidence structure should make it possible to say that one institution, department, program, teacher, or practice is better than another — and to explain **in what respect**.

The important constraint is that “better” must not become a single prestige score.

## Two questions that colleges routinely blur

For an individual student, keep at least these two questions separate:

1. **Is this a defensible financial/labor-market transaction?**
2. **Is there serious human or intellectual value in the education itself?**

A college can do well on one and badly on the other.

A literature professor who knows poetry deeply, teaches it well, and never claims to secure a job for the student may be delivering exactly what was promised. The fact that the instruction does not map directly onto an occupation is not evidence that the teaching is fraudulent.

Conversely, genuine intellectual value does not license administrators to market the same education with unsupported salary, placement, mobility, or career claims. “The poems are worth knowing” and “borrowing $120,000 for this program is financially sensible” are different propositions and require different evidence.

## Blackball should preserve positive cases

The corpus should actively record institutions and practices that behave better, rather than using good cases merely as exceptions to an indictment.

Candidate institutions such as Reed College or Oberlin College should therefore be audited rather than either pre-approved or pre-condemned. If they turn out to do some things unusually well, say so plainly and preserve the evidence.

Likewise preserve professors, departments, programs, libraries, archives, public courses, films, books, performances, and other cultural institutions that genuinely teach something worth knowing without pretending that consumption of the material guarantees employment.

## A multidimensional college audit

Do not collapse the following into one score. Keep a dashboard or vector of findings.

### 1. Financial exposure

- published price versus actual net price by family income;
- grants versus loans;
- debt at graduation and among non-completers;
- housing, food, insurance, books, travel, and other hidden costs;
- whether a poor student can realistically participate in the ordinary life of the institution without additional family money.

### 2. Completion and downside risk

- completion rates, including by income/Pell status where available;
- transfer and withdrawal;
- what happens financially to students who do not complete;
- whether bad outcomes are hidden inside averages for graduates only.

### 3. Labor-market honesty

- exact employment claims made in recruiting and advising;
- whether the school follows graduates far enough to know what actually happened;
- occupation, compensation, stability, benefits, and relevance when reported;
- whether graduate school is counted as an outcome without following the next handoff;
- whether weak occupational prospects are disclosed before enrollment;
- whether claims are causal or merely correlations with students the school admitted.

### 4. Educational substance

- what students actually read, make, study, practice, observe, and discuss;
- access to instructors who know the material deeply;
- course availability and class size;
- continuity of serious fields that may have little immediate market payoff;
- evidence that students are being taught rather than merely credentialed.

### 5. Class accessibility and class experience

Do not treat “poor students mix with rich students” as automatically beneficial or automatically harmful.

Track:

- class composition;
- net cost by income;
- food/housing insecurity and emergency support;
- unpaid internships, travel, clothing, social life, and other costs that silently sort students by family resources;
- access to faculty, research, clubs, internships, and other opportunities by class position;
- qualitative accounts of class isolation, shame, belonging, mentorship, and friendship;
- whether the institution expects low-income students to assimilate into wealthy norms while congratulating itself for diversity.

A low-income student can receive real intellectual opportunity and also experience severe class dislocation. Preserve both facts.

### 6. Administrative honesty and conflicts

- administrative growth and cost where it is relevant;
- misleading career or entrepreneurship branding;
- conflicts of interest;
- professors, administrators, or affiliated centers marketing companies, investment products, speculative assets, or consulting claims through academic authority;
- whether scandals produce correction or merely public relations.

Do not presume every administrator is bad or every professor is good. Attribute conduct to the people and offices that actually performed it.

### 7. Moral and civic environment

Success, salary, title, admission, and prestige are outcomes, not complete measures of a life.

Track what institutions teach implicitly and explicitly about:

- power;
- obligations to other people;
- truthfulness;
- exploitation and resistance;
- religion and spiritual life;
- art and beauty;
- family and community;
- conscience and moral compromise;
- whether wealth and authority are treated as self-justifying.

Price M. Cobbs and Judith L. Turnock's *Cracking the Corporate Code* belongs here as a useful source because the successful endpoint of a corporate career can coexist with racial hostility, isolation, difficult informal rules, accommodation, and other costs that are invisible in a placement statistic.

## Educational value need not mean canonical “great books”

Do not restrict serious culture to Shakespeare, Sophocles, or Einstein.

A two-hour film, a historical reconstruction, a travel documentary, an old television episode, an oral history, a poem, a religious text, a technical manual, or a skilled teacher demonstrating a craft can all be intellectually serious.

Examples currently worth preserving or auditing include:

- Werner Herzog's *Invincible* and its portrayal of Benjamin, faith, intelligence, Nazism, vulnerability, spectacle, and power;
- Ibn Battuta travel material;
- Ruth Goodman, Peter Ginn, and related historical-farm / experimental-archaeology television;
- the *Danger Man* “He feeds my mind” scene as a direct question about what a teacher is;
- poetry taught by someone who actually knows and loves poetry.

The question is not whether every person should value every item. The question is whether Blackball can expose people to serious material, say why someone found it worth preserving, establish provenance where possible, and avoid converting every cultural recommendation into a career claim.

## Direction of travel

The useful endpoint is a **gradient with receipts**.

Instead of:

> College is good.

or:

> College is a scam.

Blackball should be able to produce something closer to:

> This institution appears unusually serious about undergraduate teaching and unusually honest about what it offers. For a student with this aid package the net price is defensible; for a student paying near sticker price it is not. Its humanities education may be excellent even though it does not cause a particular salary. Its placement claims are supported here and unsupported there. Low-income students report these benefits and these class costs. These specific people and practices deserve praise; these others deserve criticism.

That is actionable. It gives students and parents something better than prestige, cynicism, or marketing, and it gives institutions a visible direction in which they could improve.
