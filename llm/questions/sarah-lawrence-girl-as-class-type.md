# What did “Sarah Lawrence girl” mean as a class type?

## Question

When American writers and critics referred to a **“Sarah Lawrence girl,”** what combination of economic class, cultural capital, gender, sexuality, and intellectual style were they naming—and how did that type change over time?

## Why this matters

Joan Didion’s 1961 dismissal of *Franny and Zooey* as self-help for the upper middle classes culminates in her “Sarah Lawrence girls” line. That phrase only works if “Sarah Lawrence girl” already carries a social meaning beyond enrollment at one college.

The goal is to reconstruct that meaning historically rather than importing a present-day stereotype backward.

## Periodized hypotheses

### 1920s–1940s: the “Lawrence girl”

Candidate type:

- affluent;
- white/WASP-coded;
- educated at a women’s institution;
- institutionally shaped by a founder who imagined a distinct women’s social sphere and marriage as central to women’s lives;
- subject to admissions boundaries that included Jewish quotas.

Best evidence:

- Lawrence founding correspondence and instructions;
- early charters/curriculum documents;
- Board of Trustees minutes;
- admissions records;
- contemporary 1926 journalism.

### 1950s–1960s: the cultivated individualist / bohemian elite woman

Candidate type:

- still economically privileged;
- deliberately nonconformist in manners and intellectual taste;
- high cultural capital;
- anti-grading / anti-conventional-status pedagogy;
- socially and sexually more independent than conventional elite-women stereotypes.

Best evidence:

- 1963 Cowan/Cowan *Harvard Crimson* profile;
- student/alumna writing;
- college magazines and admissions material;
- Didion’s 1961 use of “Sarah Lawrence girls.”

### 1970s–1980s: feminism, lesbian visibility, and institutional memory

Candidate type:

- women’s intellectual independence increasingly read through feminism and sexual nonconformity;
- Sarah Lawrence used by outside writers as a symbol of changing women’s culture;
- simultaneous public excavation of older exclusionary admissions practices.

Best evidence:

- Anne Roiphe, 1977;
- institutional/student responses to Roiphe;
- Louise Blecher Rose, 1983;
- Sarah Lawrence archive materials on diversity and admissions.

### 1990s–present: inherited stereotype versus internally stratified experience

Candidate problem:

- the rich/bohemian-girl image survives;
- students who do not possess inherited wealth encounter and describe the class boundary from inside;
- later writers consciously historicize the old type rather than simply inhabiting it.

Best evidence:

- Porochista Khakpour’s account of entering in 1996 on scholarship;
- Jamie Jordan’s *Ghosts & Dreams* genealogy;
- current and historical financial-aid / family-income data if demographic claims are needed.

## Salinger subquestion

What does Didion gain by saying “Sarah Lawrence girls” rather than simply “young women” or “college students”?

Candidate answer to test:

She is identifying a **classed form of alienation**: people with substantial economic and cultural resources who experience conventional ambition, prestige, and bourgeois life as spiritually debased, while retaining the material freedom and cultural training that make that critique possible.

Primary Salinger texts to inspect against this claim:

- *The Catcher in the Rye*;
- “Uncle Wiggily in Connecticut”;
- “Franny”;
- “Zooey.”

Critical checks:

- Joan Didion, 1961;
- Grace Byron, 2024;
- contemporary reviews by Alfred Kazin, John Updike, Mary McCarthy, George Steiner, and others where relevant.

## What would falsify or weaken the class-type claim?

- period data showing the student body was substantially less economically elite than contemporary writers assumed;
- evidence that “Sarah Lawrence girl” was primarily a pedagogical, regional, religious, or sexual label rather than a class label in a given period;
- student testimony showing that the stereotype was widely recognized as an outsider fantasy with little relation to campus distinctions;
- evidence that Didion’s phrase was idiosyncratic rather than drawing on an already recognizable cultural type.

## Evidence discipline

Do not answer this question by stacking quotations that all inherit the same stereotype. Keep four layers separate:

**population evidence → institutional policy → participant testimony → external representation**

The interesting object may be the gap between them.
