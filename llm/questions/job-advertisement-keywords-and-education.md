# Job-advertisement keywords are not proof of university job preparation

Recorded question: **2026-10-15**. Status: **open research question with one applicant-facing source receipt**.

## Invalid inference to test

A university or commentator might count *communication* in employer advertisements, point to its writing or humanities courses, and conclude that it teaches what employers need.

The inference does not follow from the keyword. It confuses at least five different observations:

1. **Text issued:** Someone placed the word *communication* in a hiring message or job posting.
2. **Work requirement:** The actual job demands identifiable kinds of communication in specified situations.
3. **Hiring rule:** An employer measures those abilities and uses the results in hiring, promotion, or pay.
4. **Instructional effect:** A particular course measurably improves those abilities, compared with plausible alternatives.
5. **Employment effect:** That improvement materially changes a student's access to work, pay, retention, or performance.

Each arrow requires evidence. Frequency of a word establishes at most a property of the collected advertising texts. It does not establish the remaining links.

This does **not** assert that communication is worthless or that no college course develops it. For a customer-facing mobile technician, communicating hazards, diagnoses, schedules, and repairs may be crucial. The employer's actual communication tasks and the school's effect still require examination.

## Original observation: the service-technician message

[2026-10-15 Adecco/Indeed screenshot receipt](../../education/receipts/2026-10-15-adecco-mobile-technician/receipt.txt) records a service-technician offer in the Ann Arbor/Detroit area. A card marked **“Adecco Finance and Accounting”** describes repair of trash compactors, balers, truck docks, and other equipment using electrical, hydraulic, and some welding skills. It requests **“Exceptional communication and customer service skills”** but the visible materials neither name the actual hiring company nor supply a numeric rate of pay.

The label mismatch is **not proof that an accounting firm hired a mechanic**: it may be a posting or platform category error. Nor do cropped screenshots establish that the complete original posting lacked the missing information. No evidence yet establishes whether the outreach was automated, manually composed, or reused from a template.

What the example *does* show: a direct applicant-facing communication can contain an impressive-sounding competence keyword while failing to communicate important terms clearly and carrying an occupational-category mismatch. Merely extracting the keyword would overlook these facts.

## The close-reading standard

The same analytical care expected of a literary or historical source should apply to an employer's advertisement. Ask:

- Who wrote, classified, distributed, and paid for this text: employer, staffing agency, job board, software template?
- What is the actual employment relationship and who holds the power to hire?
- Which claims are concrete and checkable (equipment, hours, wage, location), and which are slogans (“competitive,” “premier,” “excellent communication”)?
- How do the document's purpose and audience affect what it says and omits?
- Which skill claims are repeated defaults across unrelated vacancies? Which survive comparison with observed work?
- Does the recruiting institution itself meet the standard it imposes on applicants? Evaluate the **particular document and process**, not the moral character of its sender.

An advertisement is a **primary source for what was advertised**, but not automatically a primary measurement of what workers do, what employers actually select for, or the social value of a course.

## Evidence collection

| Proposition | What to observe | Insufficient substitute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A task needs communication | Technician/customer interactions; hazard reports; work orders; repair explanations; consequences of failure | Occurrence of “communication” |
| Employer evaluates the skill | Job-specific assessment, hiring rubrics, interview or probation records, hiring-manager account | A requirements bullet |
| A course improves it | Student work and comparable before/after assessments; alternative instructional route; transfer to real tasks | Course catalog label or learning outcome |
| The improvement leads to a job | Actual applicants, rejections, offers, accepted starts, wages, time to hire, retention, costs, and nonresponse | University placement advertisement |
| Advertisements faithfully represent jobs | Compare dated original ads with employer records and workers' actual tasks/terms, including mismatches and changes | Repeated boilerplate across postings |

For counts or causal estimates, define sampling frame, date, occupation, intermediary, duplication/deduplication rule, candidate population, exclusions, and missing observations. Classify *source of the words* separately from *evidence of the work*.

An adversarial control would show cases where communication is crucial and university training demonstrably improves job performance. A different control would show high keyword frequency with no corresponding hiring assessment. Neither can be decided by this single screenshot.

## Unresolved questions

- What proportion of the observed job-posting corpus consists of recruiter templates, syndication, wrong categories, or copied qualifications? **Unknown.**
- When does *communication* mean technical reporting, safety instruction, customer contact, writing, persuasion, or obedience? **Requires job-specific observations.**
- Which university programs make the particular keyword-to-employability claim? **No named university claim was supplied here. Locate original institutional statements before criticism of a named institution.**
- How often do advertisements differ from actual jobs, wages, schedules, and prerequisites? **One case cannot estimate prevalence.**
