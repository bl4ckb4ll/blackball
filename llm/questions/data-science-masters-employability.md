# Data-science master's employability versus the jobs being sold

Status: provisional question map

This file preserves a recurring user observation as a research hypothesis. It is **not** evidence that the pattern is universal or even prevalent.

## Seed observation

The user reports repeatedly seeing a sharp asymmetry between the people teaching or supervising data science and the employment prospects of students buying data-science master's degrees:

- faculty and established researchers can hold or obtain desirable data-science work;
- scarce corporate data-science roles may favor people with substantial scientific or research backgrounds, including Ph.D.s and academics leaving universities;
- a newly graduated master's student may therefore be purchasing training for a job category in which the effective competition includes much more experienced researchers;
- the price of the master's can be very high while the program itself does not assume responsibility for placing the graduate into a corresponding job;
- disappointed workers can finance another attempt through educational borrowing, producing a "Mulligan" dynamic: the first labor-market offer is bad, another credential is purchased, and the hoped-for payoff is deferred again.

Treat every bullet above as an observation or hypothesis until independently measured.

## Central question

When a university sells a professional or master's degree in data science, what employment outcome is actually being purchased, if anything?

The relevant comparison is not merely whether data-science jobs exist. It is whether graduates of the specific program obtain those jobs, in what numbers, on what time horizon, at what pay, and against what competing applicant pools.

## Candidate claims to test

1. **Faculty outcome is not student outcome.**
   A professor's consulting, research, or corporate opportunities do not establish that students in the professor's program have comparable access to those jobs.

2. **Job existence is not graduate access.**
   Employer demand for data scientists does not establish that newly graduated master's students are the people being hired.

3. **Applicant-pool composition matters.**
   Corporate data-science openings may draw applicants from Ph.D. programs, postdocs, academic science, statistics, mathematics, engineering, software, and experienced industry research. A master's-program placement claim is incomplete without showing who actually wins the jobs.

4. **Degree cost and placement responsibility are separate facts.**
   A program can charge a high price while making no enforceable promise that the student will obtain work in the advertised field.

5. **Additional schooling can function as a labor-market retry.**
   Access to educational credit may make it possible for a worker disappointed by available employment to buy another credential instead of immediately accepting the current job options. Whether this materially improves later outcomes must be measured rather than assumed.

## Required denominators

For each program and graduating cohort, recover where possible:

- number entering the program;
- number completing;
- number seeking employment;
- number employed at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months;
- number employed specifically in data-science or closely related work;
- number employed internally by the university or by firms with pre-existing university ties;
- number continuing to another degree;
- number unemployed or not reporting;
- salary distribution, not only maximum or average;
- geographic relocation required;
- total tuition and mandatory fees;
- typical borrowing and debt at completion.

Do not allow "known outcomes" to silently replace the full graduating cohort.

## Employer-side questions

For a sample of actual data-science openings and actual hires:

- What credentials did the employer require versus prefer?
- What credentials and prior occupations did the hired workers actually have?
- How many hires came directly from professional master's programs?
- How many came from Ph.D., postdoc, faculty, research-scientist, software, statistics, mathematics, engineering, or experienced analyst backgrounds?
- Was the position genuinely entry-level?
- Did the employer use a degree filter that excluded otherwise qualified applicants?
- Were internships, referrals, prior research relationships, or existing employment more predictive than the master's degree itself?

## Faculty/student asymmetry test

For departments selling data-science master's degrees, compare two separate populations:

### Faculty

- academic rank and employment security;
- consulting and industry affiliations;
- prior research career;
- industry exits;
- compensation where public;
- access to grants, laboratories, proprietary data, and professional networks.

### Students and graduates

- program cost;
- prior experience on entry;
- employment outcomes by cohort;
- job titles and employers;
- duration of search;
- debt;
- whether the job obtained actually required the degree.

Never use faculty success as a proxy for graduate success.

## Strong evidence

Prefer:

- cohort-level graduate outcome reports with explicit denominators;
- state or federal linked education-employment records;
- university disclosures that identify nonresponse and continuing education;
- employer payroll or workforce records where available;
- job-posting archives paired with evidence of who was actually hired;
- resumés/CVs or public biographies sufficient to reconstruct the prior careers of identifiable hires;
- tuition, fee, and borrowing records from primary sources.

## Weak evidence that can still be useful

- graduate Reddit threads;
- LinkedIn or personal-site career histories;
- job advertisements;
- university marketing pages;
- faculty biographies;
- anecdotes from students, instructors, recruiters, and hiring managers.

These are leads and case evidence, not prevalence estimates unless a valid denominator can be reconstructed.

## Falsifiers

Evidence against the seed observation would include programs where, over multiple cohorts:

- most graduates seeking work obtain genuine data-science jobs within a short defined period;
- employers routinely hire those graduates directly into the advertised roles;
- hired graduates do not generally need prior Ph.D.-level research or substantial industry experience;
- outcomes remain strong after counting nonresponders and excluding internal placements;
- the earnings gain relative to realistic alternatives is large enough to justify tuition, lost earnings, debt, relocation, and search risk.

## Retrieval queue

1. Identify several expensive U.S. data-science master's programs and obtain current tuition plus archived cohort outcome reports.
2. Recover full denominators instead of headline placement percentages.
3. Sample actual data-science hires at major employers and classify prior occupation and highest degree.
4. Look specifically for scientists, postdocs, and faculty who moved into corporate data science.
5. Compare those hires with recent master's graduates competing for nominally similar roles.
6. Search graduate discussions for unsuccessful searches, underemployment, and degree-regret cases, preserving dates and program names.
7. Search for programs that publish unusually strong outcome evidence as counterexamples.
8. Separate genuinely entry-level analyst work from research-scientist/data-scientist roles whose title obscures a much higher experience threshold.

## Blackball interpretation rule

The point is not to declare that "data science is unemployable." The narrower issue is whether a costly educational product is being sold by reference to an occupation without demonstrating that its own graduates reliably gain access to that occupation.

The burden of proof should stay on the institution making the employability implication. A student should not have to infer placement from the existence of successful professors, successful alumni selected without a denominator, or a growing occupational category.
