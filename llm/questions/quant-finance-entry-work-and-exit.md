# Quant finance: entering, working, and leaving the hedge-fund pipeline

Recorded question: 2026-10-14. Status: **open empirical question**.

For the measurable supply-versus-demand version, see [mathematics doctorates worldwide versus Jane Street's math-appropriate openings](mathematics-phds-versus-jane-street-roles.md). That source ledger corrects earlier U.S. counts and preserves the distinction between company-wide advertisements and verified hires.

## The distinction to protect

**Getting a hedge-fund quantitative job ≠ doing particular quantitative work ≠ being valued at work ≠ receiving a good lifetime economic return ≠ wanting to stay.**

Do not treat the selective recruitment pipeline as proof that the eventual job is desirable, that recruits' technical contributions are well regarded, or that the same route is realistically available to an outsider. Do not infer the opposite merely because some workers leave.

### First named case

[Todd W. Schneider](../../people/todd-w-schneider.md): his 2014 RailsConf speaker biography reports **six years building mortgage-backed-security valuation models at a hedge fund**; in that same 2014 recorded talk he describes himself as an engineer at Rap Genius. His 2015 mortgage research confirms in his own words prior work as a mortgage analyst. His later website lists additional software/data work. His **reason for leaving finance and its economic effect remain unverified**.

The record establishes a change of publicly described occupation, **not** disappointment, failure, burnout, firing, financial independence, or dislike of finance. “Exited the hedge-fund career track” is a narrower claim than “rejected that career.”

His *Wedding Crunchers* project offers a different line of inquiry: prestigious schools and Wall Street employers appear in an explicitly selective public announcement genre. Their appearance measures public presentation and editorial selection, not how recruits fared. **More directly, his own [2013 introduction](https://toddwschneider.com/posts/when-harvard-met-sally-n-gram-analysis-of-the-new-york-times-weddings-section/) and [2014 follow-up](https://toddwschneider.com/posts/do-you-work-for-an-investment-bank-or-the-investment-bank/) openly mock elite status display.** This supports an attitude of skepticism or satirical distance toward that *display*, while the proposition **"he left finance because he disliked elite culture" remains a live, unconfirmed hypothesis**.

### Claims requiring distinct evidence

| Question | Original observations required | Common invalid shortcut |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who gets in? | Named recruits, dated offers, actual recruiting criteria, unsuccessful candidates, cohort denominator | Ivy/PhD prestige or placement advertising |
| What work is done? | Code/model descriptions, desk mandate, deliverables, who relies on them, outcomes | “Quant,” “risk,” or “hedge fund” job title |
| Is the work respected? | Specific decisions that adopt/ignore quantitative advice, reporting lines, authority, compensation/promotion decisions, direct testimony with context | Assumed respect because the job is paid or elite |
| Was the job worth it? | Actual pay, hours, termination risk, skill transfer, wealth and health effects, alternatives, career continuation | Gross compensation, one winner's story, or institutional brand |
| Why did workers leave? | First-person account or original termination/transition record, date, next job, opportunity set | Reading a motive out of a LinkedIn-style résumé |
| What happened to applicants who did not get in? | Complete applicant/finalist cohort, rejections, subsequent jobs, net outcomes and nonresponse | Published biographies of successful entrants |

## Selection effects and falsifiers

Public biographies overrepresent people who later found noteworthy careers. Finance recruiting brochures overrepresent placements; later career-switch stories may overrepresent people with a financially secure exit. Those selection mechanisms must be **measured or marked unknown**, not presumed as numerical rates.

Evidence that some recruits stay for decades with measurable technical contribution and favorable compensation would matter; so would documented cases of exits, underemployment, low decision authority, or unsuccessful candidates. A credible conclusion about the pipeline requires comparable cohorts and explicit missing denominators.

Do not answer an individual's question “Will this produce an actual earning opportunity for me?” with the story of someone else's prestigious hire. Record specific open entry paths, required prior experience, costs, access restrictions, pay, and probabilities only when supported.
