# Jeff Sharlet — Dartmouth graduates into finance and consulting

## Why this belongs in Blackball

This is a compact institutional case for questions about elite higher education, occupational pipelines, prestige, recruiting, and class reproduction.

The useful distinction is between:

1. the **measured outcome** — where Dartmouth graduates actually went;
2. **Sharlet's interpretation** of that outcome as a Dartmouth writing professor;
3. **student-side mechanisms** proposed in contemporaneous Dartmouth reporting; and
4. broader conclusions about universities or class, which require more evidence than this one case supplies.

## Claim ledger

### Claim: 49% of Dartmouth's Class of 2023 worked in finance or consulting in their first fall after graduation

**Status:** directly reported by *The Dartmouth*, attributed there to Dartmouth's Center for Professional Development.

Andrew Zuo, writing in *The Dartmouth* on September 20, 2024, reported that the Center for Professional Development found that **49% of the Dartmouth Class of 2023 worked in finance or consulting roles during their first fall after graduation**.

Source:

- Andrew Zuo, "Why Do Dartmouth Students Love Finance and Consulting?", *The Dartmouth*, September 20, 2024: https://www.thedartmouth.com/article/2024/09/zuo-why-do-dartmouth-students-love-finance-and-consulting

**Evidence boundary:** the newspaper article attributes the statistic to Dartmouth's Center for Professional Development. This dossier has not yet recovered the underlying CPD table/report, so do not silently upgrade the newspaper's attribution into an inspected primary dataset.

### Claim: Jeff Sharlet publicly described the 49% figure as a "crushing statistic"

**Status:** strong contemporaneous reproduction; original X/Twitter URL not yet recovered here.

Contemporaneous reproductions dated September 23–25, 2024 identify a Jeff Sharlet post stating: "49% of the class of '23 at Dartmouth ... went into finance or consulting." Sharlet called the result a **"crushing statistic"** and objected to so much energy, education and intellect being concentrated in one sector.

## Mechanism proposed by Andrew Zuo

Zuo's September 2024 article is opinion, not a causal study, but it is useful first-person evidence from inside the recruiting environment.

He argues that finance and consulting recruit unusually early and offer a structured path to internships, return offers, high pay, prestige and early job security. On his account, students who are still undecided can face a timing problem: commit to the finance/consulting recruiting process early or risk missing it while exploring other fields. He explicitly contrasts the variety of interests students arrive with against the large number who later enter banking or consulting.

This is evidence for a possible **pipeline / option-closing mechanism**, not proof that the recruiting calendar causes the 49% outcome.

## Later Dartmouth corroboration / follow-up

These are later opinion pieces and should be treated as interpretations, but they provide useful additional measurements and campus testimony:

- Preston James, "Dartmouth's Wall Street Plague", *The Dartmouth*, February 20, 2025: https://www.thedartmouth.com/article/2025/02/james-dartmouths-wall-street-plague-2-20
  - reports that 33% of the 2023 class worked in finance alone in the first fall after graduation, attributing the figure to the Center for Professional Development;
  - contrasts this with a 2006 graduating-senior survey in which 26% planned to enter financial services.

- Eli Moyse, "Dartmouth Serves Wall Street, Not the World", *The Dartmouth*, April 17, 2025: https://www.thedartmouth.com/article/2025/04/moyse-dartmouth-serves-wall-street-not-the-world
  - cites the 2023 Cap and Gown Survey as finding 46% of graduates working in finance or business/management consulting.

These percentages describe slightly different categories, instruments and/or timepoints. Do **not** merge 49%, 46% and 33% as though they were competing estimates of one identical quantity.

## What this case can support

Reasonably:

- Dartmouth had an unusually concentrated post-graduation pipeline into finance and consulting around 2023.
- A Dartmouth creative-writing professor publicly objected to that concentration.
- A Dartmouth student writer described early recruiting, job security, pay, prestige and social comparison as mechanisms that can pull undecided students toward those sectors.
- The case is relevant to questions about whether an elite liberal-arts university also functions as a recruiting and allocation interface for high-status business occupations.

Not by itself:

- that Dartmouth education causes students to prefer finance;
- that finance/consulting work has no social value;
- that all Ivy League institutions have the same outcome;
- that graduates would have chosen different work absent Dartmouth;
- that the mechanism is purely class reproduction rather than pay, debt, risk aversion, peer effects, employer selection, preferences, family background, or other causes.

## Questions this should be linked into

- Why are banking, finance and consulting recruiting channels so strongly attached to elite universities?
- How much of an elite university's labor-market value is instruction versus selection, prestige, network and recruiting access?
- When students enter with broad academic interests but converge on a narrow set of occupations, what mechanisms produce the convergence?
- How early recruiting changes the feasible choice set for students.
- Whether liberal-arts education and elite occupational allocation are complementary functions of the same institution.
