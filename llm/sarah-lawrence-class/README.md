# Sarah Lawrence as a class and cultural type

This dossier tracks the phrase and image of the **“Sarah Lawrence girl”** as a historically changing class/cultural type rather than treating it as a demographic fact about all Sarah Lawrence students.

The central question is not “what are Sarah Lawrence students like?” It is:

> **What social work has the figure of the “Sarah Lawrence girl” done in American writing and criticism, especially around wealth, cultural capital, femininity, bohemianism, and Salinger?**

The evidence already shows several different objects being collapsed under one name. Keep them separate.

## Working claims

### 1. The type begins with wealth, gender, and institutional purpose

Sarah Lawrence’s own archives preserve founding material in which William Van Duzer Lawrence imagined a distinct “woman’s sphere” and a college specifically designed for women. A contemporary *Time* article in December 1926 described Lawrence as worried that educated women were avoiding what he considered their “real career”: marriage.

This is not the same object as the later bohemian “Sarah Lawrence girl,” but it is part of the genealogy. The college began as an expensive women’s institution embedded in a strongly gendered theory of education.

**Evidence state:** institutional archive material and contemporary journalism located; founding documents should be read in full before stronger claims are made about curriculum or student life.

### 2. Economic privilege could be rhetorically converted into internal “classlessness”

Paul S. Cowan and L. Geoffrey Cowan’s 1963 *Harvard Crimson* profile opens by calling Sarah Lawrence a **“classless community for the very rich.”** The article describes a high-tuition institution with few scholarships while emphasizing the absence of grades, clubs, faculty hierarchy, and other visible status distinctions inside the college.

That is analytically useful: an institution can suppress some *internal* status markers while remaining highly selective by wealth and background at the entrance boundary.

The same article constructs a recognizable “Sarah Lawrence girl” as intellectually self-conscious, socially sophisticated, and closer in aspiration to Doris Lessing and Simone de Beauvoir than to conventional elite femininity. This is journalism producing a type, not a representative-sample measurement of students.

### 3. The institution also had exclusionary admissions practices

Sarah Lawrence’s archival finding aids explicitly identify records concerning **Jewish student quotas**. The diversity research guide points to Board of Trustees minutes mentioning a Jewish quota in 1929, 1933, 1936, and 1942, and to a presidential admissions file covering 1932–1956 that was restricted until 2026.

This matters to the class story because elite institutional identity was not generated by wealth alone; admissions boundaries also involved ethnicity/religion and ideas about what kind of student population the college wanted.

**Evidence state:** archive guides inspected; the underlying quota records have not yet been read into Blackball. Since the stated restriction ran through 2026, these are now a priority retrieval target.

### 4. By the postwar period, “Sarah Lawrence girl” had become a cultural-capital type

The 1963 *Crimson* portrait and later references use Sarah Lawrence less as a literal college name than as shorthand for a recognizable social formation: affluent or upper-middle-class, highly literate, aesthetically self-conscious, unconventional, and female.

That formation is what makes Joan Didion’s 1961 description of *Franny and Zooey* legible. Her point is not that Salinger literally wrote only for Sarah Lawrence students. She uses “Sarah Lawrence girls” to identify an audience whose spiritual and intellectual dissatisfaction is itself class-positioned.

### 5. Salinger criticism makes the class connection explicit

In “Finally (Fashionably) Spurious,” Joan Didion calls *Franny and Zooey* self-help for the upper middle classes and ends with the “Sarah Lawrence girls” formulation.

Grace Byron returns to this line in her 2024 Los Angeles Review of Books essay on Salinger. Byron explicitly reads the Glass family as upper-class, treats Holden’s freedom to indulge ennui as materially enabled, and connects Salinger’s cultivated precocity to race and class privilege.

These are **critical interpretations**, not neutral descriptions of Salinger’s characters. They belong beside direct readings of *The Catcher in the Rye*, “Uncle Wiggily in Connecticut,” “Franny,” and “Zooey.”

### 6. First-person accounts complicate the rich-girl stereotype rather than simply confirming it

Porochista Khakpour’s 2018 memoir excerpt recalls arriving at Sarah Lawrence in 1996 as a scholarship student. She calls it a typical rich-girl liberal-arts college and remembers students telling her that their fathers were paying for her education.

That is unusually direct evidence of **class consciousness experienced from inside the institution**, but it is one person’s recollection of one period. Do not inflate it into a demographic claim without admissions/financial-aid data.

### 7. Jamie Jordan explicitly separates two historical “Sarah Lawrence girl” types

Jamie Jordan’s MFA work *Ghosts & Dreams* (submitted December 2023) gives a useful internal genealogy. Jordan distinguishes an early **“Lawrence girl”**—wealthy, white, WASP, and educated within the founder’s gendered marriage ideal—from a later **“Sarah Lawrence girl”** associated with intellectual/artistic independence and figures such as Carly Simon, Yoko Ono, and Lesley Gore.

Jordan’s formulation is valuable because it makes the changing type explicit. It is also memoir/creative nonfiction, and several embedded historical claims need to be followed upstream rather than cited as if Jordan were the final archival authority.

### 8. Sexual nonconformity became part of the public stereotype by the 1970s

Jordan points to Anne Roiphe’s 1977 *New York Times Magazine* article “The Trouble at Sarah Lawrence,” which framed visible lesbian life on campus as a threat to the college’s attractiveness. Later histories of American newspaper treatment of gay people treat the article as an example of openly homophobic institutional journalism.

The article therefore belongs in this dossier not as a trustworthy demographic account, but as evidence of how “Sarah Lawrence” was being publicly coded: elite, female, intellectual, sexually unconventional, and culturally threatening to some observers.

## Relation to Salinger

The useful connection is narrower than “Sarah Lawrence students are like Salinger characters.”

A better sequence is:

**elite women’s college → wealth plus cultivated individuality → bohemian/intellectual female type → postwar “Sarah Lawrence girl” shorthand → Didion uses that shorthand to class-position Salinger’s spiritual crisis**

This is why *Franny and Zooey* belongs in the dossier. It also provides context for reading class in *The Catcher in the Rye* and “Uncle Wiggily in Connecticut”: Salinger repeatedly gives characters substantial economic/cultural resources and then centers their disgust with the social world that produced those resources.

## Guardrails

- Never treat “Sarah Lawrence girl” as a timeless or statistically representative description of Sarah Lawrence students.
- Separate **institutional history**, **student/alumna testimony**, **journalistic stereotype**, and **literary-critical shorthand**.
- Keep year and source owner attached to every claim.
- Do not turn an internal rhetoric of “classlessness” into evidence that economic class disappeared.
- Do not use Jordan, Didion, Cowan/Cowan, or Khakpour as substitutes for admissions records when the question is demographic.
- For Salinger, distinguish claims supported by the fiction from claims made by critics about the fiction.
- Do not mirror copyrighted Salinger stories, Didion’s article, Khakpour’s memoir excerpt, Byron’s essay, or Jordan’s full MFA manuscript. Preserve citations, summaries, and legitimate access routes.
