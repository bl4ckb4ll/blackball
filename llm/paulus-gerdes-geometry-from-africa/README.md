## What the book is

Gerdes studies mathematical structure embedded in African cultural practices and artifacts and then asks how those structures can be explored mathematically and pedagogically. The book is not simply a catalog of decorative motifs. It repeatedly moves from an artifact or practice to a mathematical reconstruction: symmetry, dissection, polygons, polyhedra, area, volume, graph-like paths, algorithms, number patterns, and construction methods.

The book also matters methodologically. Gerdes is arguing that mathematical ideas can be reconstructed from culturally embedded practices rather than starting from the conventional European theorem sequence and then looking for illustrations afterward.

## Mathematical material

Examples discussed in the book/reviews include:

- textiles and woven strip patterns;
- basketry and plaiting;
- knotwork;
- architecture and house construction;
- ceramics;
- metalwork;
- carving;
- mural and body decoration;
- symmetry and rotational symmetry;
- squares, rectangles, right triangles, and dissections;
- the Pythagorean theorem;
- Latin-square-like constructions;
- polygons and polyhedra;
- area and volume;
- fullerenes and pentagon/hexagon structures;
- Chokwe/Lunda **sona/lusona** sand drawings;
- Euler-trail-like one-line drawings, arithmetic patterns, fractal/repetition ideas, and polyomino-style structures.

The Google Books index terms make the geographical/cultural breadth visible: Angola, Mozambique, Congo/Zaire, Cameroon, Nigeria, Côte d'Ivoire, Ashanti/Akan, Kuba/Bushoong, Mangbetu, Chokwe/Lunda, Tellem, Bassari and others.

- John D. Barrow review, Plus/University of Cambridge archive: https://plus.maths.org/old-files/issue15/reviews/book2/2pdf/index.html/op.pdf

Reviewers emphasize the book's unusual combination of mathematics education, African cultural diversity, and careful visual examples. Richard Kitchen has described it as a particularly comprehensive English-language volume on African ethnomathematics; other reviews also note that some classroom connections are more convincing than others. That is worth preserving: the mathematics reconstructed from artifacts and the pedagogical claims are related but not identical questions.

## Source graph and thanks

Gerdes's work depends on African artisans, builders, weavers, drawers, carvers, designers, storytellers, teachers, and communities whose practices supply the mathematical objects being analyzed. Blackball should not flatten those contributors into “Gerdes invented this geometry.” His contribution is often the reconstruction, formalization, comparison, and educational presentation of mathematics already embodied in practices and designs.

Particularly important source domains to preserve:

- **Chokwe/Lunda sona or lusona sand-drawing traditions**;
- Mozambican basketry, weaving, house-building, and craft practice;
- geometric designs documented in African textiles, carving, ceramics, body art, architecture, and metalwork;
- oral traditions associated with designs and drawing algorithms;
- photographs, museum objects, ethnographic reports, and earlier documentation of African design traditions.

The Google Books searchable terms also expose names such as **Fontinha** and **Coart** attached to drawings/documentation; the full bibliography should be extracted from the book before assigning exact intellectual credit.

Gerdes himself wrote a much larger body of work on African mathematics, often in Portuguese, French, and German. *Geometry from Africa* is therefore an English-language entry point, not the whole project.
