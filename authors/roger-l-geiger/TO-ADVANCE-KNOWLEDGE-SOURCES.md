## Access and copying

Internet Archive has the full 1986 Oxford scan, but the item is `Access-restricted-item: true` / `printdisabled`; its metadata places the notes/bibliography at roughly pp. 279–320. That is excellent evidence that a large source apparatus exists, but it is **not permission to copy the scan or its restricted OCR into this repository**.

Open Library exposes the 2004 Transaction edition as **Preview Only**. A legitimate publisher preview of that reissue is also publicly exposed through the Taylor & Francis/Routledge preview infrastructure. Blackball can use those preview pages to verify Geiger's 2003 retrospective introduction and its notes, while leaving the copyrighted book itself where the publisher put it.

# Geiger's explicit intellectual debt

## Laurence R. Veysey — *The Emergence of the American University*

Geiger says directly that his historical thinking was indebted to **Laurence R. Veysey**, *The Emergence of the American University* (University of Chicago Press, 1965). He then explains where his own account differs from Veysey's interpretation.

This is stronger evidence than a reviewer saying two books resemble one another: **Geiger himself identifies Veysey as an intellectual debt**.

Thank you to Laurence R. Veysey for providing a major interpretive framework that Geiger could inherit, criticize, and revise.

# Scholarship Geiger says changed the field after 1986

Geiger's reissue introduction is unusually useful because he explicitly surveys research that appeared after *To Advance Knowledge* was first published. He names the scholars rather than presenting his revised understanding as if it arose from nowhere.

## Institutional histories

Thank you to:

- **Robert A. McCaughey** — *Stand, Columbia: A History of Columbia University in the City of New York, 1754–2004*. Geiger identifies McCaughey's Columbia history as part of the richer institutional scholarship available by the reissue.
- **Morton Keller and Phyllis Keller** — *Making Harvard Modern: The Rise of America's University*.
- **Mary Ann Dzuback** — *Robert M. Hutchins: Portrait of an Educator*.
- **William H. McNeill** — *Hutchins' University: A Memoir of the University of Chicago, 1929–1950*.
- **Winton U. Solberg** — *The University of Illinois, 1894–1904: The Shaping of the University*.
- **John Aubrey Douglass** — *The California Idea and American Higher Education: 1850 to the 1960 Master Plan*.

These works matter because a synthesis of “the American research university” depends on detailed histories of particular institutions. The general picture is built out of work done at the local level.

## Academic disciplines, professions, and intellectual life

Thank you to:

- **Julie A. Reuben** — *The Making of the Modern University: Intellectual Transformation and the Marginalization of Morality*.
- **Jon H. Roberts and James Turner** — *The Sacred and the Secular University*.
- **Peter Novick** — *That Noble Dream: The “Objectivity Question” and the American Historical Profession*.
- **Gerald Graff** — *Professing Literature: An Institutional History*.
- **Andrew Abbott** — *Department and Discipline: Chicago Sociology at One Hundred*.
- **Ellen Condliffe Lagemann** — *An Elusive Science: The Troubling History of Educational Research*.
- **Kenneth M. Ludmerer** — *Learning to Heal: The Development of American Medical Education* and *Time to Heal: American Medical Education from the Turn of the Century to the Era of Managed Care*.

This is one of the most useful source clusters for Blackball. Departments and professional schools did not simply appear as fixed boxes. Historians have reconstructed how disciplines acquired authority, how professions built educational systems, and how ideas about what counted as legitimate knowledge changed.

## Science, foundations, philanthropy, and university research

Thank you to:

- **Robert E. Kohler** — *Partners in Science: Foundations and Natural Scientists, 1900–1945*.
- **Ellen Condliffe Lagemann** — *The Politics of Knowledge: The Carnegie Corporation, Philanthropy, and Public Policy*.
- **David C. Mowery and Nathan Rosenberg** — *Technology and the Pursuit of Economic Growth*.
- **John P. Swann** — *Academic Scientists and the Pharmaceutical Industry: Cooperative Research in Twentieth-Century America*.
- **Hugh Hawkins** — *Banding Together: The Rise of National Associations in American Higher Education, 1887–1950*.

These scholars make visible institutions that disappear if the story is reduced to professors and universities alone: foundations, corporations, professional associations, research sponsors, and organized relationships between university science and industry.

## Students, gender, athletics, and collegiate life

Thank you to:

- **Lynn D. Gordon** — *Gender and Higher Education in the Progressive Era*.
- **W. Bruce Leslie** — *Gentlemen and Scholars: College and Community in the “Age of the University,” 1865–1917*.
- **Ronald A. Smith** — *Sports and Freedom: The Rise of Big-Time College Athletics*.

Their work helps keep students, women, community life, and athletics from being treated as decorative material around a supposedly self-contained history of research.

## Quantitative history of the expansion of higher education

Thank you to:

- **Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz** — “The Shaping of Higher Education: The Formative Years in the United States, 1890–1940,” NBER Working Paper 6537 (1998).
- **Susan R. Richardson** — “Research Note: State Higher Education Database, 1870–1965,” *History of Higher Education Annual* 22 (2002).

This is especially valuable provenance: Geiger's institutional narrative also depends on people assembling historical quantitative evidence and datasets.

# Reviews

## Helen Lefkowitz Horowitz — *Journal of American History*

Horowitz's 1987 review treats the book as a major analytic contribution and values its detailed reconstruction of individual universities and research systems, while also arguing that its institutional analysis can make the people inside those systems feel overly abstract.

Citation: **Helen Lefkowitz Horowitz**, review of *To Advance Knowledge*, *Journal of American History* 74, no. 3 (December 1987): 1078–1079. DOI: 10.2307/1902215.

Thank you to Horowitz for supplying criticism rather than only publicity. A source trail should preserve the arguments people made *against* a book as well as the endorsements printed on it.

# What remains in the original 1986 notes

The original Internet Archive metadata reports the source apparatus at approximately pp. 279–320. The publisher preview only exposes the reissue introduction, not those forty-plus pages of original notes.

So the current state is:

- **reissue-introduction source graph:** substantial and directly verified;
- **original 1986 source graph:** known to be large, but not yet extracted wholesale because the IA scan is controlled.

The next lawful routes are Google Books snippets, publisher previews, reviews that quote specific notes, library digitizations with legitimate search-inside, and individual source searches anchored to an exact Geiger page/note. Do not substitute a random pirate scan merely to make the extraction faster.
