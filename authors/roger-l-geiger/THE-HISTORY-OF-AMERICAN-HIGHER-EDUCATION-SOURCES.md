## What Geiger is synthesizing

The book runs from the founding of Harvard in 1636 through World War II. The organizing problem is not simply institutional chronology. Geiger repeatedly asks how higher education combined and rebalanced three purposes:

- **knowledge** — what was known, investigated, taught, and made academically authoritative;
- **careers** — the occupations, professions, and social positions college prepared people to enter;
- **culture** — the formation of values, identities, manners, religion, citizenship, and collegiate life.

The source graph underneath that synthesis should be visible rather than hidden behind the single name “Roger L. Geiger.”

### Charles W. Eliot — the creation of American universities

Chapter 8, “The Creation of American Universities,” opens with **Charles W. Eliot's** 1869 statement that a genuine American university could not simply be transplanted from England or Germany.

Eliot is a primary historical source and historical actor rather than a later historian. He belongs in the gratitude/source graph for a different reason: Geiger's history depends on the words and records left by people who participated in building the institutions being described.

## Earlier scholarship reported as important to Geiger's synthesis

The entries in this section are **not yet being mislabeled as direct bibliography extractions**. They are separated because review literature gives useful evidence about Geiger's intellectual debts while the controlled scan prevents a full note-by-note extraction.

### Laurence R. Veysey — *The Emergence of the American University*

Review discussion of Geiger's book identifies **Laurence R. Veysey's** *The Emergence of the American University* (1965) as one of the books Geiger found especially useful. Veysey's analysis of competing conceptions of the university is an obvious predecessor to later attempts—including Geiger's—to explain how different purposes coexist and struggle inside institutions.

Thank you to Veysey for a work that subsequent historians of American higher education repeatedly have had to argue with, revise, and build upon.

**Status:** important intellectual debt reported in review literature; direct note/bibliography locator still to recover.

### David B. Potts — nineteenth-century colleges

Review discussion specifically describes Geiger's treatment of the Yale Reports and nineteenth-century liberal education in relation to **David B. Potts**, including Potts's *Liberal Education for a Land of Colleges* (2010).

Potts is also a contributor to Geiger's edited *The American College in the Nineteenth Century*. His scholarship clearly belongs in the surrounding historical research tradition.

**Status:** use by Geiger reported in review discussion; exact note locator still to recover.

### Bernard Bailyn — education in early American society

A *Journal of American History* review places Geiger in a scholarly tradition associated with **Bernard Bailyn**, especially *Education in the Forming of American Society*. That is important historiographical context, but Blackball will not call it a verified Geiger citation until the original note/bibliography is checked.

## Why the distinction matters

There are three different edges in a source graph:

1. **Geiger directly cites X.**
2. **Geiger explicitly acknowledges an intellectual debt to X.**
3. **A reviewer says X is an important predecessor or comparison.**

Those are not interchangeable. Blackball should preserve all three, but label them accurately.
