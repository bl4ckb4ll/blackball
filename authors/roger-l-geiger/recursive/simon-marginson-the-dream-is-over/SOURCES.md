# Simon Marginson — *The Dream Is Over* — recursive source trail

**Why this is here:** Roger L. Geiger directly cites Simon Marginson's *The Dream Is Over: The Crisis of Clark Kerr's California Idea of Higher Education* in *American Higher Education since World War II*. Blackball's source graph therefore continues one generation backward through Marginson.

**Book:** *The Dream Is Over: The Crisis of Clark Kerr's California Idea of Higher Education*  
**Author:** Simon Marginson  
**Publisher:** University of California Press / Luminos  
**Publication:** 2016

## Clark Kerr — *The Uses of the University*

Marginson calls **Clark Kerr's *The Uses of the University*** one of the three pathbreaking University of California contributions to the common understanding of higher education. He reconstructs the book from Kerr's 1963 Godkin Lectures through later editions and uses it as one of the main intellectual foundations of *The Dream Is Over*.

Thank you to **Clark Kerr** not only for administering institutions but for trying to describe the organizational object he had helped create: the “multiversity.” Later historians and sociologists did not have to invent that contemporary self-description after the fact because Kerr left it in print.

**Direct Marginson citation:** verified in open chapter 4, “The Uses of the University,” pp. 21–27.

---

## Martin Trow — “Problems in the Transition from Elite to Mass Higher Education”

Marginson identifies **Martin Trow's** 1973 OECD essay, **“Problems in the Transition from Elite to Mass Higher Education,”** as the second pathbreaking contribution. Trow's elite → mass → universal framework gives Marginson a way to discuss the expansion of participation across countries and over time.

Thank you to **Martin Trow** for taking institutional growth seriously enough to ask whether an education system changes *kind*, not merely size, as participation expands. That distinction became part of the conceptual equipment of later higher-education history and sociology.

Thank you also to the **Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)**, which supplied the institutional setting in which the essay was prepared and circulated.

**Direct Marginson citation:** verified in open chapter 5, “Martin Trow: Higher Education and Its Growth,” pp. 28–35.

---

## Burton R. Clark — *The Higher Education System*

Marginson identifies **Burton R. “Bob” Clark's *The Higher Education System* (1983)** as the most important of Clark's major books and papers and the third of the three California contributions around which the early part of Marginson's book is organized.

Thank you to **Burton R. Clark** for treating higher education as an organizational system with its own structures, divisions of authority, professional groups, and “academic heartland,” rather than assuming a university could be understood by borrowing the organization chart of a corporation or government agency.

This recursive edge is especially important because Geiger himself also explicitly acknowledges Clark as a long-term intellectual influence in *Knowledge and Money*. The graph therefore contains two independent paths back to Clark:

- Geiger → Burton R. Clark;
- Geiger → Marginson → Burton R. Clark.

That convergence is evidence about the shape of the literature, not duplicate clutter.

**Direct Marginson citation:** verified in open chapter 6, “Bob Clark: The Academic Heartland,” pp. 36–39.

## Sheldon Rothblatt

In the open chapter on Clark Kerr, Marginson directly invokes **Sheldon Rothblatt**, who in turn invokes Matthew Arnold when describing Kerr as someone who could see life clearly and whole.

Thank you to **Sheldon Rothblatt** for preserving and interpreting the intellectual character of the university and the people who shaped it. Thank you also to **Matthew Arnold**, whose language survives here through an explicitly visible chain of quotation and reuse.

**Status:** direct Marginson citation to Rothblatt is verified; the exact Rothblatt work/note is not yet normalized. Do not invent the title merely because Rothblatt wrote extensively on universities.

## Arthur Levine

Marginson directly credits **Arthur Levine** with a four-part characterization of Kerr's achievement: modernizing the public research university, replicating it across the University of California system, formulating the multiversity, and placing it within a differentiated higher-education system.

Thank you to **Arthur Levine** for an interpretation concrete enough that Marginson could state what Kerr's institutional achievement consisted of rather than simply calling it “important.”

**Status:** direct Marginson citation verified in open chapter 3; exact Levine source still to normalize from Marginson's note apparatus.

---

## Kenneth J. Arrow — *Social Choice and Individual Values*

Marginson's chapter on the “impossibility of public good” opens with **Kenneth J. Arrow's** work on social choice and the problem of deriving collectively rational decisions from individual preferences.

Thank you to **Kenneth Arrow** for formulating a result powerful enough to escape economics and become part of later arguments about democracy, collective choice, public policy, and the conceptual limits of aggregating private preferences.

Marginson uses Arrow historically: the point is not that a theorem mechanically determines higher-education policy, but that a different language of public action and individual choice was being developed while California's public higher-education compact was being built.

**Direct Marginson citation:** verified in open chapter 17. The standard bibliographic object is *Social Choice and Individual Values* (1951); the exact edition in Marginson's reference list should still be checked before edition-specific metadata is asserted.

## Charles Holcombe

Marginson's chapter “Enter the Dragon” explicitly begins from **Charles Holcombe's** characterization of East Asia as a culturally and historically coherent region rather than a merely geographic collection of countries.

Thank you to **Charles Holcombe** for the regional historical work on which Marginson relies before turning to the growth of East Asian higher education and science.

**Status:** direct Marginson citation verified; exact cited Holcombe title remains to normalize from the notes/references.

## Kishore Mahbubani

In comparing China and the United States, Marginson explicitly quotes **Kishore Mahbubani** on the tension between China's politically closed institutions and its willingness to learn from outside.

Thank you to **Kishore Mahbubani** for providing one of the contemporary interpretations Marginson tests against the observable international behavior of Chinese universities.

**Status:** direct Marginson citation verified; exact cited work remains to normalize.

---

## Joseph E. Stiglitz — *The Price of Inequality*

Marginson's final chapter explicitly invokes **Joseph E. Stiglitz's *The Price of Inequality*** when discussing the continuing but unequal protective value of higher education in a society with widening economic inequality.

Thank you to **Joseph E. Stiglitz** for supplying economic evidence and interpretation that Marginson can put beside the institutional history. Whether one accepts every part of Stiglitz's explanation or not, the source belongs visibly in the chain rather than disappearing into uncited statements about “the economy.”

**Direct Marginson citation:** verified in open chapter 22, “Higher Education and Society,” pp. 178–192.
