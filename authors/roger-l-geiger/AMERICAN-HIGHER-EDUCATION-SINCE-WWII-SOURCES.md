# Roger L. Geiger — *American Higher Education since World War II* — source trail

**Book:** *American Higher Education since World War II: A History*  
**Author:** Roger L. Geiger  
**Publisher:** Princeton University Press  
**Copyright:** 2019, Princeton University Press  
**ISBN:** 978-0-691-17972-8  
**JSTOR:** https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv9hvtbz

## Access and copying

A complete 397-page PDF is publicly reachable through the Institute for Higher Education Research at Martin Luther University Halle-Wittenberg:

https://www.hof.uni-halle.de/documents/t2550.pdf

The PDF itself says **Copyright © 2019 Princeton University Press — All Rights Reserved**. Public web access is not an open-content license. Blackball therefore uses this copy to verify the book's notes and acknowledgments but **does not mirror the PDF**.

This is exactly the distinction Blackball wants to preserve: *we can follow the scholarship without pretending that access equals permission to republish the book*.

## Why this source trail matters

Geiger's book is a synthesis. Its value does not come from one historian inventing the history from nothing. He is assembling decades of historical research, sociology, institutional studies, government reports, memoirs, quantitative work, and earlier syntheses. The citations below are therefore part of the intellectual object.

This is a **first extraction**, not the finished bibliography. The completion target is every identifiable cited author and collective author in the book.

# Verified cited scholars and works

## General education and liberal education after World War II

Thank you to the scholars and committees whose work lets Geiger reconstruct the postwar argument over what a college education was supposed to contain:

- **Nelson Lyons** — “A Further Bibliography on General Education,” *Journal of General Education* 4, no. 1 (October 1949): 72–80. Geiger uses the bibliography while reconstructing the extraordinary contemporary literature on general education.
- **Bruce A. Kimball** — *Orators and Philosophers*. Geiger cites Kimball for the historical forms and traditions behind liberal education.
- **James B. Conant** — *My Several Lives*. Conant is also a historical actor in the narrative: as Harvard president he commissioned the faculty committee on general education.
- **Paul H. Buck and the Harvard Committee** — *General Education in a Free Society: Report of the Harvard Committee* (Harvard University, 1945), the famous “Redbook.” Thank you to the whole committee rather than reducing a committee report to Conant alone.
- **Gerald Grant and David Riesman** — *The Perpetual Dream: Reform and Experiment in the American College* (1978), used in Geiger's account of curricular reform and the recurring effort to remake undergraduate education.

The point here is particularly important for Blackball: debates about curricula and required reading have a history. A syllabus is downstream from older arguments about specialization, common learning, liberal education, majors, electives, survey courses, and professional education.

## The academic revolution and the research university

- **Christopher Jencks and David Riesman** — *The Academic Revolution* (1968). Geiger treats their book as a contemporary attempt to describe the transformation of American higher education in the 1960s: academic departments, graduate education, research, stratification, and the power of the professoriate.
- **Rakesh Khurana** — *From Higher Aims to Hired Hands: The Social Transformation of American Business Schools and the Unfulfilled Promise of Management as a Profession* (Princeton University Press, 2007). Geiger cites Khurana while discussing what happened when disciplinary scholars and research norms transformed professional schools.
- **Allan M. Cartter** — *An Assessment of Quality in Graduate Education* (1966), among the sources Geiger uses for graduate education and the academic labor market.
- **Richard M. Freeland** — *Academia's Golden Age*. Geiger draws on Freeland's institutional histories while describing universities upgrading faculties, departments, graduate programs, and research capacity.
- **Oliver Fulton and Martin Trow** — “Research Activity in American Higher Education,” in Martin Trow, ed., *Teachers and Students: Aspects of American Higher Education* (1975), pp. 39–83.
- **Clark Kerr** — *The Uses of the University* (1963), a primary contemporary interpretation of the multiversity and a recurrent source throughout postwar higher-education history.

## Mass higher education, institutional quality, and differentiation

- **Andrew M. Greeley** — *From Backwater to Mainstream: A Profile of Catholic Higher Education* (1969). Geiger uses Greeley's study while describing Catholic universities' movement into the academic mainstream.
- **Frank Hamilton Bowles and Frank A. DeCosta** — *Between Two Worlds: A Profile of Negro Higher Education* (1971), a Carnegie Commission study used by Geiger for evidence on Black enrollment and institutional opportunity.
- **Anthony O. Edmonds and E. Bruce Geelhoed** — *Ball State University: An Interpretive History*, used in Geiger's reconstruction of the transformation of state teachers colleges and regional universities.
- **W. Bruce Leslie and Kenneth P. O'Brien** — “The Surprising History of the Post-WWII State Teachers College,” another source for the transformation of teacher-training institutions into broader colleges and universities.
- **Steven Brint and Jerome Karabel** — *The Diverted Dream*, an important history and sociology of community colleges and stratification.

## California and the public-university system

- **Clark Kerr** — also *The Blue and the Gold: A Personal Memoir of the University of California, 1949–1967*, vol. 1. Kerr appears simultaneously as historical actor, institutional president, and retrospective source.
- **John Aubrey Douglass** — *The California Idea and American Higher Education: 1850 to the 1960 Master Plan* (Stanford University Press, 2000), a major history of the California system.
- **John Aubrey Douglass** — *The Conditions for Admission: Access, Equity, and the Social Contract of Public Universities* (Stanford University Press, 2007), explicitly used in Geiger's account of California admissions and affirmative action.
- **Sheldon Rothblatt**, editor — *The OECD, the Master Plan and the California Dream*, part of the source trail for the Master Plan and the international influence of California's public-higher-education model.
- **Simon Marginson** — *The Dream Is Over: The Crisis of Clark Kerr's California Idea of Higher Education* (University of California Press, 2016). This book is itself open access through UC Press's Luminos program, making it a particularly useful downstream Blackball source that can be linked in full lawfully.

## Coeducation, admissions, selectivity, and elite reproduction

- **Nancy Weiss Malkiel** — *“Keep the Damned Women Out”: The Struggle for Coeducation*. Geiger uses Malkiel in reconstructing the move to coeducation at elite institutions.
- **Jerome Karabel** — *The Chosen*, a major source on admissions and the construction of selectivity at Harvard, Yale, and Princeton.
- **Elizabeth A. Duffy and Idana Goldberg** — *Crafting a Class*, another source on selective-college admissions.
- **Joseph A. Soares** — *The Power of Privilege: Yale and America's Elite Colleges*, used in the history of elite admissions and social selection.
- **John Aubrey Douglass** — *The Conditions for Admission*, again, for public-university admissions, access, race, and the political consequences of affirmative-action policy.

## Race, Black higher education, affirmative action, and Black studies

- **Frank Hamilton Bowles and Frank A. DeCosta** — *Between Two Worlds*, for Black higher education before and during the transformation of the 1960s.
- **Fabio Rojas** — *From Black Power to Black Studies*, used in Geiger's reconstruction of the institutional creation of Black Studies.
- **Carnegie Council on Policy Studies in Higher Education** — *Making Affirmative Action Work*. The collective authorship matters: policy reports are institutional products, not anonymous facts.
- **Neil Gross and Solon Simmons**, editors — *Professors and Their Politics* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 2014), cited in Geiger's discussion of the politics of the professoriate.
- **Robert Maranto, Richard E. Redding, and Frederick M. Hess**, editors — *The Politically Correct University: Problems, Scope, and Reforms* (AEI Press, 2008), cited alongside Gross and Simmons in that discussion.
- **Dinesh D'Souza** — *Illiberal Education*, cited by Geiger in the discussion of affirmative action and campus politics. Inclusion here is source provenance, not endorsement.

## Political and social context of the 1960s

- **G. Calvin Mackenzie and Robert Weisbrot** — *The Liberal Hour: Washington and the Politics of Change in the 1960s*, used for the larger political setting in which federal higher-education policy changed.
- **Richard H. Pells** — *The Liberal Mind in a Conservative Age*, another source for the intellectual and political context surrounding postwar universities.
- **James T. Patterson** — a historian Geiger invokes when identifying 1965 as a national tipping point. The exact Patterson work attached to that particular note remains to be normalized before this entry is treated as complete.

## Economics, finance, and the crisis of the 1970s

- **William G. Bowen** — *The Economics of Major Private Universities* (Carnegie Commission on Higher Education, 1968). Geiger cites Bowen in explaining the cost structure of labor-intensive universities and the financial pressure of the 1970s.
- **Hugh Graham and Nancy Diamond** — *The Rise of American Research Universities*, cited in Geiger's discussion of tenure, faculty structure, and research universities.
- **Selma J. Mushkin** — “A Note on State and Local Financing of Higher Education,” in *The Economics and Financing of Higher Education in the United States*, used in the history of public financing.

## Returns to college, educational attainment, and inequality

- **Claudia Goldin and Lawrence F. Katz** — *The Race between Education and Technology*, a major historical-economic source for the changing returns to education and supply of educated labor.
- **David P. Baker** — *The Schooled Society*, used in thinking about the expansion and social role of formal schooling.
- **David Autor** — “The Polarization of Job Opportunities in the U.S. Labor Market,” used in the account of changing labor-market structure.
- **Robert J. Gordon** — *The Rise and Fall of American Growth*, part of the broader economic-historical context for productivity, wages, and the changing economy.
- **James E. Rosenbaum** — *Beyond College for All: Career Paths for the Forgotten Half*, a counterweight to treating bachelor's-degree enrollment as the only legitimate postsecondary path.
- **Sarah E. Turner** — “Going to College and Finishing College: Explaining Different Educational Outcomes,” in **Caroline M. Hoxby**, ed., *College Choices*. Both Turner and Hoxby belong in the source graph.
- **Stuart Rojstaczer and Christopher Healy** — “Where A Is Ordinary: The Evolution of American College and University Grading, 1940–2009,” used in Geiger's discussion of grades and academic standards.

## Research policy and the federal research system

- **Harvey M. Sapolsky** — *Science and the Navy: The History of the Office of Naval Research*, a source for the federal research system that helped make the postwar research university.

## Faculty politics and the culture wars

- **Neil Gross** — *Why Are Professors Liberal and Why Do Conservatives Care?*, a source for the empirical study of faculty political orientation.
- **David Horowitz** — *The Professors: The 101 Most Dangerous Academics in America* and *Indoctrination U.* Geiger cites Horowitz as part of the historical record of conservative attacks on universities. Citation is not endorsement.

# Method for continuing this file

For every remaining note in the book:

1. transcribe the cited author or collective author;
2. normalize the full name only after checking an authoritative catalog/publisher/source;
3. preserve the exact cited title and edition where it matters;
4. mark whether the source is a primary source, contemporary account, later historical scholarship, sociology, quantitative work, memoir, government report, or institutional study;
5. thank editors when the cited object is an edited volume;
6. thank institutional/committee authors instead of turning their work into anonymous “data”;
7. follow especially important cited books one level backward into *their* sources;
8. never infer endorsement from citation;
9. never mirror a copyrighted source merely because a PDF happens to be reachable.

The end product should make visible how much accumulated work sits behind one synthetic history.
