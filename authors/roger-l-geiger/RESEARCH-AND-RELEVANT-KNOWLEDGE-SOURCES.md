IA metadata reports a bibliography/notes section at roughly **pp. 339–404**—about sixty-six pages of source apparatus. That is the material Blackball ultimately wants to trace, but the controlled scan is not an open source corpus and will not be mirrored here.

A later Transaction/Routledge edition is available commercially and through legitimate previews. Perlego exposes a limited book preview including the opening of chapter 1; Routledge's preview infrastructure also exposes part of the 2004 reissue. These previews are useful for verifying individual historical actors and claims, not for pretending that the whole book has been released openly.

## What the book is doing

This is the sequel to *To Advance Knowledge*. The center of gravity moves from private philanthropy and the prewar university research system to the **federal research economy** built through World War II and the Cold War.

The book follows the creation of contract research, federal science policy, the growth of organized research, private foundations, Sputnik-era expansion, the “golden age” of the 1960s, political conflict, the fiscal crisis of the 1970s, and the altered research environment of the 1980s. It also works institution by institution—MIT, Berkeley, Yale, Stanford, UCLA, Pittsburgh, Georgia Tech, Arizona, and others—rather than treating “the research university” as one homogeneous object.

# Historical actors directly visible in the legitimate preview

The opening pages of chapter 1 directly identify the people who created the wartime arrangement that became the postwar research system.

## James Bryant Conant

**James Bryant Conant**, president of Harvard, appears at the opening of the story as one of the scientists called into wartime mobilization. Geiger uses Conant not merely as an administrator but as a participant who immediately understood the institutional implications of funding research through contracts with universities rather than moving all scientific work into government laboratories.

Thank you to Conant's preserved writings and institutional record for making that decision historically reconstructible.

## Vannevar Bush

**Vannevar Bush**, then president of the Carnegie Institution of Washington, is the central organizer in the opening scene. The preview directly records his insistence on contracting with universities, research institutes, and industrial laboratories.

Bush belongs in the source graph in two senses: as a historical actor whose decisions shaped the system and as an author of documents and retrospective accounts historians use to reconstruct it.

## George Ellery Hale, Karl T. Compton, Frank B. Jewett, and Richard C. Tolman

The early wartime science-policy network also includes **George Ellery Hale**, **Karl Taylor Compton**, **Frank B. Jewett**, and **Richard C. Tolman**. Their roles connect universities, engineering, national scientific organizations, and federal mobilization.

These names are currently recorded as **primary historical actors directly visible in Geiger's narrative**, not yet as normalized bibliography entries. The exact archival documents, memoirs, reports, or secondary works attached to each note still need to be extracted from Geiger's controlled notes section.

# The Transaction reissue introduction

The legitimate Routledge preview exposes the end of Geiger's February 2004 “Introduction to the Transaction Edition.” There he describes the book's purpose as reconstructing the enormous and irregular history by which American universities developed from roughly 1940 to 1990 and explicitly points forward to his next book:

- **Roger L. Geiger**, *Knowledge and Money: Research Universities and the Paradox of the Marketplace* (Stanford University Press, 2004).

That is an authorial bridge rather than an inherited source, but it is useful for keeping the long-form sequence straight:

*To Advance Knowledge* → *Research and Relevant Knowledge* → *Knowledge and Money*.

The preview pages recovered so far do **not** expose a comparable list of newly published scholarship to the one Geiger supplied in the reissue introduction to *To Advance Knowledge*. Therefore Blackball should not manufacture such a list from books that happen to cover the same subject.

# Likely source territory versus verified Geiger citations

The historiography of wartime and Cold War science includes major works by people such as **Daniel J. Kevles, Irvin Stewart, Stuart W. Leslie, Rebecca S. Lowen, John W. Servos**, and others. Later historians frequently cite those works alongside Geiger.

That fact alone does **not** prove that Geiger cited each one in *Research and Relevant Knowledge*. Until an exact Geiger note, searchable snippet, publisher preview, or other direct locator is recovered, these names must remain outside the “verified citation” ledger.

This rule matters because source-tracing becomes worthless if thematic similarity silently turns into fake bibliography.
