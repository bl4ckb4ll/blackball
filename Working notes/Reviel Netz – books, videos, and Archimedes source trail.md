## One terminology correction

The object people usually mean here is the **Archimedes Palimpsest**, not a papyrus. The surviving object is a medieval parchment manuscript: a tenth-century copy containing Archimedes was taken apart, erased/scraped, and reused in 1229 for a Greek prayer book. Modern imaging recovered much of the erased writing.

Project: https://www.archimedespalimpsest.org/

Digital image-bank introduction: https://www.archimedespalimpsest.org/digital/

Palimpsest bibliography: https://archimedespalimpsest.org/links/bibliography.php

## Best first recording

### *Early Mathematics Day: The Archimedes Codex* — Gresham College, 6 May 2011 — 38:40

Full lecture page, video, transcript, and related material:

https://www.gresham.ac.uk/watch-now/early-mathematics-day-archimedes-codex

This is probably the single best fast introduction found in this sweep. Netz discusses:

- how the palimpsest was made and recovered;
- Heiberg's 1906 work and why a new edition became necessary;
- multispectral and X-ray-fluorescence imaging;
- *The Method* and Archimedes' use of infinitely many two-dimensional sections;
- the *Stomachion* and the possibility that Archimedes was doing a combinatorial counting problem;
- why ancient mathematical diagrams are evidence rather than decoration;
- *On Floating Bodies* and the construction of mathematical physics;
- why generalizations such as “the Greeks did not use infinity” can be false when they are really generalizations from Aristotle.

It is unusually useful because the transcript is public, so this can later be indexed and checked against the books rather than relying on remembered video impressions.

## Books: core history of mathematics

### *The Shaping of Deduction in Greek Mathematics: A Study in Cognitive History* — 1999

Cambridge University Press.

Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/the-shaping-of-deduction-in-greek-mathematics/6801E135656F4401979F431F6FF48A28

Core question: what actually made Greek deductive mathematics work as a cognitive practice? Netz emphasizes the combination of **lettered diagrams** with a tightly regulated, formulaic mathematical language. This is not merely a history of results; it treats the material and linguistic interface through which deductions were produced and communicated.

This is one of the books to read rather than merely summarize from reviews.

### *The Transformation of Mathematics in the Early Mediterranean World: From Problems to Equations* — 2004

Cambridge University Press.

Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/the-transformation-of-mathematics-in-the-early-mediterranean-world/F8B69BB197D898C3B0830CF200C93699

Netz follows one problem from Archimedes through late antiquity and Arabic mathematics, ending largely with Omar Khayyam. His historical object is the transformation of mathematical practice itself: a localized geometrical problem becomes an equation and eventually something approached by more systematic procedures.

Useful source-trail names already visible from the book's framing: **Archimedes, Eutocius, Omar Khayyam** and the intervening Arabic mathematical tradition.

### *Ludic Proof: Greek Mathematics and the Alexandrian Aesthetic* — 2009

Cambridge University Press.

Stanford description: https://classics.stanford.edu/publications/ludic-proof-greek-mathematics-and-alexandrian-aesthetic

Cambridge introduction: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/ludic-proof/introduction/DE03E28DEDE7E76AE64B83FE01EA4E5F

The book asks about **mathematical style**, placing Hellenistic mathematical writing beside Hellenistic literature. Netz argues for shared preferences including surprise, mosaics of apparently unrelated material, narrative construction, and profusion of detail. This is a direct attempt to understand scientific writing as a historical aesthetic practice rather than as timeless neutral packaging around propositions.

### *A New History of Greek Mathematics* — 2022

Cambridge University Press, 540 pp.

Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/a-new-history-of-greek-mathematics/8FF4CD50DEBDD5E0ABF75334027E79AB

Netz presents this as the first complete new history of Greek mathematics in roughly a century, replacing the position occupied by T. L. Heath's 1921 history. It covers Near Eastern antecedents; the rise of Greek mathematics; Archytas, Euclid and Archimedes; mathematics in social life; astronomy; canonization; and the route into modern science.

Contents:

1. To the Threshold of Greek Mathematics
2. The Generation of Archytas
3. The Generation of Archimedes
4. Mathematics in the World
5. Mathematics of the Stars
6. The Canonization of Greek Mathematics
7. Into Modern Science: The Legacy of Greek Mathematics
8. Epilogue: Bringing to the Boil

The 2019 Stanford talk announcing the project is useful because Netz explicitly frames the historiographical problem: how to describe exceptional features of Greek mathematics without simply reproducing an old triumphalist “Greek miracle” narrative.

## Archimedes: editions, commentary, and the palimpsest

### *The Works of Archimedes, Volume 1: The Two Books On the Sphere and the Cylinder: Translation and Commentary* — 2004

Cambridge University Press. Netz's translation/commentary project includes a critical treatment of the diagrams and Eutocius' commentary.

This is primary-text work, not a popular account.

### *The Archimedes Codex* — with William Noel — 2007

Popular account of the rediscovery, conservation, imaging, transcription, and mathematical significance of the Archimedes Palimpsest.

Google Books bibliographic/contents page: https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Archimedes_Codex.html?id=Yv0xmDB13gwC

Especially useful chapters/leads from the table of contents include **Visual Science**, **Archimedes' Method**, **The Digital Palimpsest**, **The Stomachion**, and **New Light on an Old Subject**.

This won the British Society for the History of Mathematics' Neumann Prize.

### *The Archimedes Palimpsest*, Vols. I–II — 2011 — edited with William Noel, Nigel Wilson, Natalie Tchernetska, et al.

Technical scholarly publication of the project.

The project's own bibliography identifies:

- Volume I: catalogue/commentary and project scholarship;
- Volume II: facsimile/transcription.

Project bibliography: https://archimedespalimpsest.org/links/bibliography.php

This should be treated as a major **source hub**, because its chapters bring together conservators, imaging scientists, classicists, historians, and manuscript scholars rather than just Netz.

### *The Works of Archimedes, Volume 2: On Spirals: Translation and Commentary* — 2017

Cambridge University Press.

Cambridge material: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/works-of-archimedes/introduction/ABB7762E057C24BD974604991B463AF3

This is another primary-text/commentary volume. Netz's discussion treats Archimedes' diagrams and presentation as part of the mathematics rather than disposable illustrations.

## Wider books

### *Barbed Wire: An Ecology of Modernity* — 2004

Wesleyan University Press.

Publisher/author page: https://www.weslpress.org/author/reviel-netz/

This is outside Greek mathematics. Netz uses barbed wire to write a history of modernity through **control over movement and space**, moving across cattle, agriculture, military enclosure, concentration camps and other systems of confinement. It is useful mainly as evidence of how Netz does large-scale material/cognitive history outside Classics.

### *Scale, Space and Canon in Ancient Literary Culture* — 2020

Cambridge University Press, about 900 pages.

Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/scale-space-and-canon-in-ancient-literary-culture/FADD7C408FFBE5867A61D0BD5C8D8C34

This may be the **most unexpectedly relevant Netz book for Blackball's prestige/class questions**, although it is about ancient literary culture, not universities.

Its organizing variables are canon, geography, and scale. It tries to reconstruct the literary field quantitatively: audience/book ownership, numbers of authors, geographical concentration, patronage, and the total circulating “bibliosphere.” The later chapters explicitly include **patronage and the quest for status**.

That does not establish any thesis about modern universities. It does give a concrete historical model for asking how cultural prestige, authorship, patronage, scarcity and canonization are produced rather than taking “great culture” as a primitive fact.

### *Why the Ancient Greeks Matter: The Problematic Miracle That Was Greece* — 2025

Cambridge University Press.

Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/why-the-ancient-greeks-matter/6C08DD345E62246E02BC0AAD89C3D37C

Contents:

1. The Problematic Greek Miracle
2. A Less Problematic Miracle: Greek Science
3. A Less Problematic Canon
4. Post-miracle

Of particular interest to the current research question: Netz's account of Greek science emphasizes the emergence of the **named author seeking fame**, producing new and surprising results in a polemical culture, rather than an anonymous teacher reproducing a settled algorithmic tradition. His account of the canon likewise rejects both “timeless transcendent values” and a simple gatekeeper conspiracy; he argues for canon formation through concrete social and poetic forces and a culture structured around contest.

Again: this is evidence for Netz's model of ancient cultural production, not evidence by itself for the proposed modern higher-education/class thesis.

## Source trail to bootstrap

Do not stop at Netz. Names that already recur in his books, editions, talks, acknowledgments, or the Palimpsest project and deserve their own source checks include:

- **T. L. Heath** — the 1921 *History of Greek Mathematics* that Netz explicitly positions the 2022 book against/replaces.
- **Johan Ludvig Heiberg** — identified and worked on the palimpsest in 1906; produced the old Archimedes edition that later discoveries forced scholars to revisit.
- **Wilbur Knorr** — major historian of ancient mathematics repeatedly engaged by Netz; Netz dedicated/titled talks around Knorr's work and memory.
- **G. E. R. Lloyd (Geoffrey Lloyd)** — Netz's doctoral supervisor and a major historian of Greek science; explicitly thanked in the introduction to *Scale, Space and Canon*.
- **William Noel** — manuscript curator/project director and co-author of *The Archimedes Codex*.
- **Nigel Wilson** — classicist and central collaborator on the palimpsest transcription/edition.
- **Natalie Tchernetska** — editor/scholar on the Palimpsest project and the recovered texts.
- **Fabio Acerbi** — collaborator on reconstruction of the *Stomachion* and ancient mathematics.
- **Roger Easton, Keith Knox, Mike Toth, Abigail Quandt** and the imaging/conservation teams — essential if the goal is to understand how the physical manuscript became readable evidence rather than treating “the text” as if it simply appeared.
- **Eutocius** — late antique commentator, crucial to the transmission/transformation of Archimedean problems.
- **Omar Khayyam** and earlier Arabic mathematicians — essential for the *Transformation of Mathematics* source trail.

The later pass should extract **actual bibliographies and cited works**, not just follow famous names.

## Why this source drop matters for the current Blackball question

The current working question is not simply “is mathematics valuable?” or “is higher education useful?” It is closer to: **how are prestige, respectability, scarcity, authorship and cultural authority themselves produced, and what can people subsequently do with those distinctions?**

Netz should not be recruited as if he had already answered that question about American universities. But several of his historical objects are unusually good comparisons:

- lettered diagrams + formulaic language as material practices that make a recognizable kind of reasoning possible;
- named authorship and fame as incentives in scientific production;
- polemic/competition as a mechanism producing novelty;
- patronage and status in sustaining a literary field;
- canon formation as a historical process;
- scale and scarcity of authors/readers/books;
- the survival, destruction, copying, editing and technological recovery of texts that later generations call “the tradition.”

Those are mechanisms. They can later be compared with the mechanisms Geiger, Tamanaha, Kirp, and historians of American higher education actually document. The comparison should be made **after** reading, not assumed in advance.
