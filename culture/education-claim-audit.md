# Education claim audit: degree-only value and actual learning

## Status

This is a proposed Blackball falsification protocol, not a finding that any particular degree, course, school, or instructor fails it.

It is meant for claims that education produces economic value or teaches a stated competence. Keep those two claims separate.

## When the test applies

If a school, program, instructor, adviser, or other seller uses claims such as “this degree opens doors,” “this is a lifetime investment,” or similar language about wages, employment, access, or durable economic value, the claim should be testable.

A contractual guarantee would make the promised result unusually explicit. If the seller will not make such a guarantee, that does not by itself make the claim false. It does mean Blackball should not let promotional language float between a mere aspiration and a causal promise depending on which interpretation is convenient after the outcome is known.

The stronger the economic claim, the stronger the evidence it should survive.

## Test 1: the degree-only résumé

The core question is deliberately severe:

**What valuable door does the degree itself open for a person who brings no other professional advantage?**

Construct a baseline résumé whose work history contains only ordinary jobs that can plausibly be obtained without a college credential: counter-service food work, retail, a home-and-garden store, and similar walk-in employment. Exclude professional internships, prior career-track experience, unusual credentials, and other accomplishments that could independently explain a hiring response.

Then run a matched audit:

- résumé A is the baseline résumé plus the degree being tested;
- résumé B is the same baseline résumé without that degree;
- send the two versions, randomized, to comparable openings;
- use no family ownership, family friends, personal referrals, insider introductions, or pre-existing professional network;
- record callbacks, interviews, offers, compensation, schedule, stability, role quality, and plausible advancement;
- specify in advance what counts as a **valuable door**.

The last requirement matters. A claim about valuable occupational access should not be rescued after the fact by pointing to any response at all. If the only new opportunity is a low-paid role, a graveyard shift, an unusually bad schedule, or work that was already available to the baseline applicant, that may be an employment result without being the valuable door that was advertised.

The matched résumé is useful for attribution as well as sufficiency. If the degree-bearing résumé does not obtain the relevant outcomes, a strong claim that the degree by itself opens those doors has failed this test. If both résumés obtain the same outcomes, the outcome cannot simply be credited to the degree. If the degree-bearing résumé materially outperforms the control on the predeclared outcomes, that is evidence for a degree effect in that tested job market.

This is the attribution rule:

**Degree only means degree only. Do not credit the degree for a door opened by a parent, a parent's friend, a referral, prior professional experience, an unrelated credential, or some other person's intervention. Credit the mechanism that actually did the work.**

Institutional recruiting, alumni access, placement services, and internships can be tested separately if they are part of what the school sells. Do not silently relabel their effect as evidence that the degree alone did it, and do not relabel networking value as instructional value.

A serious audit should also predeclare the geography, application window, target occupations, number of applications, and job-quality threshold. Otherwise the definition of success can move after the results are seen.

## Test 2: did the students learn the thing taught?

A different claim is instructional: the institution says that its classes teach some subject or skill.

That claim should face an external outcome measure rather than being established by the instructor's own grades or by course completion alone.

For example, if a multi-semester Spanish sequence is supposed to teach students to use Spanish, test whether students can actually converse in Spanish after the sequence and whether their proficiency improved from where they started. An independent oral assessment is closer to the claimed competence than six semesters of course credit by itself.

The general design should prefer:

- an external benchmark or assessor independent of the instructor;
- a pre-course baseline and a post-course measure when possible;
- a test that resembles the competence being claimed rather than only the classroom exercise used to award grades;
- reporting of attendance, effort, prior preparation, attrition, and other major factors needed to interpret failure or success.

A poor outcome does not prove that every failure is the teacher's fault. Students can skip class, refuse to practice, start at different levels, or otherwise affect their own results. The point is narrower: if the advertised competence does not appear in the learners, the fact that material was presented in class is not by itself evidence that the institution successfully taught that competence.

## Keep the two claims separate

Economic value and instructional value can come apart.

A course can teach its subject well without producing a labor-market premium. A credential can create hiring access without teaching much of the competence that an employer later uses. A school can provide a useful network without that network being evidence for the curriculum. A student can learn a great deal without receiving the economic return used to market the degree.

Therefore ask separately:

1. **Did the credential itself open the kind of economic door that was claimed?**
2. **Did the instruction produce the competence that was claimed?**

Do not move credit between them after the fact.

## Evidence boundary

Passing this audit would be evidence for the particular claim, population, place, time, and threshold tested. It would not prove that the effect is universal or permanent.

Failing it would not establish that education has no cultural, intellectual, personal, or civic value. It would establish a problem for the particular economic or instructional claim that the test was constructed to examine.

This protocol is designed to prevent a recurring attribution error: charging money on the strength of one claimed benefit, then taking credit for benefits actually produced by family connections, prior experience, student self-teaching, unrelated credentials, or some other mechanism.
