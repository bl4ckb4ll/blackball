## The scene

Drake finds a poor boy caring for an impoverished man he calls “master.” Drake asks why he calls him master. The boy explains that the man teaches him all he knows. The man has almost no money and is eating almost nothing. The boy gets him what food he can, largely bread.

The exchange reaches the line:

> “He feeds my mind.”

That line is the reason to keep the scene.

## Blackball question: what is a teacher?

This is a useful counterexample to treating “teacher” as synonymous with a modern salaried employee of a school system, holder of a credential, classroom manager, or member/nonmember of a teachers union.

Here the teacher is defined by a relationship:

- one person possesses knowledge and deliberately gives it to another;
- the student recognizes an obligation in return;
- the return is not tuition but subsistence;
- the teacher’s material poverty is not evidence that the teaching has little value;
- indeed, the child describes the exchange as food for food: bodily sustenance for intellectual sustenance.

That makes the scene useful before entering arguments about teachers unions. It asks what activity and social relation are being protected, priced, professionalized, credentialed, unionized, or attacked in the first place.

## Labor-history connection

A Blackball liberal-arts syllabus can make labor history central without first deciding that every union claim is correct. Teacher-union controversies are more intelligible once “teacher” and “teaching” have been separated from the later institutional arrangements around them.

Possible comparison set:

- Massachusetts / U.S. history of teacher organization and unionization;
- anti-teacher-union arguments from Milton Friedman, Cato, EconTalk guests, and similar sources;
- pro-union and labor-history accounts;
- historical household tutors, religious teachers, rabbis, itinerant teachers, and other forms in which teaching was supported directly by households or communities rather than by a modern school payroll.

The point is not romanticization. The master in this episode is close to starvation. A system can revere learning rhetorically while failing to feed the person doing the teaching. That is itself part of the labor question.

## Related Blackball motif: honored because underfed

Cross-link this with the Nathan Ausubel / Jewish folklore material about rabbis whose poverty or hunger can be redescribed as holiness or fasting. The recurring question is whether honor-language disguises material neglect: the community says the intellectual or religious worker is exalted while not actually supporting him.

The *Danger Man* scene supplies the other side of the relation. The boy does not deny the material problem. He feeds the teacher because, in his formulation, the teacher feeds him intellectually.

## Geographic / cultural note

The episode’s generalized North-African setting also belongs to a separate cultural-history question: what “North Africa,” Egypt, Morocco, the Mediterranean, Mexico, etc. signified as reachable/exotic spaces to British versus American audiences in the early 1960s. Do not collapse those geographies. For a British audience, North Africa sits inside a different imperial, Mediterranean, travel, and cultural map than it does for an American audience.

That strand should be researched separately rather than inferred from the episode’s scenery.
