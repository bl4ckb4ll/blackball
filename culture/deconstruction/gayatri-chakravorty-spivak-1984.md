## Spivak's argument, as stated in the discussion

1. **Deconstruction is a “radical acceptance of vulnerability” (pp. 18–19).** Narratives, including political programs meant to deliver social justice, select ends and leave other ends and persons out of view. The task is to ask what is excluded and whether those exclusions can be recovered, not to pretend the thinker stands outside every narrative.
2. **Narration remains necessary (pp. 31–34).** She explicitly accepts that people are bound to narrate; the demand is to acknowledge a narrative's limits, the interests invested in constructing it, and the possibility that further questions remain. She does not claim every account is equally adequate.
3. **“Text” is not only printed or spoken language (pp. 24–25).** In explaining Derrida and Lyotard, she describes a historical, social, and political web of relations. Interpreting her claim as simply “everything is words” misses the distinction she draws.
4. **Causal reasoning is not discarded (p. 23).** She allows causal inquiry while questioning the move from a useful causal account to the claim that the identified cause is final or beyond revision.
5. **Political categories can conceal exclusions (pp. 27–32).** Her examples include treating a particular male wage worker as the universal proletarian subject and overlooking the people or labor such a formulation sidelines. This is a criticism of particular constructions of Marxist history and political subjecthood, not proof that analysis of class or capitalism must cease.

These points are descriptions of her position in this exchange, not automatic endorsements of its soundness.

## Strong objections in the same source

- **Geoffrey Hawthorn:** If deconstruction directs attention to absences, how does the resulting claim count as an argument supported by evidence? How does inquiry continue to distinguish truth from falsehood? (pp. 20–21)
- **Ron Aronson:** Political activity still needs accounts of capitalism, imperialism, historic disasters, and material threats. In the railway example, he asks whether one can judge the relative contributions of capital, labor, and technology and call one account the most adequate available. Spivak agrees that an account can be the best *at the moment*, while refusing to make it the last word. (pp. 21–22, 26–27, 30–33)
- **John Dunn:** Noticing that descriptions are selective may be valid but is not by itself a method of better causal inference or political action. A concern about the dangers of rationalism should not stop investigators from using cumulative knowledge, evidence, and reason. He also argues that rational inquiry need not entail a specific oppressive political practice. (pp. 22–24, 29–34)

The tension is substantive: *Does attention to exclusion produce better understanding of a case, or merely add skepticism to an explanation that already admits it is provisional?* The discussion does not settle this question by assertion.

## A concrete Blackball test

Take Aronson's “who built the railroads?” example (pp. 32–33). A conventional account might credit owners and financiers. A corrective account might credit Irish and Chinese laborers. Spivak warns that merely exchanging one definitive set of protagonists for another still leaves out other agents, conditions, and investments. Aronson asks whether one can nevertheless construct the best available material explanation.

**Testable extension, not an established result of this debate:** for a specific historical claim, list the included and omitted actors and causal forces; inspect primary sources; check whether adding the omitted evidence changes the estimated causes, chronology, or outcomes. Preserve a best-supported provisional account rather than equating incomplete knowledge with no knowledge.
