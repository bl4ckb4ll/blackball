# Match factories, white phosphorus, and the price of not being free to study

## Why this case is here

When Blackball compares a protected life of study with ordinary wage work, do not make the wage job an abstract "character-building" alternative.

For many working-class girls and young women in nineteenth-century London, factory work was undertaken because wages were needed. In the match industry that could mean long hours, low pay, fines, and exposure to white phosphorus severe enough to destroy the jaw.

The relevant contrast is not:

> one person virtuously studies while another person should learn discipline through hard work.

It is:

> one household can afford to allocate a young person's time to study, art, languages, travel, or scholarship; another household needs wages now.

That is a material difference before any theory of education enters.

## Bryant & May and the matchgirls

The National Archives' reconstruction of the 1888 Bryant & May strike describes a largely female workforce working long hours for very low wages. Pay could be reduced through fines for such things as lateness, talking, or going to the toilet.

An 1898 inspection record for Bryant & May counted 1,231 workers. Of these, 83 were directly exposed to yellow/white phosphorus and 644 indirectly exposed. The exposed workforce included people from age 14 upward. The same archive records a 19-year-old worker who had already spent five years at the factory.

That chronology matters. A teenager of roughly fourteen working in a phosphorus match factory is not choosing between "education" and "work" on the same terms as the child of a household able to finance years of specialized study.

## Phosphorus necrosis: "phossy jaw"

White phosphorus was used in strike-anywhere matches because it made ignition easy. Chronic exposure could produce phosphorus necrosis, or osteonecrosis, of the jaw.

Contemporary and later medical descriptions record:

- toothache and swelling of the gums or jaw;
- abscesses and persistent discharge;
- progressive death of jaw bone;
- difficulty chewing;
- severe pain;
- facial disfigurement;
- removal of portions, or in extreme cases the whole, of the jaw;
- systemic illness and sometimes fatal complications, including brain damage.

The Royal College of Surgeons describes the disease as overwhelmingly affecting poor match-factory workers. Its account includes the 1850s case of Cornelia, a 16-year-old New York match worker who had worked eight-hour days for two and a half years and continued working even after swelling and pus drainage had begun.

The British National Archives reports that a Bryant & May record covering 1882–1897 put mortality among documented phossy-jaw cases at **19.15%**, almost one in five. It also records at least five deaths from consequences of phosphorus poisoning between 1892 and 1896 and seventeen unreported poisoning cases exposed in 1898.

Do not call this "bone cancer." The historical disease was phosphorus necrosis / osteonecrosis of the jaw.

## Workers could be trapped by the need for wages

The health rules themselves reveal the constraint. Workers with symptoms risked being removed from phosphorus work. The National Archives notes that workers could fear reporting symptoms because being forced to stop work also meant losing income.

This is the point Blackball should preserve.

A household with enough money can rationally say:

> spend the next several years reading, painting, translating Old Norse, or studying mathematics.

A household living from wages may instead need:

> bring home money this week.

The second person is not demonstrating less respect for learning. Their time is under a different economic constraint.

## Strike and regulation

The 1888 matchgirls' strike challenged the fines, pay, dismissals, and working conditions at Bryant & May. The company eventually ended the fines system and reinstated dismissed workers.

White phosphorus remained in use after the strike. Bryant & May did not stop using it until 1901, and broader legal prohibition came later.

The 1899 Home Office report on phosphorus in match manufacture shows how extensive the hazard remained nationally: 4,152 people were employed across 25 match factories in 1897, including 3,082 females; in 23 factories using yellow phosphorus, 1,521 workers were in phosphorus processes. Thirty-six known cases of phosphorus necrosis were recorded in 1894–98, while the report explicitly warned that older case records were incomplete.

## Blackball use

This case is a control against romanticizing compulsory wage labor.

It does **not** establish that every factory job was equivalent to Bryant & May, that every affluent scholar was idle, or that manual work is inherently degrading.

It establishes something narrower and harder to evade:

**the freedom to spend years learning without immediate economic return is itself a material privilege.**

If education is then sold to people who must work for wages as the thing that supposedly creates that freedom, the causal claim must be demonstrated rather than assumed.
