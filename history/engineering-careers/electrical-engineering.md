# Electrical engineering careers

## Who gets to design a Wi-Fi chip?

Morse Micro brought Neil Weste onto its team in 2016. He had coauthored *CMOS VLSI Design* and had led the design of Radiata’s Wi-Fi modem chip. At Morse Micro he designed the core physical-layer engine for Wi-Fi HaLow. The founders’ account describes meeting an engineer who had already delivered closely related work. What reading his book did for a student’s hiring prospects remains unmeasured. [Weste’s career and recruitment sources](../../people/neil-weste.md#who-got-the-work)

A graduate at the same company described a different sequence of assignments. Alex Hiller joined the graduate program in March 2021, tested Wi-Fi silicon on the bench, and helped scale production testing. In 2023 an op-amp he designed was fabricated on one of the company’s chips. In 2024 he moved into full-time design. His account identifies who first gave him time on the hardware and when he began designing circuits. [Hiller’s interview in the Cambridge University Engineering Society magazine](https://magazine.cues.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Magazine_25-26.pdf)

Morse Micro’s first paid graduate intake had more than 50 applicants, 12 interviews and four selections. Those figures belong to the January 2020 intake. Hiller entered the following year. Measuring how many entrants eventually reached design work would require following all members of a given intake through later assignments. [Morse Micro’s first-intake report](https://medium.com/@MorseMicro/reach-farther-in-your-career-c2f0521482d8)

## An electrical engineering master’s graduate driving for Uber

In July 2025 a Blackball contributor reported that his Uber driver had a master’s in electrical engineering. He described the encounter again in October 2025 and September 2026. Those accounts refer to one driver. In September 2026 he also reported encountering civil engineering graduates picking orders at Amazon. These are observations of actual work that a count of people described simply as *employed* would conceal. The source for the driver is the passenger’s account. His other employment, borrowing and reasons for driving were not established in those conversations.

A federal survey supplies a national comparison. In the 2023 National Survey of College Graduates, an estimated **413,000 employed people** had a master’s as their highest degree in **electrical and computer engineering**. Of these, 297,000 described their principal job as closely related to the degree, 93,000 as somewhat related and **23,000 as unrelated** (about **5.6%**). These are rounded estimates for employed graduates across career stages. The category includes computer engineering. The survey measures the relationship to a principal job. [NCSES, table 1-3, master’s panel, p. 3](https://www.ncses.nsf.gov/pubs/nsf25322/assets/data-tables/tables/nsf25322-tab001-003.pdf)

## How often would a passenger encounter this?

Sampling through rides starts with drivers. Hall and Krueger’s table for a December 2014 survey of 601 Uber drivers across 20 U.S. markets reported 36.9% with a college degree and 10.8% with a postgraduate degree, or **47.7% combined**. Uber commissioned the survey. The education categories cover all fields. The published table reproduces the 47.7% figure cited in the December 2025 conversation. [Hall and Krueger, table 1 and survey methods](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22843/w22843.pdf)

For a numerical illustration, suppose a passenger met ten distinct drivers independently drawn from a population with the survey’s 10.8% postgraduate share. The chance of meeting at least one postgraduate would be **68.1%** (1 − 0.892¹⁰). The chance of meeting at least two would be **29.5%** (subtract 10 × 0.108 × 0.892⁹ from the first probability). Ten is an illustrative exposure count. Local driver composition, hours driven, repeated encounters and whether people discuss education can change a passenger’s odds. The table does not break out electrical engineering.

The contributor recalls an earlier assistant bootstrap of how often he encountered graduates outside their fields. The recovered discussions document the driver and warehouse encounters, but do not supply the original run, its exposure count, benchmark or numerical result. The illustration above is a new calculation. To test an institution’s placement claim against the recurring observations, first recover its definition of placement and time horizon. Then count distinct people encountered in each setting, including the people asked about their degrees who did obtain the work they trained for. Preserve which encounters came from the same workplace and which people’s educational histories were never learned. Resampling by workplace or setting can preserve clustering once those records exist. A list containing only the remembered mismatches cannot reconstruct the original sampling process.
