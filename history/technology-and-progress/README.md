# Technology and progress

This directory collects historical cases that complicate the easy equation:

> higher productivity = better technology = social progress

Those three things can coincide, but they are not the same variable.

## Questions to ask

When a source reports rising output per worker, separate at least these mechanisms:

1. **mechanical technology** — tools, engines, machines, power systems;
2. **biological technology** — crop varieties, breeding, disease resistance, seed characteristics;
3. **organizational technology** — accounting, task division, workflow design, measurement, scheduling, supervision;
4. **labor intensification** — more hours, faster pace, fewer breaks, denser supervision, higher quotas;
5. **coercion** — threats, punishment, violence, legal unfreedom, or lack of a credible exit option.

A productivity statistic alone does not tell us which mechanism changed.

It also does not tell us whether workers became better off. Track worker welfare separately: autonomy, injury, pain, exhaustion, leisure, bargaining power, compensation, punishment risk, and who captures the additional output.

## Edward Baptist and enslaved cotton pickers

The first Blackball case here is Edward Baptist's argument about antebellum cotton picking.

Average cotton picked per enslaved worker per day rose from roughly 28.1 pounds in 1801 to 114.2 pounds in 1862, about 2.3 percent per year. A 2026 paper by Sven Beckert and Mark Stelzner notes that this growth was on par with output-per-worker growth in Manchester cotton mills during the Industrial Revolution. Yet cotton picking itself remained hand labor.

Baptist argues that individualized quotas, weighing, ledgers, escalating expectations, and violence formed a labor-control system that raised manual productivity. Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode instead place much more causal weight on improved cotton varieties. Beckert and Stelzner's 2026 reassessment argues that biological innovation mattered but that intensified monitoring and forced work effort were also significant.

The point for this directory is not to settle the entire slavery-and-productivity debate by slogan. It is to force a better decomposition of "progress." A worker can produce more because a machine expands productive capacity; because a plant variety is easier to harvest; because work is organized differently; or because someone extracts more effort from the worker. The same upward-sloping productivity curve can conceal radically different human realities.

## Measurement rule

For every case added here, try to separate:

- output per person;
- output per hour;
- hours worked;
- capital or machinery per worker;
- organizational change;
- biological or material input change;
- work intensity;
- coercion or bargaining power;
- worker compensation;
- worker injury or other welfare costs;
- distribution of gains.

Do not call the residual "technology" merely because output rose.
