# UMKC six-year B.A./M.D. program

## Why this case belongs in Blackball

The University of Missouri-Kansas City School of Medicine is a durable U.S. counterexample to the idea that physician training must be organized as four undergraduate years followed by four medical-school years.

UMKC opened its medical school in 1971 around a deliberately nontraditional design developed by E. Grey Dimond and colleagues: admission largely from high school, an integrated baccalaureate/medical curriculum, early and continuous patient contact, year-round study, and long-running physician-led docent teams. The combined B.A./M.D. route can be completed in six years after high school rather than the eight years typical of the conventional U.S. 4+4 route.

This case does **not** by itself establish that six years is superior, cheaper in total social cost, appropriate for every student, or evidence that other professional degrees should simply be shortened. Its evidentiary value is narrower: a major accredited U.S. medical school has operated for decades without treating the usual pre-med/medical-school boundary as structurally necessary.

## Atomic claims

### Founding and structure

1. **UMKC School of Medicine opened in 1971.**
   - UMKC's institutional history says the first official day of class was August 30, 1971.
   - Source: UMKC School of Medicine, “Our History.”

2. **E. Grey Dimond was appointed in 1968 to help establish the school and its nontraditional curriculum.**
   - UMKC identifies Dimond as a central founder and says he worked on the school and its docent-based model beginning in 1968.
   - Sources: UMKC “Our History”; Drees, “UMKC School of Medicine 40 Years of Excellence.”

3. **The core B.A./M.D. design admits students directly from high school and awards both a bachelor’s degree and M.D. after six years.**
   - UMKC currently describes the program as an integrated six-year dual-degree route beginning immediately after high school.
   - Source: UMKC, “Six Years, Two Degrees”; UMKC Academic Catalog.

4. **The first two years are not simply two discarded college years.**
   - The current catalog says roughly three-fourths of student time in years 1–2 is devoted to arts and sciences for the baccalaureate and one-fourth to medical coursework; the balance shifts toward medical coursework in years 3–6.
   - Source: UMKC Academic Catalog, B.A./M.D. program description.

5. **Clinical exposure begins during the first year rather than waiting until the conventional clinical years.**
   - UMKC says students begin medical study immediately and enter docent-based clinical experiences at the start of the program.
   - Sources: UMKC “Six Years, Two Degrees”; “Learning in Docent Teams”; Academic Catalog.

6. **The docent system is a defining structural feature.**
   - Physician docents mentor small student groups longitudinally; the system combines clinical teaching, mentoring, peer learning, and continuing patient-care exposure.
   - Sources: UMKC “Learning in Docent Teams”; Drees 2012.

### Deliberate departure from 4+4

7. **The six-year structure was a deliberate alternative to the standard American sequence, not an accidental compression.**
   - In a 1983 JAMA letter, Dimond described the experiment as direct high-school admission, a 12-month calendar, a liberal-arts and medical degree in six years, early/repeated patient contact, and four-year clinician-counselor assignments.
   - Source: E. Grey Dimond, “The University of Missouri-Kansas City School of Medicine,” JAMA 1983.

8. **Dimond connected the design to his own accelerated wartime medical education.**
   - UMKC's institutional history says his wartime medical education was condensed into 36 months without normal spring or summer breaks and that this experience helped inspire his later six-year plan.
   - Source: UMKC “Six Years, Two Degrees.”
   - Qualification: this is an institutional retrospective account and should be checked against Dimond’s own memoir or contemporary documents before being treated as the complete causal history.

### Accreditation and institutional resistance

9. **The LCME explicitly recognized the school as a major experiment in medical education before it opened.**
   - Dimond’s 1988 JAMA article reproduces language from a September 1969 LCME survey team calling the proposed UMKC program a “significant departure” from the typical U.S. pattern and an experiment that should be permitted to continue with LCME knowledge and consent.
   - Source: E. Grey Dimond, “The UMKC Medical Education Experiment: An Alternative Pathway to Physicianhood,” JAMA 1988.

10. **The unconventional model later faced serious accreditation doubts but persisted.**
    - UMKC’s account of dean Harry Jonas says LCME questioned the six-year route and direct admission from high school; UMKC remains LCME-accredited.
    - Sources: UMKC, “UMKC Celebrates a Champion of Medical Education”; UMKC accreditation pages.
    - Qualification: the description of the dispute is UMKC’s retrospective institutional account. The relevant LCME reports should be recovered if possible.

### Outcomes: what is established and what is not

11. **The program has persisted for more than five decades and produced thousands of physicians.**
    - UMKC says more than 3,300 graduates have passed through the school, most through the six-year route.
    - Source: UMKC “Six Years, Two Degrees.”
    - Qualification: current institutional count; preserve the retrieval date when using as a statistic.

12. **Published medical-education literature reports outcomes comparable to conventional programs on several conventional measures.**
    - A 2007 Academic Medicine review by UMKC-affiliated authors says graduates were successful according to outcome measures used by other medical schools.
    - A later review of time-variable medical education summarizes favorable comparisons on board scores and residency match rates.
    - Sources: Drees, Arnold & Jonas 2007; Cangiarella et al. 2018.
    - Qualification: these are not clean randomized comparisons, and some key authors were institutionally affiliated. Do not convert “comparable on selected measures” into “six-year education is generally superior.”

13. **Dimond himself framed the program as an alternative, not a universal replacement.**
    - His 1988 article states that the UMKC program was not for all students and was not offered as a replacement for the traditional American system.
    - Source: Dimond 1988.

## Blackball relevance

This case bears on several recurring questions:

- Which years of professional credentialing are educationally necessary, and which are institutional convention?
- When a profession requires both a general degree and a professional degree, must they be sequential institutions, or can they be integrated?
- Does early professional commitment justify earlier admission, or does it prematurely narrow a student’s options?
- What exactly is gained by the conventional pre-med bachelor’s period: liberal education, scientific preparation, selection, maturation, signaling, or merely compliance with an inherited admissions sequence?
- If an accredited medical school can combine liberal arts, basic science, clinical education, and the M.D. in six years, what evidence is required before treating eight post-high-school years as the natural baseline rather than one institutional design among others?
- Conversely, what costs or selection effects are hidden by acceleration: workload, attrition, loss of exploration time, socioeconomic selection, or greater dependence on high-school preparation?

The strongest use of UMKC in Blackball is therefore **institutional counterexample**, not slogan. It shows that the boundary between undergraduate education and professional medical education is mutable. It does not settle how the boundary should be drawn.

## Retrieval queue

1. Recover the **1969 LCME survey report** quoted by Dimond rather than relying on his quotation alone.
2. Recover Dimond’s book **_Take Wing!_** and inspect his own full account of the program’s design and wartime influence.
3. Find contemporary 1968–1974 planning documents, state approvals, curriculum plans, and accreditation correspondence.
4. Establish historical **tuition and living-cost comparisons** between UMKC’s six-year route and conventional 4+4 medical education. Do not infer savings from “six instead of eight” without actual cost data.
5. Measure **attrition, transfer, specialty choice, licensing outcomes, residency placement, and long-run practice location** against comparable students in conventional programs.
6. Investigate selection: who could credibly commit to medicine at 17–18, what high-school preparation was expected, and whether the model shifted risk toward adolescents and families.
7. Compare UMKC with other U.S. combined B.A./M.D. programs and with the British/European direct-entry model. Distinguish acceleration from simple institutional integration.
8. Determine which parts of the original 1971 design survive unchanged and which have been substantially revised.
