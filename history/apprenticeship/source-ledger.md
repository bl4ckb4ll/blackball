# Apprenticeship source ledger

This ledger starts with sources strong enough to stop Blackball from treating apprenticeship as either an automatic cure for credential-first education or an undifferentiated form of exploitation.

A source's presence here does **not** mean Blackball accepts all of its interpretation. It means the source is worth inspecting and can answer specific questions.

## 1. George C. Sikes — “The Apprentice System in the Building Trades”

- **Citation:** George C. Sikes, “The Apprentice System in the Building Trades,” *Journal of Political Economy* 2, no. 3 (June 1894): 397–423.
- **JSTOR:** https://www.jstor.org/stable/1819235
- **Publisher DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1086/250227
- **Status:** contemporary historical analysis; public-domain issue on JSTOR.
- **Use for:** late-nineteenth-century U.S. building-trades apprenticeship, conflicts over apprentice numbers, training, employer and union interests, and the institutional condition of apprenticeship during industrialization.
- **Do not use for:** a timeless description of apprenticeship or a neutral final judgment on unions/employers. It is a period source and should be read alongside later scholarship and primary records.

The same issue contains **“Appendix: System of Apprenticeship,” pp. 479–483**, JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/1819247. Preserve it separately when inspected rather than treating it as part of Sikes automatically.

## 2. Daniel Jacoby — “The Transformation of Industrial Apprenticeship in the United States”

- **Citation:** Daniel Jacoby, “The Transformation of Industrial Apprenticeship in the United States,” *The Journal of Economic History* 51, no. 4 (December 1991): 887–910.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050700040158
- **Status:** peer-reviewed historical/economic analysis.
- **Use for:** the change from employer-dominated to union-dominated industrial apprenticeship between the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries; employer use of forfeitable performance bonds; the problem created when employers who held the bond also judged disputes involving it.
- **Blackball relevance:** this is unusually direct evidence for the **back-door accountability** problem. A formally reciprocal training contract can give one party control over discipline, termination, adjudication, and a deferred or forfeitable asset at the same time.
- **Caution:** inspect the article itself before promoting the abstract's mechanism into claims about prevalence, magnitude, occupations, or individual cases.

## 3. Gillian Hamilton — “The Decline of Apprenticeship in North America: Evidence from Montreal”

- **Citation:** Gillian Hamilton, “The Decline of Apprenticeship in North America: Evidence from Montreal,” *The Journal of Economic History* 60, no. 3 (September 2000): 627–664.
- **JSTOR:** https://www.jstor.org/stable/2566433
- **Status:** peer-reviewed historical/economic analysis.
- **Use for:** apprenticeship in Montreal, changes in contract duration/completion and the decline of the institution, and the role of enforcement costs and changing alternatives.
- **Blackball relevance:** apprenticeship should not be modeled as a fixed seven-year bargain that either succeeds or fails. Contract exit, enforcement, changing outside options, and institutional flexibility are themselves part of the history.
- **Caution:** Montreal is evidence, not a universal North American average.

## 4. John E. Murray and Ruth Wallis Herndon — “Markets for Children in Early America”

- **Citation:** John E. Murray and Ruth Wallis Herndon, “Markets for Children in Early America: A Political Economy of Pauper Apprenticeship,” *The Journal of Economic History* 62, no. 2 (June 2002): 356–382.
- **JSTOR:** https://www.jstor.org/stable/2698184
- **Status:** peer-reviewed historical/economic analysis.
- **Use for:** pauper apprenticeship as a distinct institution; how local political/institutional arrangements affected terms imposed on poor children; the allocation of maintenance, schooling/training, labor, and end-of-term obligations.
- **Blackball relevance:** the nominal bargain cannot be evaluated without asking **who represented the child at formation and enforcement** and whether the officials negotiating with masters had aligned interests.
- **Caution:** do not generalize pauper apprenticeship to voluntary craft apprenticeship or modern registered apprenticeship.

## 5. Ruth Wallis Herndon and John E. Murray, eds. — *Children Bound to Labor*

- **Citation:** Ruth Wallis Herndon and John E. Murray, eds., *Children Bound to Labor: The Pauper Apprentice System in Early America* (Cornell University Press, 2009).
- **JSTOR book:** https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7591/j.ctt7z8zt
- **Status:** scholarly edited volume; chapters cover different colonies/states and institutional variants.
- **Use for:** the heterogeneity that a one-word label hides. The volume treats schooling, literacy, orphan/pauper administration, master-servant relations, family/state relations, and outcomes across multiple settings.
- **Especially relevant chapters:**
  - Herndon and Murray, “A Proper and Instructive Education,” pp. 3–18;
  - Herndon, “Proper Magistrates and Masters,” pp. 39–51;
  - Paul Lachance, “The Extent and Limits of Indentured Children’s Literacy in New Orleans, 1809–1843,” pp. 119–132;
  - Jean B. Russo and J. Elliott Russo, “Responsive Justices,” pp. 151–165;
  - Gillian Hamilton, “The Stateless and the Orphaned among Montreal’s Apprentices, 1791–1842,” pp. 166–182.
- **Blackball relevance:** some arrangements supplied education/maintenance and some functioned largely as access to cheap vulnerable labor. That variation is the reason to measure performance rather than infer it from the institution's name.

## 6. Farley Grubb — “The Statutory Regulation of Colonial Servitude: An Incomplete-Contract Approach”

- **Citation:** Farley Grubb, “The Statutory Regulation of Colonial Servitude: An Incomplete-Contract Approach,” *Explorations in Economic History* 37, no. 1 (January 2000): 42–75.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1006/exeh.1999.0730
- **Status:** peer-reviewed historical/economic analysis; publisher access may be restricted.
- **Use for:** statutory end-of-contract payments and differences among indentured, convict, and apprentice contracts.
- **Blackball relevance:** useful for analyzing contracts whose meaningful reciprocal payment is deferred until completion, after the worker has already supplied most or all of the service.
- **Caution:** this is not evidence that masters generally sabotaged completion to evade final payments. That stronger claim requires case-level evidence.

## 7. Patrick Wallis — *The Market for Skill: Apprenticeship and Economic Growth in Early Modern England*

- **Citation:** Patrick Wallis, *The Market for Skill: Apprenticeship and Economic Growth in Early Modern England* (Princeton University Press, 2025).
- **JSTOR:** https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.19307125
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.2307/jj.19307125
- **Status:** recent scholarly synthesis based on large historical apprenticeship datasets.
- **Use for:** a modern reconstruction of how early-modern English apprenticeship actually worked, including learning, early exit, enforcement, outcomes, access restrictions, gender/class barriers, and occupational mobility.
- **Particularly relevant chapters:**
  - “Apprenticeship as Learning,” pp. 111–133;
  - “The Flexibility of Apprenticeship,” pp. 134–154;
  - “Enforcing Apprenticeship,” pp. 155–187;
  - “Outcomes,” pp. 188–218;
  - “Laws, Rules, and Barriers to Skill,” pp. 221–238;
  - “Exclusion in Practice?,” pp. 239–262;
  - “Cultural Barriers,” pp. 263–278.
- **Blackball relevance:** Wallis explicitly complicates both the story that apprenticeship was merely restrictive and the story that it was automatically a successful training bargain. Early exit and flexibility may have been features rather than simple failure; exclusion and unequal access remain separate questions.

## 8. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — *Apprenticeship in Building Construction*

- **Citation:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, *Apprenticeship in Building Construction*, Bulletin No. 459 (1928).
- **Full text / scans:** https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/apprenticeship-building-construction-4048
- **Status:** government primary/contemporary institutional survey.
- **Use for:** city-by-city and trade-by-trade evidence about formal indentures, helper systems, paid school time, joint committees, employer refusal to train, and the practical administration of building-trades apprenticeship.
- **Blackball relevance:** shows why the word apprenticeship is insufficient even within one country and period. Some trades/cities had formal joint systems; others relied on helpers or had employers who preferred already partly trained workers who could be dropped when no longer needed.

## 9. U.S. Department of Labor history — Edward Pessen, “Builders of the Young Republic”

- **Link:** https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/chapter2
- **Status:** government-hosted secondary history, useful as an orientation source and citation lead rather than the last word.
- **Use for:** the transition from artisan/craft production toward factory production and the changing place of skilled workers and apprenticeship in the early United States.
- **Blackball relevance:** a government history itself describes the breakdown of older apprenticeship arrangements as employers substituted cheaper labor and reduced reliance on fully trained skill. Follow its cited historians and primary records before using strong claims.

# Source-use rule

For this topic, discovery order should be:

**contract / court petition / administrative record / contemporary survey -> inspected scholarly analysis -> later synthesis -> discovery lead**

Do not let either current apprenticeship promotion or retrospective anti-apprenticeship rhetoric substitute for the actual institutional record.
