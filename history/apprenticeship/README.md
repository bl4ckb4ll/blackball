# Apprenticeship

Apprenticeship belongs in Blackball as an institution to investigate, not as a ready-made answer to the failures of college, graduate school, engineering education, or credential-first training.

The governing rule is:

> **Do not infer the realized deal from the advertised form of the deal.**

"Earn while you learn" can describe a materially better allocation of training risk than tuition-first schooling. It can also conceal cheap labor, weak or selective instruction, employer-controlled discipline, forfeitable payments, exclusion from skilled status, or a contract whose valuable reciprocal obligations arrive only after the apprentice has already supplied years of labor. The label **apprenticeship** therefore proves very little by itself.

The research question is not "Is apprenticeship good?" It is: **What institution actually existed, who controlled it, what did each side owe, what was actually delivered, who could exit, what was forfeited, and what happened afterward?**

## Do not collapse unlike institutions

Keep at least these categories separate unless a source itself establishes the relationship:

1. craft/guild apprenticeship;
2. pauper or orphan apprenticeship/binding-out;
3. indentured servitude;
4. nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century industrial apprenticeship;
5. helper systems and informal workplace learning;
6. union/joint apprenticeship;
7. modern registered apprenticeship;
8. pre-apprenticeship and other training marketed with apprenticeship language.

Historical vocabulary overlaps. Legal powers, age, voluntariness, family/state involvement, payment, training obligations, exit rights, and occupational outcomes can differ radically.

## Core measurement questions

For every historical or modern case, preserve where possible:

- entry conditions and selection;
- age, sex, race, class, family status, prior schooling, and migration status;
- master/employer/sponsor identity;
- exact term and probationary period;
- wages, premiums, fees, bonds, tuition, tools, room and board;
- instruction promised;
- instruction actually received;
- skilled/trade work versus errands, household work, helper work, or other unrelated labor;
- who controlled discipline;
- who adjudicated disputes;
- whether the adjudicator had a financial interest in the outcome;
- grounds for discharge, cancellation, or extension of the term;
- apprentice exit rights and practical ability to use them;
- money, tools, clothing, credentials, or other end-of-term benefits promised;
- end-of-term benefits actually received;
- completion, cancellation, disappearance, transfer, and still-active counts with denominators preserved;
- transition to journeyman/skilled-worker status;
- later movement to independent/master/employer status where that was part of the institution's social promise;
- exclusion mechanisms and restrictions on entry to the occupation.

## Blackball comparison rule

Do not reason:

**college can fail -> therefore skilled trades/apprenticeship are the answer.**

The correct inference is:

**one familiar institution failed the accountability test -> apply the same test to the proposed alternative before recommending it.**

That means investigating apprenticeship with the same hostility to advertising language that Blackball applies to universities: inspect contracts, actual training, completion, cancellation, wages, access, employer incentives, union rules, occupational demand, and post-training outcomes.

## Relationship to engineering, trades, and "progress"

Use apprenticeship to test rather than repeat status stories about engineers, technicians, tradespeople, production workers, maintenance workers, and sales workers. Record who actually designed, fabricated, installed, commissioned, operated, maintained, repaired, inspected, sold, and financed a product or system, along with training costs, authority, pay, bargaining structure, and exposure to unemployment or outsourcing.

No occupation gets presumptive moral or economic superiority from its cultural status.
