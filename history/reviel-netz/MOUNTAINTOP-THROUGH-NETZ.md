# Martin Luther King Jr.'s “I've Been to the Mountaintop” through Reviel Netz

Martin Luther King Jr. delivered “I've Been to the Mountaintop” at Mason Temple in Memphis on 3 April 1968. Near the beginning, King imagines being allowed to move through history. His panorama passes through Egypt, classical Greece, the Roman Empire, the Renaissance, Martin Luther, Abraham Lincoln, and Franklin D. Roosevelt's America. In Greece he specifically names Plato, Aristotle, Socrates, Euripides, and Aristophanes.

This note asks a deliberately different question from King's rhetorical one:

**If we keep King's list of canonical people and periods, what happens when we analyze their achievement using the kind of historical explanation Reviel Netz applies to Greek intellectual culture?**

This is not an attempt to put invented sentences into Netz's mouth. The entries below are labeled according to whether Netz directly supplies the claim, his published framework strongly supports the extension, or the connection is our own research hypothesis.

## There is a direct Netz–King bridge

The comparison is not entirely external. In the concluding “Post-miracle” chapter of *Why the Ancient Greeks Matter* (2025), Netz explicitly invokes Martin Luther King Jr.; “Martin Luther King Jr.” is among the chapter's keywords. Netz argues that the Greek canon's important historical function came from performance, internal friction, and traditions of open contest rather than from possession of a set of timeless ethnic values. He closes by retaining admiration for liberating past experience as part of a hopeful attitude toward the “arc of the moral universe.”

So King is already inside Netz's own concluding argument about what, if anything, should survive from the old story of the “Greek miracle.”

https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/why-the-ancient-greeks-matter/postmiracle/D09A336F9CB920645EFCC5EC2472B29B

## First correction: patronage is important, but “rich guys paid philosophers” is too narrow

A fair compression of Netz's recent books is:

**Greek intellectual achievement was historically contingent, not evidence of a Greek racial essence.**

But his positive explanation uses more than patronage. Across *Why the Ancient Greeks Matter*, *Scale, Space and Canon in Ancient Literary Culture*, *A New History of Greek Mathematics*, and his earlier work on deduction, the ecology includes:

- named authors competing for recognition;
- novelty and surprise as intellectual goods;
- public performance, argument, and polemic;
- cities and institutions concentrating writers, readers, teachers, books, and money;
- patrons and political elites supporting cultural production;
- a sufficiently large “bibliosphere” for texts to answer and transform other texts;
- manuscript and educational traditions that transmit work;
- contact with Near Eastern mathematical traditions;
- later canonization and repeated acts of reception.

In *Scale, Space and Canon*, patronage is nonetheless concrete enough to matter quantitatively: Netz argues that when patronage dried up in the third century CE, the scale of ancient literary production collapsed. Patronage is therefore one causal variable in an institutional ecology, not a synonym for the whole explanation.

| **Plato** | Do not begin with an isolated genius who somehow embodies “Greek thought.” Ask about named authorship, agonistic writing, schools and audiences, and a culture in which intellectual prestige could be won by answering and surpassing predecessors. | **Aristophanes** | The same ecology supports comedy as public polemic. Aristophanes is especially useful against a sanitized “Greek miracle”: canonical culture can be mocking, partisan, obscene, self-critical, and openly hostile to other canonical figures. | **Roman Empire** | Netz's story does not stop at Greece. Greek textual practices were reconfigured under Roman conditions, including elite leisure, patronage, copying, libraries, education, and networks spanning a much larger political space. | **Renaissance** | A Netz-style account asks why ancient texts became newly productive in particular Italian courts and cities. | **Martin Luther** | Luther is a strong non-Greek test of the method. | **Abraham Lincoln** | King's scene of Lincoln signing the Emancipation Proclamation is deliberately concentrated in one person. ## Plato: the “great mind” becomes an agonistic author

A particularly useful Netz move is to replace “Greek genius” with competition among named authors. In *Why the Ancient Greeks Matter*, Greek science is described as departing from earlier algorithmic traditions in part through a system in which a **named author seeks fame** by producing new and surprising results and presenting them in proof.

That does not make authorship an illusion. It changes the causal question. Instead of:

> Why was Plato so much smarter than everyone else?

ask:

> What social world made an identifiable author, argumentative novelty, a durable text, students/readers, and centuries of response into a stable route to prestige?

The second question can explain both individual achievement and why achievement clusters historically.

## Aristotle: Netz explicitly rejects making one thinker stand for “the Greeks”

The Archimedes Palimpsest lecture supplies the sharpest correction to King's compressed panorama. Netz notes that historians can cite Aristotle and slide immediately into “the Greeks believed…” He resists that move because Archimedes can provide evidence for mathematical practices that do not fit Aristotle's philosophical prescriptions.

This matters beyond Aristotle. Canonical figures survive partly because later readers make them representatives. Netz's method restores disagreement among contemporaries and asks what practices were actually used.

## Socrates, Euripides, Aristophanes: public argument is infrastructure

Netz's recent account of Athens emphasizes a hyper-performative culture: drama, public speech, debate, and outspoken intellectual figures. King's choice of Socrates, Euripides, and Aristophanes is therefore almost a list of outputs from the same civic machine.

A Netz-style causal map would include:

- recurrent public audiences;
- institutions that rewarded performance;
- competition among speakers and playwrights;
- wealthy citizens and public mechanisms underwriting expensive performances;
- dense face-to-face political life;
- copying and education that selected a small subset for canonization.

The claim is not that institutions wrote the works. It is that a history explaining only the author leaves out why this kind of authorial production became common, competitive, visible, and transmissible in that place and period.

## Renaissance: patronage becomes impossible to ignore

King jumps from Rome to the Renaissance. Here the user's patronage formulation becomes especially concrete. The Metropolitan Museum's histories of Renaissance Florence describe Medici funding as central to Florence's role as a center of humanist scholarship and art; Lorenzo de' Medici gathered artists, the poet Poliziano, and philosophers including Marsilio Ficino and Pico della Mirandola.

A Netz-style comparison would ask whether the famous cluster of Renaissance “geniuses” is partly the visible output of unusually concentrated patronage, institutions, manuscripts, workshops, libraries, and prestige competition. That is structurally close to Netz's explanation of ancient literary and scientific ecologies even though the institutions are not identical.

## Martin Luther: printing + university + protector

Luther is perhaps the cleanest test outside antiquity because the surrounding machinery is so visible.

The Library of Congress describes the printing press as profoundly shaping the Reformation and notes that the German print industry turned Luther into a bestselling author. It also records that the University of Wittenberg had been founded by Elector Frederick the Wise, who supported and protected Luther.

Sources:
https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/dres/dres2.html
https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/dres/dres1.html

A purely heroic story says Luther challenged Europe. An institutional story asks why *this* challenge could be duplicated, sold, translated, protected, debated, and carried across jurisdictions before a central authority could extinguish it.

## Lincoln and Roosevelt: offices can amplify individuals

The same reasoning applies when King's figures are political rather than intellectual.

The Emancipation Proclamation is not merely a text authored by Lincoln. The document grounds itself in Lincoln's constitutional authority as President and Commander in Chief during an armed rebellion. A Netz-style history therefore has to include the institution that gives the named author operative power.

Library of Congress primary-source record:
https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbpe.22904500/

Roosevelt's “fear itself” line comes from the first inaugural address of 4 March 1933, in the emergency setting of the Great Depression. A serious Netz-style extension would spider the institutions that transformed presidential speech into national political action: broadcasting, newspapers, party organization, Congress, federal administration, banks, and emergency legislation.

FDR Library recording/context:
https://www.fdrlibrary.org/first-inaugural-address

## King's speech itself partly escapes its own great-person opening

The opening time-travel passage is canonically organized around famous civilizations and famous men. But the rest of “I've Been to the Mountaintop” moves toward **collective causal power**: masses of people rising, organized sanitation workers, boycotts, economic withdrawal, churches, unions, and coordinated nonviolent action.

That creates a useful tension rather than a contradiction. King uses great historical names as rhetorical landmarks while arguing in Memphis that ordinary organized people can become historical causes.

A Netz-style reading therefore need not attack King's list. It can split two questions:

1. **Why do certain individuals become unusually creative or consequential?**
2. **What social machinery makes their creativity or consequence durable, scalable, and historically visible?**

Netz is particularly useful for the second question.

## Working thesis for Blackball

The comparison currently supports this hypothesis:

> What older histories called a succession of “great minds” may often be better modeled as a succession of unusually productive intellectual and political ecologies. Individual differences remain real, but concentrations of patrons, audiences, institutions, technologies of reproduction, argument, and political power help explain why canonical achievement clusters in particular places and periods—and why later institutions remember a few named people as if they generated the surrounding world by themselves.

That is a **Blackball synthesis**, not a quotation or declared thesis by Netz.
