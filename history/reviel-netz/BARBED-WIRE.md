# *Barbed Wire: An Ecology of Modernity*

Reviel Netz, *Barbed Wire: An Ecology of Modernity* (Wesleyan University Press, 2004; later paperback edition).

Publisher:
https://www.weslpress.org/9780819569592/barbed-wire/

## Scope

This is Netz's major work outside ancient mathematics. Wesleyan describes it as a history of humans and animals through barbed wire, following the technology from its nineteenth-century use in controlling cattle and transforming the American West to twentieth-century systems for controlling people, including Nazi concentration camps and the Soviet Gulag.

The organizing concept is **movement prevented by a cheap material technology**. That makes the book useful well beyond a narrow history of fencing.

## Blackball themes

### Technology as spatial control

Barbed wire matters because it changes what can be done to bodies over large areas at low cost. A fence does not merely symbolize ownership or a boundary; it physically modifies the possible movements of animals and people.

### Animals and humans belong in one history

The book deliberately refuses a clean split between an earlier “agricultural” history of cattle and a later “political” history of camps. The same family of material techniques for constraining animal movement becomes part of systems for constraining human movement.

### Settler expansion and enclosure

The American-West part of the story should be cross-linked to land enclosure, ranching, settler colonialism, indigenous dispossession, railroad/logistics history, and changing property regimes. These are thematic links until an exact source relation is checked.

### Camps, prisons, and coercive infrastructure

Later uses belong with the history of concentration camps, the Gulag, military perimeter systems, prisons, borders, and other technologies that make confinement materially cheap and scalable.

### “Ecology of modernity”

The subtitle signals that Netz is not writing a simple invention-to-adoption chronology. The object sits inside relations among bodies, landscapes, animals, states, markets, and violence. This makes the book relevant to environmental history and the history of technology as well as political history.

## Source-spider plan

The book's own notes/bibliography need a separate verified spider. When inspected, classify sources into at least:

- invention/patent/manufacturing history;
- cattle ranching and fencing;
- American West and land policy;
- indigenous history;
- military uses;
- colonial uses;
- concentration camps and genocide;
- Gulag/prison systems;
- histories of animals and domestication;
- histories/theories of space, boundaries, and confinement.

Do not treat later works on borders or camps as sources *for Netz* unless the direction of the relationship is verified. Those should instead be stored as reception or topical links.
