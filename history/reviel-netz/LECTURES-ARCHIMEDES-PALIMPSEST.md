# Reviel Netz lectures on the Archimedes Palimpsest

This is the preferred entry point to the Reviel Netz material in Blackball.

The lectures are useful before the books because Netz can show, in one object, how mathematical argument, diagrams, manuscript transmission, conservation, imaging, philology, and historical interpretation fit together. They also contain an important methodological warning: do not turn “the Greeks” into a single mind. Greek intellectual culture was internally argumentative, and an Archimedean practice can conflict with what Aristotle says Greek mathematics ought to be.

#### Lecture notes

**The object and its transmission**

- A tenth-century manuscript containing Archimedean works was later scraped and overwritten as a Byzantine prayer book.
- The prayer book was dedicated in Jerusalem in 1229.
- Johan Ludvig Heiberg examined the manuscript in 1906 and recovered important Archimedean material before the codex subsequently disappeared from scholarly access.
- It re-emerged and was sold in 1998, after which a long conservation, imaging, and scholarly project began.

**Imaging is part of the evidence chain**

Netz describes ultraviolet/multispectral methods and X-ray fluorescence as ways of separating overwritten layers and recovering text that cannot be read reliably from ordinary photographs. For Blackball, preserve the chain:

physical leaf → image/data → transcription → edited Greek text → mathematical/historical interpretation.

A conclusion at the right-hand end should never be silently treated as if it were simply visible on the parchment.

**The Method and infinity**

Netz uses new readings in Archimedes' *Method* to discuss what he calls evidence relevant to actual infinity. His own caution matters more than the headline: one should not conclude “the Greeks believed X.” Greek authors disagreed, and Aristotle is not a proxy for every Greek mathematician.

**The Stomachion and combinatorics**

The Palimpsest supplies material for reconstructing Archimedes' *Stomachion*. Netz presents the possibility that the work was concerned with counting arrangements of a dissection puzzle, which would put combinatorial play much nearer the center of Archimedes' activity than the standard canonical picture suggests.

**Diagrams are not illustrations pasted onto proofs**

The lecture emphasizes that ancient mathematical diagrams can carry logical work. Modern printed editions often regularized diagrams according to modern expectations. Reconstructing manuscript diagrams can therefore change how an argument is understood.

**The larger lesson**

The Palimpsest does not reveal a single timeless “Greek mind.” It reveals competing mathematical styles, contingent manuscript survival, and a modern chain of technical and scholarly labor through which an ancient argument becomes readable again.

## Netz's own warning about the discovery story

The official project essay is a useful corrective to publicity around the manuscript. Netz stresses that the Palimpsest's importance is often exaggerated. Most of Archimedes was known independently; Heiberg had already seen the manuscript in 1906; the post-1998 project made damaged and previously inaccessible evidence substantially more legible and enabled new readings.

Blackball should therefore avoid the false story:

> lost Archimedes → suddenly discovered in 1998 → ancient mathematics rewritten from scratch.

The better provenance chain is:

> ancient composition → Byzantine copying → palimpsesting → partial 1906 recovery → disappearance → 1998 re-emergence → conservation/imaging → new transcription → argument among historians of mathematics.

## Why these lectures lead into Netz's broader historical argument

The lecture already contains the move that matters for *Why the Ancient Greeks Matter* and *Scale, Space and Canon*: explain intellectual achievement through a historically specific ecology instead of an ethnic essence.

That ecology includes competition among named authors, public performance and argument, institutions of patronage, concentrations of readers and books, manuscript transmission, and the material technologies that preserve or destroy texts. Patronage matters, but it is not the whole explanation.
