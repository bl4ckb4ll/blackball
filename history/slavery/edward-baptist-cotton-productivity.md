# Edward Baptist — cotton productivity, coercion, and the limits of technology-as-progress stories

## Why Blackball is recording this

Edward E. Baptist is useful because he makes a quantitative fact about enslaved cotton pickers central rather than incidental: cotton picked per worker per day rose dramatically during the antebellum period. He then asks what actually produced that increase.

That matters for Blackball's broader treatment of technology and progress. A rise in output per worker is not, by itself, evidence that machines improved work or that workers experienced progress. Output can also rise through more intense supervision, quotas, longer or harder effort, threats, and violence.

The historical dispute is therefore especially valuable because it forces the causal question into the open: **how much of the productivity increase came from biological or technical change, and how much came from extracting more work from enslaved people?**

## The productivity numbers

Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode's cotton-picking series is the numerical starting point used across this debate. A 2026 reassessment by Sven Beckert and Mark Stelzner restates the series as follows:

- average cotton picked per enslaved worker per day: **28.1 pounds in 1801**;
- average cotton picked per enslaved worker per day: **114.2 pounds in 1862**;
- more than a fourfold increase;
- about **2.3 percent average annual productivity growth**.

Beckert and Stelzner also note that this rate was roughly on par with output-per-worker growth in the Manchester cotton mills during the height of the British Industrial Revolution. Yet the act of cotton picking remained manual: enslaved workers still harvested cotton bolls by hand.

This is the comparison Blackball should preserve. Manchester is the familiar technology story: machinery, mills, industrial equipment. The cotton field shows that a similarly striking measured productivity trajectory can occur where the worker is still using their hands.

Source:

- Sven Beckert and Mark Stelzner, "Productivity Growth of Enslaved Workers," *Social Science History*, published online July 23, 2026: https://doi.org/10.1017/ssh.2026.10131
- Alan L. Olmstead and Paul W. Rhode, "Biological Innovation and Productivity Growth in the Antebellum Cotton Economy," *Journal of Economic History* 68, no. 4 (2008): https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050708000811

## Baptist's causal argument

In *The Half Has Never Been Told* Baptist argues that plantation labor organization converted measurement into coercion. His account emphasizes a system in which:

1. each picker had an individualized daily target;
2. cotton was weighed and recorded;
3. yesterday's demonstrated output could become tomorrow's expected minimum;
4. failure to meet the target exposed the worker to whipping or other punishment;
5. enslaved people therefore had to discover ways to pick faster under an externally imposed ratchet.

Baptist calls this a system of **"calibrated torture"** and uses the image of a **"whipping machine."** The core claim is not merely that slavery was violent. It is that violence, measurement, accounting, and escalating expectations formed a labor-control system capable of increasing measured manual productivity.

Blackball can describe this as analogous to scientific management or proto-Taylorist labor control, but should not confuse the analogy with chronology: Frederick Winslow Taylor's formal system came later. The useful comparison is the combination of measurement, individual output targets, monitoring, and management pressure.

## Public lecture evidence

Baptist repeated this argument in public lectures, not only in the book.

### Boston College, October 4, 2016

The Boisi Center's event page for "Slavery and the Making of American Capitalism" is particularly useful because it preserves both the abstract and a written recap of the lecture.

The abstract says that the expansion of slavery drove U.S. modernization and that, before the Civil War, important economic innovations included ways to make slavery more profitable; it specifically says forced migration and torture extracted continual increases in efficiency from enslaved African Americans.

The recap places the argument directly beside the standard machinery story. It describes new British machinery and the factory system as expanding world demand for cotton, then describes enslavers responding with higher picking quotas, ledgers, coercion, and torture. The recap gives the figure of roughly 2 percent more cotton picked per work day with each passing year and summarizes Baptist's position as "the whip, not seeds" driving the growth.

## The major dispute: torture, seeds, and work intensity

The productivity increase itself is not the main point of disagreement. The fight is over causal attribution.

### Olmstead and Rhode

Olmstead and Rhode argue that improved cotton varieties were a major driver of rising picking rates. New varieties could produce taller plants, more lint per boll, and cotton that was easier to remove from the boll. Their later work directly criticizes Baptist's claim that increasingly efficient torture explains the long-run productivity series and argues that his account does not fit the plantation records or the full body of former-slave testimony.

- Olmstead and Rhode, "Biological Innovation and Productivity Growth in the Antebellum Cotton Economy," 2008: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050708000811
- Alan L. Olmstead and Paul W. Rhode, "Cotton, slavery, and the new history of capitalism," *Explorations in Economic History* 67 (2018): 1–17: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eeh.2017.12.002

### Clegg and other criticism

John Clegg and other critics have likewise argued that Baptist's evidence does not establish a continuous rise in torture sufficient to explain the productivity series, and that some of the testimony used by Baptist is selective or misread.

- John Clegg, "Capitalism and Slavery," *Critical Historical Studies* 2, no. 2 (2015): 281–304: https://doi.org/10.1086/683036

### Beckert and Stelzner, 2026

The 2026 Beckert-Stelzner paper is important because it does not simply reproduce either pole of the older argument. It says the existing evidence had been inconclusive, accepts that biological innovation mattered, but finds substantial evidence that increased monitoring and pressure to labor more intensely were also significant drivers of increased output per enslaved worker.

Their formulation is narrower than Baptist's strongest claim. They explicitly note that increasing work intensity does **not** require a continuously increasing frequency of torture. The mechanism could involve tighter monitoring, higher quotas, threats of violence, or combinations of these inside an already violent labor regime.

That distinction should be preserved:

- **Baptist's strongest thesis:** calibrated torture and the quota ratchet are central causal engines of the long-run increase.
- **Olmstead-Rhode:** biological innovation in cotton varieties explains much of the increase; Baptist overstates torture-led growth.
- **Beckert-Stelzner 2026:** seeds mattered, but intensified supervision and forced work effort also appear to have been significant; the evidence does not require an ever-rising number of whippings.

## Technology-and-progress connection

This case should be cross-linked whenever Blackball encounters a story in which "technology" or "innovation" is inferred from a productivity chart.

Separate at least four mechanisms:

1. **mechanical technology** — machines, tools, power systems;
2. **biological technology** — crop varieties, breeding, seed characteristics;
3. **organizational technology** — task division, measurement, accounting, scheduling, supervision;
4. **coercive labor intensification** — quotas, surveillance, threats, punishment, longer or harder work.

The moral and material consequences of these mechanisms are radically different even when they produce the same statistical object: more output per worker.

A productivity graph can therefore be a very bad proxy for progress. It records a numerator and denominator; it does not automatically record pain, exhaustion, autonomy, bargaining power, punishment, leisure, risk, or who captures the additional output.

## Metrics Blackball should preserve

When this material is turned into measurable comparisons, keep separate variables rather than collapsing everything into "productivity":

- pounds picked per worker-day;
- pounds picked per worker-hour, when hours can actually be estimated;
- daily quota and change in quota over time;
- hours in the field;
- punishment frequency and type;
- threats of punishment even when punishment was not carried out;
- overseer-to-worker ratio or other monitoring intensity;
- use of written ledgers and individual output records;
- cotton variety / seed characteristics;
- land quality and geographic movement;
- worker age, sex, experience, and health where records permit;
- free-labor comparison after emancipation;
- share of increased output captured by the worker versus the owner.

The essential distinction is **increased productive capacity** versus **increased extraction of effort**. Both can raise observed output per person. Only the first can safely be treated as a technological improvement without additional evidence.
