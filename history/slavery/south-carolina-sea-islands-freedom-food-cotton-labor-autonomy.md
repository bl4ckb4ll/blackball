# South Carolina Sea Islands — freedom, food crops, cotton, and control of labor

## Why Blackball is recording this

The South Carolina Sea Islands are unusually useful for studying what enslaved agricultural workers did when plantation coercion was suddenly weakened or removed.

After Union forces captured Port Royal in November 1861, white planters fled and left thousands of enslaved people behind. The result was one of the earliest large-scale experiments in Union-protected freedom during the Civil War. Contemporary observers immediately encountered a labor problem that is easy to flatten in later economic histories: many freedpeople were willing to cultivate food for themselves but were strongly reluctant to resume cotton production on plantation terms.

The strongest defensible version of the point is **not** that freedpeople did not care about money or permanently rejected markets. It is that they initially prioritized food security, land, family subsistence, and control of their own labor over producing cotton for other people. Cotton had been the crop of coerced labor. When cotton later became voluntary, paid, or connected to farming one's own land, many freedpeople did grow it.

That distinction matters. The Sea Islands supply unusually direct evidence about worker preferences immediately after an enormous change in bargaining power.

## Evidence-state summary

**Direct contemporary evidence supports:**

- freedpeople planted corn and potatoes in their own patches while showing strong reluctance to plant cotton;
- they associated cotton with slavery and with enriching masters rather than feeding their own households;
- observers believed that, if left to themselves in 1862, many would have produced sufficient food but little or no export cotton;
- when cotton work became paid and more clearly voluntary, resistance diminished;
- after the war, Sea Islands freedpeople repeatedly sought land and independent production and often resisted becoming hired plantation laborers.

**The evidence does not support:**

- a blanket claim that freedpeople were indifferent to money;
- a claim that they permanently refused to grow cotton;
- a literal claim that the Port Royal freedpeople were the first enslaved Americans anywhere to become free. Contraband communities such as Fort Monroe appeared earlier in 1861. Port Royal is better described as one of the earliest *large communities* placed under sustained Union control and one of the first large-scale emancipation experiments.

## 1862: food first, cotton resisted

Edward L. Pierce's official 1862 reports from Port Royal are the most useful primary source for this point.

Pierce reported that freedpeople had already begun putting **corn and potatoes into their own patches**, while no comparable preparation had been made for cotton. He said they were strongly disinclined to plant cotton, readily understood why corn was necessary for food, and distrusted promises that cotton work would later be paid. His sharpest summary was:

> “It had enriched the masters, but had not fed them.”

Pierce then made the counterfactual unusually explicit: if protected and left to themselves, he believed most would have raised enough corn and potatoes for food, but **no cotton or other exportable crop**.

Primary source:

- Edward L. Pierce, *The Freedmen of Port Royal, South-Carolina. Official Reports of Edward L. Pierce* (Boston, 1862), especially the discussion of crop preparation and labor preferences: https://archive.org/details/freedmenofportro00pier

This is stronger evidence than a later historian merely saying that freedpeople preferred subsistence. It is a contemporary administrator describing the crop mix that emerged immediately after planters fled and coercive plantation organization broke down.

## McKim's 1862 corroboration: cotton as the old enemy

J. Miller McKim, reporting on the same experiment in July 1862, gave a similar account. He said freedpeople were reluctant to work cotton while being ready to raise corn. He explicitly connected that reluctance to slavery: cotton was their old enemy and represented the labor regime from which they were escaping.

McKim also reproduced the acreage imbalance reported from Port Royal: substantially more acreage had been planted in provisions than in cotton.

Primary source:

- J. Miller McKim, *The Freedmen of South Carolina: An Address Delivered by J. Miller McKim, in Sansom Hall, July 9th, 1862* (Philadelphia, 1862), Library of Congress: https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbaapc.20100/

The evidentiary limitation is important: McKim was a white northern abolitionist observer, not a freedperson speaking in a surviving first-person transcript. His report corroborates behavior and contemporary interpretation, but it should not be substituted for direct testimony where direct testimony exists.

## The initial refusal was not a permanent rejection of cotton or markets

A year later, Pierce himself described an important change. In his 1863 account, he said the earlier idea that freedpeople would raise no more cotton had largely passed away. Once cotton labor was **remunerative** and increasingly connected to a prospect of ownership and self-direction, people repaired cotton gins and voluntarily planted cotton in addition to adequate corn.

Primary source:

- Edward L. Pierce, “The Freedmen at Port Royal,” 1863: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Freedmen_at_Port_Royal

This makes the interpretation much cleaner. What changed was not simply a sudden discovery that money was desirable. The institutional terms of the work changed. Cotton produced under slavery for an owner was one thing; cotton grown voluntarily for wages or on one's own account was another.

A useful economic phrasing is therefore:

> **When exit from plantation coercion became possible, freed workers shifted labor toward food and own-account production; cotton labor returned when compensation, security, and control improved.**

That is a claim about labor supply, bargaining power, and control of output. It is more defensible than describing the Sea Islanders as simply anti-market.

## 1865–66: the land-and-autonomy question persists

The same preference for independent production did not disappear when the war ended.

Documents published by the Freedmen and Southern Society Project show former slaves across the Sea Islands struggling over land restoration, labor contracts, and whether they would work for former owners or outside employers. In January 1866, an agent describing James, Johns, Wadmalaw, and Morris Islands reported that many people were unwilling to contract and were determined **“to work for no one but themselves.”** Migrants were also arriving on the islands intending to find land and make crops for themselves.

At St. Helena, other 1865 reports documented freedpeople who had acquired small holdings, livestock, tools, houses, and in some cases cotton crops of their own. That is particularly useful because it rules out an over-simple interpretation: independence and subsistence could coexist with commercial production.

Documentary collection:

- Freedmen and Southern Society Project, University of Maryland, *Freedom: A Documentary History of Emancipation, 1861–1867*, especially the land-and-labor and wartime free-labor materials: https://freedmen.umd.edu/

## What the Sea Islands reveal about plantation “productivity”

This material belongs beside Blackball's note on Edward Baptist and cotton productivity.

Antebellum plantation statistics tell us how much cotton enslavers succeeded in extracting under a coercive labor regime. They do **not** tell us what workers would have chosen to produce under freedom.

The Port Royal evidence gives something close to a revealed-preference experiment. When workers acquired substantially greater control over their labor:

1. food production rose in relative importance;
2. plantation cotton production became difficult to induce;
3. cotton became more acceptable when people were paid, could own land, or could capture more of the crop's benefit themselves.

This does not by itself quantify how much antebellum cotton output was caused by coercion. It does show that the slave-era crop mix cannot be interpreted as though enslaved workers had freely selected it.

## Eric Foner and the scholarly Sea Islands tradition

Eric Foner's *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* belongs to a much larger scholarly literature that treats the Sea Islands as a crucial test case for emancipation, land, labor, and the meaning of freedom.

A useful research trail is:

- Willie Lee Rose, *Rehearsal for Reconstruction: The Port Royal Experiment* (1964). A foundational monograph focused directly on Port Royal and the Sea Islands.
- Joel Williamson, *After Slavery: The Negro in South Carolina During Reconstruction, 1861–1877* (1965).
- Martin Abbott, *The Freedmen's Bureau in South Carolina, 1865–1872* (1967).
- Eric Foner, *Nothing But Freedom: Emancipation and Its Legacy* (1983).
- Eric Foner, *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* (1988).
- Ira Berlin et al., eds., *Freedom: A Documentary History of Emancipation, 1861–1867*, Series I, vol. 3, *The Wartime Genesis of Free Labor: The Lower South* (1990).
- Julie Saville, *The Work of Reconstruction: From Slave to Wage Laborer in South Carolina, 1860–1870* (1994).
- Akiko Ochiai, *Harvesting Freedom: African American Agrarianism in Civil War Era South Carolina* (2004).
- Ira Berlin et al., eds., *Freedom: A Documentary History of Emancipation, 1861–1867*, Series III, vol. 1, *Land and Labor, 1865* (2008), and the companion volume on 1866–67.

This list is a **research trail**, not a claim that every item appears in a particular Foner endnote. Individual citation relationships should be checked against the relevant edition and page before being represented as Foner's own bibliography.

## A useful Blackball formulation

The striking Sea Islands fact is not merely that formerly enslaved people disliked cotton. It is that the first large experiments in freedom altered what they chose to produce and whom they were willing to work for.

Under slavery, plantation owners could make cotton production look like the natural economic activity of the region. When freedpeople got some ability to choose, many initially chose something much more elementary: **food, family subsistence, land, and work directed by themselves.** Cotton did not vanish, but it had to be placed on different terms.

That is a compact historical warning against treating observed production under coercion as evidence of worker preference.
