# Louis Hughes — *Thirty Years a Slave* (1897)

Primary source by Louis Hughes (1832–1913), published in Milwaukee in 1897 as *Thirty Years a Slave: From Bondage to Freedom: The Institution of Slavery as Seen on the Plantation and in the Home of the Planter*.

## Full text and archival copies

- Library of Congress: https://www.loc.gov/item/11021103/
- Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10431
- Gutenberg HTML text used for passage checks: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10431/10431-h/10431-h.htm

The Library of Congress describes Hughes as having been born in Virginia around 1832, sold in 1844, enslaved principally in Mississippi and Tennessee, repeatedly attempting escape, and finally reaching freedom during the Civil War. After emancipation he eventually settled in Milwaukee and worked as a nurse, drawing on medical skills learned while enslaved.

## Blackball: skill is not bargaining power

This is an unusually clean historical counterexample to any story in which acquiring a skilled trade by itself creates worker bargaining power.

Hughes says that Edmund McGee owned **Uncle Ben, a skilled blacksmith**, for whom McGee had paid **$1,800**. The plantation depended on enslaved blacksmithing, carpentry, woodworking and repair work. Hughes says that most farm implements were manufactured on the plantation and that keeping them repaired occupied the blacksmith and his assistant, including substantial work at night.

The critical point is not merely that an enslaved person could possess a skilled trade. It is that the skill could increase the economic value of the person **to the owner without giving the skilled worker the corresponding wage, freedom, exit option, or bargaining power**. Uncle Ben's blacksmithing skill did not make him cease to be enslaved. In a system where the worker has no lawful outside option, human capital can raise the owner's asset value rather than the worker's compensation.

Use this as an extreme limiting case for the Blackball labor-market framework:

- skill and bargaining power are different variables;
- productive value does not automatically become worker compensation;
- a worker can be highly skilled and economically important while having essentially no power to refuse work;
- the value of a skill may be captured by whoever controls access to work, property, movement, or legal rights;
- models of wages that move directly from skill/productivity to compensation omit the institutional question: **who is able to claim the value created?**

This does not imply that present-day wage labor is slavery. It gives a boundary case that makes the logical distinction impossible to miss: skill is one possible source of leverage only when the worker possesses institutions and outside options through which that skill can actually be exercised elsewhere.

## Louis Hughes: intelligence, medical aptitude, and a denied arena

Hughes himself is another strong case for separating demonstrated or plausible ability from access to the arena in which ability can be developed and recognized.

McGee's runaway advertisement described Hughes as **"bright and intelligent."** Hughes recounts that McGee, who had previously practiced medicine, taught him the names and properties of medicines, their uses, and dosages. Hughes says he **"seemed to grasp the idea very well."** McGee repeatedly drilled him until he knew the material and then added more.

Hughes later prepared medicines from McGee's medical book, compounded the preparations while McGee read the recipes, administered doses under direction, and cared for sick enslaved people. He says that working with medicine and caring for the sick were the parts of his work he liked best. After emancipation he was employed as a nurse, including nursing a physician.

He also reports that after slavery he attended a freedmen's night school because one of the bitterest injuries of slavery was having been denied education. He later produced this substantial autobiographical book.

### Counterfactual note

The statement **"Hughes could probably have become a doctor if he had not been enslaved"** cannot be proved from the surviving evidence and should not be stored as an established historical fact. It is, however, a serious Blackball counterfactual rather than an empty compliment. The evidence shows intellectual ability recognized even by his owner, unusual interest and competence in medical work, practical experience preparing and administering medicine, later nursing work, active pursuit of education after emancipation, and the ability to produce an extended written account of his life.

The relevant question is therefore not merely whether Hughes had a credential or ever became a physician. He was denied the ordinary educational and occupational route through which that possibility could have been tested. This is a case of **denial of the chance to try**, not evidence that the ability was absent.

For Blackball, use Hughes as a concrete example when discussing how many people who appear in the historical record only as laborers, servants, porters, field hands, craftspeople, or other subordinate workers may have had capacities that the surrounding institution never allowed them to develop or demonstrate.

## Whipping, witnessed violence, and psychological injury

Hughes repeatedly describes the effect of seeing other enslaved people tortured.

Early in the book he recounts an overseer whipping a sick man so severely that a physician had to wrap the man's body in a poultice. The wounds left a running sore that Hughes says never healed, and the man was sent back to field work once he could walk.

In the section on overseers and whippings, Hughes says that even after becoming partly accustomed to severe treatment, events would still occur that made him **"wish I were dead."** He immediately recounts the flogging of Riley, whose delay at work resulted in a bull-whip beating severe enough that the whip's knot became embedded in his arm and caused a festering wound.

### User recollection to preserve, but not misquote

The remembered formulation was that **watching another man be whipped made Hughes want to crawl out of his own skin and die**. The emotional meaning is consistent with Hughes's account, but I have not found the phrase "crawl out of my own skin" in the text. Keep it as a paraphrase of the reaction, not a quotation. The text that has been verified says that recurring scenes of cruelty made him "wish I were dead."

This matters analytically because workplace domination is not exhausted by the direct injury to the person being beaten. Public and witnessed punishment disciplines everyone who sees it. A Blackball metric for coercive institutions should therefore distinguish:

- direct physical punishment;
- threat of punishment;
- compulsory witnessing of punishment;
- psychological injury from seeing punishment inflicted on others;
- exemplary punishment intended to govern the behavior of a wider group.

Hughes later describes enslaved people being deliberately forced to view the bodies of two people who had been hanged after trying to escape. That makes the group-discipline function explicit: terror was used not only against the immediate victim but as information sent to everyone else.
