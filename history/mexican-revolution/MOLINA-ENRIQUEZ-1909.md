# Andrés Molina Enríquez — *Los grandes problemas nacionales* (1909)

## Bibliographic node

**Andrés Molina Enríquez, *Los grandes problemas nacionales* (Mexico: Imprenta de A. Carranza e Hijos, 1909).**

Primary digital edition:

- Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes: [*Los grandes problemas nacionales*](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/nd/ark:/59851/bmc7h1w3/)

This is one of the most useful pre-revolutionary attempts to say, systematically, what was structurally wrong with Porfirian Mexico. It is especially valuable because it does not reduce the problem to Díaz personally. Molina moves through landholding, haciendas, credit, irrigation, population, race/class categories, foreign exploitation, and political power.

## Architecture of the book

The table of contents itself is evidence for the scope of the diagnosis.

### Part I — *Los antecedentes indeclinables*

Molina moves through geography and territory, historical development, the Reform laws and property, and finally **"El secreto de la paz porfiriana"**.

### Part II — *Los problemas de orden primordial*

The five named problems are:

1. property;
2. territorial credit;
3. irrigation;
4. population;
5. politics.

The point is not merely that Mexico had a bad president. The book attempts to identify a coupled social system whose pieces reinforce one another.

## The hacienda system

Molina's account repeatedly treats the **gran propiedad / hacienda** as a structural obstacle. He contrasts the large estate with smaller productive agriculture and describes the hacienda peon's position as bound up with debt, dependency, and inherited forms of subordination. Elsewhere he characterizes the hacendado as much closer to a rentier or feudal proprietor than to the productive agriculturist represented by the ranchero.

This makes the work useful for the question: **what, specifically, did an educated contemporary critic think was wrong with the hacienda system before the Revolution?**

It also matters that Molina did not arrive at this question only abstractly. His father was a lawyer and notary; Molina later worked in that practice and encountered the legal mechanics of land concentration and the dispossession of ranches and communal lands. His family also had landed-property connections, including Hacienda de Doxichó. Calling him simply "the son of a hacienda owner" therefore compresses a more interesting biography into a misleading slogan.

## Foreign ownership and exploitation

Molina discusses the post-independence opening of Mexico to foreigners as an opportunity for outside exploitation, including in mining and commerce. This belongs beside the [Guggenheim / ASARCO](GUGGENHEIM-ASARCO.md) node.

The citation rule here matters: Molina's structural discussion of foreigners and mines is evidence for **his diagnosis of foreign exploitation**. It is not automatically evidence that he named the Guggenheims or ASARCO. The Guggenheim claim has its own corporate and historical sources.

## Porfirismo and the científicos

The book is plainly a critique of the social and political arrangements of the Porfiriato, including concentration of effective political power and the property order that sustained the regime.

A later historiography often places Molina against the assumptions or prescriptions of the **científicos**. Keep that as a retrospective interpretive edge unless a particular primary-text passage explicitly names the political group. The word *científico* appearing as an ordinary adjective is not enough to establish such a citation or target.

## Readership versus influence

This is one of the most important distinctions in the source trail.

In 1912 Luis Cabrera described *Los grandes problemas nacionales* as a book that **almost nobody had read**, while at the same time crediting it with doing a great deal to clarify Mexico's economic and agrarian questions. The apparent contradiction is the point: the book did not need mass circulation to affect the small political and intellectual network that would formulate agrarian arguments and later revolutionary law.

A century later, Emilio Kourí's edited volume *En busca de Molina Enríquez: cien años de Los grandes problemas nacionales* deliberately returned to the same puzzle: a famous book, widely invoked, but often not actually read, whose reputation rests heavily on its role in revolutionary agrarian thought and the genealogy of Article 27.

So the safest formulation is:

> **limited direct readership; disproportionate elite/intellectual and legal influence.**

That is more precise than either "the book inspired the Mexican people" or "nobody read it, so it did not matter."

## From diagnosis to revolutionary land law

Molina's arguments fed into later agrarian reform debates. Luis Cabrera drew on related diagnoses, and Molina himself participated in the revolutionary-era legal/intellectual environment that culminated in the constitutional treatment of land and subsoil in **Article 27 of the 1917 Constitution**.

This does not make the 1909 book a blueprint copied line-for-line into Article 27. The useful research problem is to trace specific concepts and people from the book through Cabrera, agrarian legislation, commissions, debates, and the constitutional text.

## Intellectual baggage that should not be hidden

Molina's diagnosis also uses racial and evolutionary categories characteristic of some late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century positivist thought, including claims about superior/inferior social groups, indigenous populations, and mestizaje. These are part of the source and should be recorded rather than silently modernized away. They complicate any simple attempt to turn Molina into a present-day political spokesman.

## Questions for the citation spider

1. Extract Molina's explicit named authorities, books, laws, statistics, and historical sources from the 1909 text.
2. Separate his own empirical observations from borrowed theories and inherited racial/evolutionary schemes.
3. Trace Cabrera's direct citations or conceptual borrowings from Molina rather than relying only on later claims of influence.
4. Trace Molina → agrarian commissions/legislation → Article 27 with documents at each edge.
5. Inspect every chapter in Kourí's 2009 volume for both its Molina citations and its own bibliography.
6. Compare Molina's account of foreign capital with quantitative mining/ownership studies and with the concrete [Guggenheim / ASARCO](GUGGENHEIM-ASARCO.md) case.
