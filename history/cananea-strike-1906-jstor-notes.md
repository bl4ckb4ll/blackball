# Cananea strike, 1906 — first gunfire and JSTOR source notes

**Wikipedia hub:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cananea_strike

The Cananea strike began on June 1, 1906, at the Cananea Consolidated Copper Company in Sonora. Mexican workers protested unequal pay, long hours, discrimination in promotion, and the wider power of a U.S.-owned mining company operating under the Porfirian regime.

## Who fired first?

The narrow question is important because later accounts often collapse the sequence into “a riot.” The evidence more often describes an initially organized strike and march that became lethal at the company lumberyard.

A recurring sequence in Mexican accounts and in the modern synthesis used by Wikipedia is:

1. striking miners marched to the company lumberyard to bring the remaining workers into the strike;
2. George Metcalf and/or the Metcalf brothers used a high-pressure fire hose against the marchers;
3. strikers responded with stones and other improvised objects;
4. gunfire came from the lumberyard/Metcalf side and killed Mexican workers;
5. the crowd then attacked and killed George and William Metcalf and burned the lumberyard;
6. further gunfire followed elsewhere in Cananea, including firing by armed Americans.

That is stronger than a claim that the miners simply opened an armed attack. Ricardo Flores Magón's near-contemporary polemical account explicitly says the Mexican workers approached peacefully and “were received with bullets,” answering first with stones. Wikipedia's current narrative likewise says George Metcalf used the hose and then fired into the crowd.

There is still reason to preserve uncertainty over the exact micro-sequence. Some English-language retellings place a physical rush on George Metcalf before describing the first shots, while others place the first lethal shot before the crowd kills either brother. The safest formulation is therefore:

> The first lethal gunfire is most commonly attributed to company-side Americans at the lumberyard, after the use of a fire hose and stone throwing; surviving narratives differ over the precise seconds immediately before the shooting.

## JSTOR and scholarly sources

### D. L. Turner, “Arizona's Twenty-Four Hour War: The Arizona Rangers and the Cananea Copper Strike of 1906”

- *The Journal of Arizona History* 48, no. 3 (Autumn 2007), pp. 257–288.
- JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/41697060

This is one of the principal modern English-language reconstructions of the violence and the cross-border intervention. Wikipedia relies on Turner for the lumberyard sequence, the armed American response, inflated casualty reports, and the intervention of the Arizona volunteers/Rangers.

The larger significance of Turner's article is that Cananea cannot be treated only as a local labor riot. Armed men from Arizona crossed an international border and entered a Mexican labor conflict, creating an immediate sovereignty problem as well as a labor conflict.

### Nicolás Cárdenas García, “La huelga de Cananea en 1906. Una reinterpretación”

- *Estudios Sociológicos* 16, no. 46 (Jan.–Apr. 1998), pp. 117–146.
- JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/40420503
- El Colegio de México record/full-text route: https://estudiossociologicos.colmex.mx/index.php/es/article/view/641

Cárdenas explicitly presents the strike as something that deserves reinterpretation rather than repetition of the standard heroic or official narrative. It is useful for separating the industrial dispute itself from later revolutionary memory and for recovering the workers' concrete demands and organization.

For this corpus, this is especially valuable as a check against accounts that start with the later Mexican Revolution and read revolutionary intent backward into every act of the strikers.

### Michael J. Gonzales, “United States Copper Companies, the State, and Labour Conflict in Mexico, 1900–1910”

- *Journal of Latin American Studies* 26, no. 3 (Oct. 1994), pp. 651–681.
- JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/158311
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022216X00008555

Gonzales puts Cananea inside the political economy of Porfirian copper: rapid foreign investment, preferential treatment of foreign capital, discriminatory labor structures, falling real wages, and the political backlash produced by foreign corporate power.

His framing is important because it explains why the arrival of armed Americans from Arizona was explosive politically. The incident became not merely a strikebreaking episode but evidence, to Mexican critics of the Díaz regime, that foreign capital could call on armed U.S. citizens and Mexican authorities to defend its position.

### C. L. Sonnichsen, *Colonel Greene and the Copper Skyrocket*

- University of Arizona Press, 1974.
- JSTOR book: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1qwwjkg
- Chapter 18, “In Cold Blood”: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1qwwjkg.18
- Chapter 19, “In Hot Blood”: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1qwwjkg.19

Sonnichsen's biography of William Cornell Greene provides company-centered context. Chapters 18–19 cover the strike and violence. It is useful precisely because Greene's business empire, his relationships on both sides of the border, and his role in summoning aid are difficult to understand from a labor-history summary alone.

Read it against worker and Mexican political sources, not as a neutral last word: a biography centered on Greene naturally organizes the event around Greene's experience and documentary trail.

### Rodney D. Anderson, “Mexican Workers and the Politics of Revolution, 1906–1911”

- *Hispanic American Historical Review* 54, no. 1 (Feb. 1974), pp. 94–113.
- JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/2512841

Useful for the larger question of how labor conflict between 1906 and 1911 related to revolutionary politics. Cananea is often presented as a direct opening scene of the Mexican Revolution; Anderson helps put worker politics on its own timeline rather than assuming a simple straight line from strike to revolution.

### Herbert O. Brayer, “The Cananea Incident”

- *New Mexico Historical Review* 13, no. 4 (1938), pp. 387–415.
- Free University of New Mexico copy: https://digitalrepository.unm.edu/nmhr/vol13/iss4/5/

Not a JSTOR item, but too important to omit because later scholarship repeatedly cites it. Brayer compiled an early documentary reconstruction and preserves accounts of American firing on Mexican crowds. Because it was written in 1938, it is neither contemporary journalism nor modern revisionist scholarship; it sits between them and is useful for tracing how the standard English-language narrative formed.

## Contemporary political source

### Ricardo Flores Magón, June 1906 account

- Archivo Electrónico Ricardo Flores Magón: https://archivomagon.net/en/obras-completas/articulos-periodisticos/ap1906/

Flores Magón wrote from an explicitly anti-Díaz and revolutionary position, so this is partisan evidence, not detached reportage. But its timing matters. His account says Mexican workers approached American workers to seek solidarity and were met by gunfire, after which they answered with stones. It is valuable evidence for how the violence was immediately understood and politicized by the Mexican Liberal movement.

## Analysis

Cananea is a useful comparison with Homestead. In both cases later shorthand can make the confrontation sound as if labor violence simply appeared from nowhere. Reconstructing the sequence changes the picture.

At Cananea the core labor dispute concerned wages, hours, promotion, nationality and corporate hierarchy. The first major lethal confrontation occurred when strikers tried to extend the work stoppage to a company department still operating. Company-side resistance escalated from a fire hose to firearms; worker violence then became lethal in response. Later armed American intervention transformed an industrial dispute into a national-sovereignty scandal.

The historiographical dispute is therefore not only “who fired first?” but also **where the story begins**. Starting the story with the deaths of the Metcalf brothers makes the workers look like an attacking mob. Starting with the strike demands, march, hose, stones and first gunfire produces a substantially different causal sequence.
