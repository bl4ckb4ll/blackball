# Data-science master's: professor/student employment asymmetry

## Status

Research hypothesis seeded from repeated first-person observation. This note is deliberately **not** a population estimate or a claim that every data-science master's program has poor outcomes.

## Observation to preserve

A recurring asymmetry is worth testing:

- professors, instructors, and research-trained scientists associated with data science can have access to strong academic or corporate jobs;
- students paying for a data-science master's degree may have no comparable assurance of obtaining data-science work;
- some corporate data-science openings may draw applicants with PhDs, postdoctoral experience, or substantial domain-science experience who are leaving academic research;
- a new master's graduate can therefore be competing not merely with other new graduates, but with experienced scientific labor.

The important Blackball distinction is **teacher outcome versus student outcome**. A professor's ability to obtain an attractive job does not establish that the program reliably converts its students into the same employment market.

## Candidate mechanism: the master's as a Mulligan

One possible mechanism is:

1. a worker or recent graduate is dissatisfied with the employment options currently available;
2. borrowing makes another credential immediately purchasable even when the buyer does not have the cash price;
3. the graduate program offers a new recruiting window, a new credential label, and the hope of entering a better occupational category;
4. the school receives tuition whether or not that occupational transition happens;
5. if the transition fails, the student bears most of the debt, time, and opportunity-cost downside.

This is a hypothesis about incentives, not a claim about any particular student's motives or any particular program's intent.

## Claims to test

1. **Relevant corporate data-science jobs are scarce relative to the number of graduates seeking them.** This needs a denominator: graduates, credible applicants, openings, and hires over the same geography and time window.
2. **Research-trained scientists are an important competing labor pool.** Measure the prior education and prior occupations of people actually hired into corporate data-science roles.
3. **A data-science master's often does not substitute for prior scientific, engineering, statistical, or software experience.** Inspect postings and actual hires rather than curriculum descriptions alone.
4. **Faculty career success can create a misleading availability heuristic for students.** Compare faculty biographies with student first destinations and do not treat the former as evidence for the latter.
5. **Graduate school can operate as a recruiting reset after a disappointing first job search.** Determine how many entrants were already underemployed, unemployed, or trying to change fields, and whether the degree produced the intended transition.
6. **Easy access to educational borrowing can sustain enrollment even when placement risk is high.** Compare price, debt financed, completion, placement, and repayment outcomes rather than treating willingness to enroll as evidence of program value.

## What would count as strong evidence

For each program or cohort, prefer:

- entering cohort size;
- graduating cohort size;
- total program price and typical amount financed;
- prior degrees and prior occupations of entrants;
- internships and relevant work experience before graduation;
- first destination at fixed intervals after graduation;
- exact job title and employer, not merely `employed`;
- whether the job plausibly uses the advertised data-science training;
- salary distribution over the whole graduating cohort, with nonrespondents visible;
- number still seeking target-field work;
- number employed outside the target field;
- number continuing in education, with reason where available;
- response rate and survey methodology.

For the employer side, collect a sample of actual hires into roles labeled data scientist, research scientist, applied scientist, statistician, machine-learning scientist, and adjacent titles. Record highest degree, field, prior occupation, years of experience, publications/research background where relevant, and whether the person moved from academia.

## Failure modes to avoid

Do not infer student employability from:

- professor biographies;
- a handful of spectacular alumni;
- employer-logo walls without cohort denominators;
- salary figures restricted to employed respondents;
- `employed or continuing education` aggregates;
- job postings alone;
- the existence of a curriculum that resembles work done by practitioners;
- the fact that students were willing or able to borrow the tuition price.

Do not assume that every corporate data-science role is interchangeable. Some are software-heavy, some statistical, some domain-science-heavy, and some are effectively research positions. The competition set can differ substantially by role.

## Accountability question

For a prospective student, the useful question is not:

> Are the professors impressive, and does data science exist as an occupation?

It is:

> Of people who entered this program from a background like mine, how many obtained the kind of data-science job used to justify the program, how long did it take, what prior experience did the successful students already have, and what happened to everyone else?

Until a program can answer that with inspectable cohort data, the professor's career and the existence of well-paid corporate data-science jobs should be treated as separate facts from the student's expected outcome.
