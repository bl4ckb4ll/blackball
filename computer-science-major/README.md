# The computer science major

This folder asks a narrow Blackball question:

> What does a recent U.S. bachelor's degree in computer science actually buy in the employment system, especially for graduates trying to enter software or adjacent technical work?

The immediate purpose is not to decide that computer science is "good" or "bad." It is to separate the credential, the curriculum, internships and prior work, recruiting pipelines, geography, school effects, and the actual first job obtained.

## Current status

Initial Reddit discovery pass: 2026-09-09.

Reddit is being used here as a **failure-mode and lead generator**, not as an unemployment-rate estimator. Self-reports can establish that a particular pathway or failure occurs; they cannot provide a denominator for how common it is.

See [reddit/recent-graduate-employability.md](reddit/recent-graduate-employability.md) for the initial thread inventory.

## Candidate claims to test

These are hypotheses, not settled findings.

1. **The bachelor's degree by itself is not a reliable immediate-job guarantee.** Recent graduates report long searches even with respectable GPAs, projects, career-center help, and large application counts.
2. **Internship or equivalent work experience may function as a second credential layered on top of the degree.** Several successful counterexamples in the Reddit sample have unusually strong internship histories, while graduates without internships repeatedly describe difficulty obtaining interviews.
3. **The new-graduate recruiting window may create a cliff.** Graduates who miss campus/new-grad pipelines can remain inexperienced while also becoming ineligible for postings targeted at the next graduating class.
4. **Continuing education is not automatically evidence of a successful first destination.** Some Reddit participants explicitly discuss graduate school as a way to re-enter internship recruiting or because they could not get the job they wanted. School outcome reports therefore need the reason for further study, not just a separate "continuing education" bucket.
5. **Application count is not an employment outcome.** Reports of 500, 750, 3,000, or more applications are useful evidence of search friction, but counts are not comparable without knowing role fit, location, work authorization, duplicate postings, application quality, and the applicant's actual skill set.
6. **School first-destination reports are not directly comparable without methodology.** A Reddit compilation of school outcome dashboards shows very large differences in "seeking" rates, but response rates, survey dates, degree groupings, and category definitions differ enough that same-year cross-school ranking would be unsafe.

## Evidence standard for this folder

Keep at least four layers separate:

- **individual report** — one graduate's claimed search and outcome;
- **Reddit discussion** — useful for recurring mechanisms and counterexamples, but selected and unverifiable;
- **institutional first-destination data** — stronger for denominators if response rate, timing, and categories are known;
- **independent population data** — preferred for claims about the major as a whole.

Do not turn "I sent 3,000 applications" into a market statistic. Do not turn a school career survey into a national unemployment rate. Do not count graduate school as employment. Do not assume a job requiring any bachelor's degree is work that uses the CS curriculum.

## Retrieval queue

The Reddit pass points to several next steps:

- verify longitudinal first-destination data at the same schools rather than comparing schools with different survey methods;
- distinguish **employed in software/CS-related work**, **employed elsewhere**, **seeking**, **not seeking**, and **continuing education**;
- record survey response rates and the date at which graduates were surveyed;
- compare graduates with and without internships where a source exposes that variable;
- measure how long new-grad eligibility remains open after graduation;
- inspect actual entry-level postings for experience requirements over time;
- look for older cohorts who missed the first recruiting cycle and track whether they entered the field later;
- compare the CS bachelor's with adjacent paths such as IT, electrical/computer engineering, statistics, and non-degree software entrants without assuming any of them is a control group.

## Question to keep in view

The useful Blackball question is not merely "Can a CS graduate get a job?"

It is:

> For a student considering the major, what employment probability, waiting time, additional unpaid or paid preparation, geographic flexibility, and fallback work should reasonably be expected — and which of those requirements are actually supplied by the college rather than left to the student to acquire independently?
