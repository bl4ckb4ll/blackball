# University of Michigan

This folder tracks University of Michigan claims, costs, student experiences, and downstream outcomes without treating institutional prestige as an outcome in itself.

The first local lead concerns psychology graduates reportedly working in retail under a manager who had less formal education but more applicable work experience and who advanced into management and a corporate position.

See [Psychology graduates in retail — southeast Michigan interview lead](psychology-graduates-in-retail-southeast-michigan-interview.md).

## Why psychology is a useful test

Psychology is one of the largest undergraduate fields in U-M LSA. The department reports that in 2024–2025 it granted 388 undergraduate psychology degrees and 354 undergraduate BCN degrees. A large program creates enough graduates that broad phrases such as “many career possibilities” should be replaceable with actual destination distributions.

The department’s career page says that “major does not equal career” and lists possible fields and job-search resources. That may be sensible advice. It also leaves the central customer question unanswered unless accompanied by counts:

- What work do psychology graduates actually obtain?
- How much of it requires a bachelor’s degree?
- How much is related to psychology?
- What do graduates earn after debt and local living costs?
- How many require graduate or professional school before reaching the occupation they expected?
- How many work in retail, food service, warehousing, administration, or other occupations omitted from alumni profiles?
- How many would choose the same degree and price again?

## Broad outcome statistics are not a psychology outcome table

Current U-M pages report LSA-wide first-destination results using varying cohorts and combined categories such as “employed or continuing education.” One current Career Center page reports 61.1% employed, 28.7% continuing education, and 10.2% unemployed/underemployed and seeking for LSA. A separate LSA page advertises 96% employed or attending graduate or professional school. A 2025 LSA Opportunity Hub report says more than 78% accepted jobs or pursued graduate school.

These figures may refer to different cohorts, response methods, deadlines, and definitions. They should not be treated as contradictions until the metadata are reconstructed. They do demonstrate why Blackball must preserve:

- cohort and graduation year;
- survey deadline;
- response and knowledge rate;
- employed versus further study;
- full-time versus part-time;
- degree-required versus non-degree work;
- major-specific versus college-wide results;
- job relevance, pay, location, and duration;
- respondents versus the entire graduating class.

Combining employment with more education can conceal whether the first degree converted into work. College-wide figures can conceal the distribution for a large individual major.
