# Olive Bray — *The Elder or Poetic Edda* (1908)

## Why this book is here

Olive Bray's 1908 bilingual edition and translation of *The Elder or Poetic Edda: Commonly Known as Sæmund's Edda* belongs in Blackball for two separate reasons.

First, it is a real piece of scholarship and translation worth reading on its own terms.

Second, Bray's biography makes a useful class-and-cultivation case. The point is **not** that family wealth invalidates the work. The point is to keep visible the material conditions that can make years of specialized linguistic and literary work possible.

## Translator and family position

The University of Victoria's MyNDIR record identifies **Olive Bray (1878–1909)** as an English scholar, translator, and editor. It records that she was one of the daughters of:

- **Sir Reginald More Bray (1842–1923)**, a High Court judge;
- **Emily Octavia Bray**, a novelist;
- the family associated with **Shere Manor near Guildford**;
- and, at the time of Olive Bray's death, the family home at **17 The Boltons, Kensington, London**.

MyNDIR also records that Bray joined the Viking Society for Northern Research in 1902 and was a vice-president in 1909.

Source:
- https://myndir.uvic.ca/Bry01.html

## The book

Bray edited and translated *The Elder or Poetic Edda* for the Viking Club in 1908. The edition presents Old Norse and English in parallel and includes introduction, notes, and illustrations by W. G. Collingwood.

## Blackball interpretation

Do **not** turn this into:

> Olive Bray was privileged, therefore her translation does not count.

That would throw away the evidence.

The useful question is:

> What does it take materially for a young adult to spend years learning Old Norse, joining a specialist learned society, editing texts, producing notes, and publishing a bilingual literary translation?

A High Court judge does not need to send his daughter into a low-paid factory in order to prove that she has character. If the household can support specialized study, it is perfectly rational for the family to spend resources on education, culture, travel, books, and scholarly work.

For a person without that independent support, the constraint is different. Ordinary paid work may be compulsory because rent, food, transport, and family obligations have to be met. That difference is not an argument against scholarship. It is a reason not to treat access to scholarship as though the **education itself** created the underlying freedom.

This case belongs beside Paul Klee's diary material: resources can make a cultural life possible; family dependence can still constrain freedom; and neither fact makes the resulting art or scholarship fake.

## Questions to keep open

- What formal education did Olive Bray receive, and who paid for it?
- Did she have independent income, an allowance, inheritance expectations, or other household support?
- How much time did the Edda edition require?
- What did Viking Club membership and office imply socially and financially?
- Who financed the printing and publication?
- How unusual was a woman in her position within contemporary Old Norse scholarship?
- What comparable translators came from materially different backgrounds?

Do not infer answers merely from her father's office or address.
