## How this source trail is labeled

This matters because Stephan published a closely related survey before the book.

- **2012-confirmed** — a source or claim can be tied directly to the 2012 book through accessible book material or a review that names it.
- **precursor** — present in Stephan's 2010 chapter, “The Economics of Science,” which is a major intellectual precursor to the book. It is *not* automatically claimed to occur in the 2012 bibliography.
- **followed** — I independently followed the cited work far enough to identify what it contributes to Stephan's argument.
- **follow-forward** — later work by Stephan or collaborators that tests or sharpens a question raised in the book.

Paula Stephan's 2010 chapter explicitly says that it “draws extensively” on her 1996 *Journal of Economic Literature* article. That gives a documented chain:

## What the book is actually trying to explain

The useful object here is not “economics” as an academic department. It is the machinery around scientific work:

- who pays for research and under what conditions;
- who absorbs the risk when a grant ends;
- why universities hire graduate students and postdocs rather than permanent researchers;
- how priority, reputation, prizes, citations, and publication affect choices;
- why researchers may avoid risky work when renewal depends on short-term output;
- how laboratories and teams have grown;
- how patents, materials, equipment, and access restrictions affect research;
- how the labor market for scientists repeatedly produces bad forecasts and unstable career paths;
- and how publicly supported research enters industrial innovation and economic activity.

James Wilsdon's review captures another important correction: Stephan does not reduce scientists to cash-maximizers. Curiosity, puzzle-solving, discovery, recognition, publication, citations, prizes, and money coexist as motives. Review: <https://www.timeshighereducation.com/books/how-economics-shapes-science/419647.article>

## Source trail

### 1. Why scientific knowledge is shared, and why priority matters

**2012-confirmed / precursor / followed**

- **Robert K. Merton**, “Priorities in Scientific Discovery” (1957), and later work on the Matthew effect. Merton supplies the sociological account of priority and recognition: scientific credit is a reward, and unequal recognition can compound. This is one of the clearest ancestors of Stephan's treatment of non-wage incentives.
- **Warren O. Hagstrom**, *The Scientific Community* (1965). Hagstrom treats publication and recognition as part of an exchange system inside science. Open Library/Internet Archive record: <https://openlibrary.org/search?q=The+Scientific+Community+Hagstrom>
- **Derek J. de Solla Price**, *Little Science, Big Science … and Beyond* (1963/1986). Price supplies the empirical picture of science as a rapidly growing organized activity and helped found quantitative study of scientific publication and collaboration. Open Library: <https://openlibrary.org/search?q=Little+Science+Big+Science+Price>
- **Michael Polanyi**, “The Republic of Science” (1962). A classic argument that decentralized choices by researchers can still form a coordinated order through mutual adjustment and shared standards. Authorized full text: <https://www.polanyisociety.org/mp-repsc.htm>
- **John Ziman**, *Public Knowledge* (1968). Science is not merely private discovery; it becomes science through communication, criticism, and incorporation into a body of public knowledge. Open Library: <https://openlibrary.org/search?q=Public+Knowledge+Ziman>
- **Kenneth Arrow**, “Economic Welfare and the Allocation of Resources for Invention” (1962). Arrow's key problem is that information is hard to sell like an ordinary commodity: disclosure, uncertainty, and non-rival use create reasons markets alone underproduce research. NBER: <https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/rate-and-direction-inventive-activity-economic-and-social-factors/economic-welfare-and-allocation-resources-invention>
- **Richard Nelson**, “The Simple Economics of Basic Scientific Research” (1959). Nelson asks why firms have weak incentives to fund basic research when benefits spill beyond the funder, one of the classic arguments for public support.
- **Partha Dasgupta and Paul David**, “Toward a New Economics of Science” (1994). Important because it explicitly joins the Arrow/Nelson information problem to the Mertonian institutional system of priority, openness, and reputation: <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0048733394010021>
- **Bernard Barber / Bernard Cohen / Bernard Cole traditions**, and especially **Jonathan and Stephen Cole**, *Social Stratification in Science* (1973), push the analysis toward unequal distributions of recognition, positions, and resources. Open Library: <https://openlibrary.org/search?q=Social+Stratification+in+Science+Cole>
- **Thomas S. Kuhn**, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), is explicitly identified by Hicks as part of Stephan's opening lineage. Kuhn contributes a very different account from incentive models: fields work inside historically structured paradigms, not just through isolated optimization.

**Why the cluster matters:** Stephan's account of “incentives” is unintelligible if stripped of the sociology and history. Priority and reputation are institutions of science before they are variables in a model.

### 2. Scientists are workers: careers, wages, and the training pyramid

**precursor / followed**

- **David Blank and George Stigler**, *The Demand and Supply of Scientific Personnel* (1957). One of the early systematic attempts to treat scientific labor as a labor market. NBER provides the book and chapter files: <https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/demand-and-supply-scientific-personnel>
- **Kenneth Arrow and William Capron**, “Dynamic Shortages and Price Rises: The Engineer-Scientist Case” (1959). A warning that rapid changes in demand make static “shortage” claims misleading.
- **Richard Freeman**, work beginning in the 1970s on the market for scientists and engineers. Stephan repeatedly works in this lineage: long training lags make forecasts and policy responses unstable.
- **National Research Council**, *Forecasting Demand and Supply of Doctoral Scientists and Engineers* (2000). Especially relevant because the report examines repeated forecasting failures while the number of PhDs and postdocs grew and career prospects deteriorated. National Academies: <https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/9865>
- **Mary Frank Fox and Paula Stephan**, “Careers of Young Scientists: Preferences, Prospects and Realities by Gender and Field” (2001). Survey of roughly 3,800 doctoral students across chemistry, computer science, electrical engineering, microbiology, and physics, comparing desired careers with expected/actual opportunities: <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/030631201031001006>
- **Scott Stern**, “Do Scientists Pay to Be Scientists?” (2004). Using multiple job offers to postdoctoral biologists, Stern finds evidence that scientists accept lower wages for jobs that provide stronger opportunities to do and publish science: <https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1040.0241>
- **Sharon Levin and Paula Stephan**, work on research productivity over the life cycle, and later on foreign-born scientists, connects career stage, productivity, migration, and the scientific labor force.
- **Ronald Ehrenberg, Michael Rizzo, and George Jakubson**, “Who Bears the Growing Cost of Science at Universities?” (2003). As research costs rise and outside sponsors do not cover all costs, universities shift resources internally; their results connect research expansion to student/faculty ratios and tuition at private institutions. NBER: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w9627>

**Why the cluster matters:** “funding science” is also a choice about employment structure. A system can increase the number of temporary researchers and grant-dependent positions faster than it creates stable careers.

### 3. Why science became a team activity

**precursor / followed**

- **James Adams, Grant Black, Roger Clemmons, and Paula Stephan**, “Scientific Teams and Institutional Collaborations” (2005). Using about 2.4 million papers from 110 U.S. universities, they document larger and more geographically dispersed teams, with a marked acceleration in collaboration. NBER: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w10640>
- **Stefan Wuchty, Benjamin Jones, and Brian Uzzi**, “The Increasing Dominance of Teams in Production of Knowledge” (2007). Across 19.9 million papers and 2.1 million patents, teams become more common and tend to receive more citations: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17431139/>
- **Benjamin Jones**, “The Burden of Knowledge and the Death of the Renaissance Man” (2005/2009). As the stock of knowledge grows, researchers require longer training and narrower specialization, increasing the need for teams. NBER working paper: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w11360>

**Why the cluster matters:** bigger laboratories are not simply administrative bloat. Some of the pressure toward teams comes from the accumulated amount of knowledge required to work at the frontier. But large-team organization also changes who receives credit, who controls equipment, and who is employable independently.

### 4. Patents, materials, and the ability to use knowledge

**precursor / followed**

- **Rebecca Eisenberg**, “Proprietary Rights and the Norms of Science in Biotechnology Research” (1987). Trade secrecy directly conflicts with scientific dissemination; patents at least disclose inventions, but exclusive rights can still delay or constrain use: <https://repository.law.umich.edu/facarticles/1220/>
- **Michael Heller and Rebecca Eisenberg**, “Can Patents Deter Innovation? The Anticommons in Biomedical Research” (1998). Fragmented rights over upstream discoveries can create transaction barriers for downstream research: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9563938/>
- **John Walsh, Ashish Arora / Charlene Cho, and Wesley Cohen** work on whether patents actually block biomedical researchers. The empirical question is important because the dramatic anticommons hypothesis must be checked against researchers' actual behavior.
- **Fiona Murray**, work on the Oncomouse and stem-cell patent landscapes, studies how property rights interact with scientific communities and access to research materials.
- **Pierre Azoulay, Waverly Ding, and Toby Stuart**, “The Impact of Academic Patenting on the Rate, Quality and Direction of (Public) Research Output” (2006/2009). Their evidence does not support a simple story in which patenting merely kills publication; patenting is associated with changes in both output and the direction of research. NBER: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w11917>
- **Saul Lach and Mark Schankerman**, “Incentives and Invention in Universities” (2008). Universities that give researchers a larger share of royalty income obtain more licensing income, largely through researcher effort/sorting and invention quality: <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.0741-6261.2008.00020.x>

### 5. Funding design can select for safe work

This is the source trail most directly relevant to science funding.

**2012-era / followed**

- **Pierre Azoulay, Joshua Graff Zivin, and Gustavo Manso**, “Incentives and Creativity: Evidence from the Academic Life Sciences” (NBER 2009; published 2011). They compare Howard Hughes Medical Institute investigators, who received longer horizons and greater tolerance for early failure, with NIH-funded researchers facing more conventional project review and renewal. HHMI-style funding is associated with more high-impact work and more exploration of novel research directions. NBER: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w15466>
- **Paula Stephan**, “Perverse Incentives” (*Nature*, 2012). A short companion to the book's argument: the funding and employment system can make researchers spend time chasing money, produce excessive numbers of trainees, and respond rationally to incentives that are bad for science as a whole: <https://www.nature.com/articles/484029a>

**follow-forward**

- **Jian Wang, Reinhilde Veugelers, and Paula Stephan**, “Bias Against Novelty in Science: A Cautionary Tale for Users of Bibliometric Indicators” (NBER 2016; published 2017). Highly novel papers are riskier and often take longer to be recognized even though they are more likely to become very highly cited in the long run. Short-horizon evaluation by journal prestige or immediate citations can therefore penalize exactly the work funding agencies claim to want. NBER: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w22180>

**The practical conclusion from this line of work:** the funding contract is part of the research apparatus. Grant length, renewal timing, tolerance of failure, what counts as output, and whether a person's salary depends on the next grant all change which questions are rational to attempt.

### 6. Public research and industrial/economic spillovers

**precursor / followed**

- **Vannevar Bush**, *Science—The Endless Frontier* (1945). The foundational U.S. postwar argument for federal support of basic research and scientific training.
- **Zvi Griliches**, work on R&D, productivity, and spillovers, developed tools for asking how research benefits escape the organization that paid for it.
- **Adam Jaffe**, “Real Effects of Academic Research” (1989), and **Jaffe, Trajtenberg, and Henderson**, “Geographic Localization of Knowledge Spillovers as Evidenced by Patent Citations” (1993). The latter finds that knowledge spillovers are geographically localized, especially at shorter lags. NBER working paper: <https://www.nber.org/papers/w3993>
- **Edwin Mansfield**, studies in 1991 and 1995 on how academic research contributes to industrial innovation, asking firms which products/processes depended on recent academic work.
- **Wesley Cohen, Richard Nelson, and John Walsh**, “Links and Impacts: The Influence of Public Research on Industrial R&D” (2002), examines the channels by which public research affects firms, not only patents but publications, conferences, informal exchange, and trained people.
- **Nathan Rosenberg**, “Why Do Firms Do Basic Research (With Their Own Money)?” (1990). Even firms that cannot capture all returns may fund basic research because scientific competence lets them recognize and absorb outside discoveries: <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0048733390900469>
- **Paul Romer**, “The Origins of Endogenous Growth” (1994), represents the growth-theory side of the story: discovery and technical change are made endogenous rather than left as unexplained residual forces: <https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.8.1.3>

## Reviews and useful disagreements

- **Diana Hicks**, *Science and Public Policy* 39(6), 2012, 840–841. Valuable because she inventories the book's interdisciplinary lineage and reports the striking 13% economics-reference figure: <https://academic.oup.com/spp/article-abstract/39/6/840/1617002>
- **James Wilsdon**, *Times Higher Education*, 12 April 2012. Emphasizes that the book's first half is a broad empirical account of the costs and incentives surrounding research and that motives include curiosity and recognition as well as money: <https://www.timeshighereducation.com/books/how-economics-shapes-science/419647.article>
- **David Tyfield**, review of *How Economics Shapes Science*. Useful counterpoint: he argues that the book is impressively comprehensive while remaining too willing to organize the discussion through a mainstream economic vocabulary of incentives, costs, and scarce resources.

The tension is worth preserving rather than resolving by fiat: Stephan's evidence base is much broader than economics, while her organizing vocabulary can still narrow what questions are easiest to ask.
