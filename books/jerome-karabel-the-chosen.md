## Why it belongs in Blackball

Karabel reconstructs the development of selective admission at Harvard, Yale, and Princeton over roughly the twentieth century. The book is useful here because admissions criteria are treated as historically changeable institutional decisions rather than as timeless or mechanically meritocratic rules.

The relevant Blackball questions include:

- how elite institutions defined and redefined "merit";
- how admissions rules interacted with class, religion, race, gender, alumni power, geography, and institutional competition;
- how the schools described their policies publicly versus how they made decisions internally;
- how institutional autonomy and competitive position constrained reform;
- how money, financial aid, and competition among elite schools altered admission policy; and
- what primary institutional records support Karabel's reconstruction.

## Ivy/MIT financial-aid antitrust connection

Karabel's late-century discussion intersects directly with the **Ivy Overlap** controversy: the Ivy League institutions and MIT coordinated need-analysis practices and reconciled expected family contributions for students admitted to more than one participating school. The Justice Department challenged those arrangements under Sherman Act § 1.

The legal record should be read independently rather than allowing Karabel's narrative to stand in for the pleadings or opinions.
